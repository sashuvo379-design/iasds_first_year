01 Bernoulli Trial & Distribution ($\text{Bernoulli}(p)$)

The Atomic Building Block

A Bernoulli trial is a single random experiment with exactly two mutually exclusive outcomes: Success ($X = 1$, probability $p$) or Failure ($X = 0$, probability $q = 1 - p$), where $0 \le p \le 1$.

PMF ($x \in \{0,1\}$)
$p^x (1-p)^{1-x}$
Mean
$E(X) = p$
Variance
$V(X) = p(1-p) = pq$
MGF
$M_X(t) = q + pe^t$
Visual Indicator Connection A Binomial random variable $X \sim B(n,p)$ is simply the sum of $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ indicator variables:
$$X = \sum_{i=1}^{n} I_i, \quad I_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(p)$$

02 The Binomial Distribution $B(n, p)$ & BINS Conditions

ISRT Year Final 2022 Q5 · 2025 Q7
B Binary Outcomes

Each trial results in exactly two categories: Success ($1$) or Failure ($0$).

I Independent Trials

The outcome of one trial does not influence the outcome of any other trial.

N Number $n$ Fixed

The total number of trials $n$ is predetermined in advance (no early stopping).

S Same Probability $p$

The success probability $p$ remains strictly constant across every trial.

PMF, Mean, Variance & Generating Functions
$P(X = x) = \dbinom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}, \quad x = 0, 1, \dots, n \;\;(q = 1-p)$
$E(X) = np, \qquad V(X) = npq, \qquad \gamma_1 = \dfrac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{npq}}$
$M_X(t) = (q + pe^t)^n, \qquad P_X(t) = (q + pt)^n$
Recurrence Ratio & Mode
$\dfrac{P(X = i)}{P(X = i - 1)} = \dfrac{n - i + 1}{i}\cdot\dfrac{p}{q}, \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, n$
Mode: $m = \lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$.

If $(n+1)p$ is an integer, the distribution is bimodal at $(n+1)p - 1$ and $(n+1)p$.

03 The Hypergeometric Distribution $\text{Hyp}(N, K, n)$

Sampling Without Replacement · 2022 Q2(c)

Consider a finite population of size $N$ containing $K$ successes (e.g., defective bulbs, tagged fish) and $N - K$ failures. Draw a random sample of size $n$ without replacement, and let $X$ be the number of successes in the sample.

$$P(X = x) = \frac{\dbinom{K}{x}\dbinom{N - K}{n - x}}{\dbinom{N}{n}}, \qquad \max(0,\, n - (N - K)) \le x \le \min(n,\, K)$$
Mean (Identical to Binomial!)
$$E(X) = n\frac{K}{N} = np \qquad \left(\text{where } p = \frac{K}{N}\right)$$

Even though draws are dependent, linearity of expectation preserves the exact same mean $np$.

Variance & Finite Population Correction (FPC)
$$V(X) = n\frac{K}{N}\left(1 - \frac{K}{N}\right)\underbrace{\left(\frac{N - n}{N - 1}\right)}_{\text{FPC} \le 1} = npq \cdot \text{FPC}$$

Because draws without replacement are negatively correlated, $V_{\text{Hyp}}(X) \le V_{\text{Bin}}(X)$ strictly for $n \ge 2$. As $N \to \infty$ (rule of thumb: $n/N \le 0.05$), $\text{FPC} \to 1$ and $\text{Hyp}(N,K,n) \to B(n, K/N)$.

!
ISRT Exam-Trap Spotlight

Three High-Frequency AST 102 Traps

Trap 1: Mean vs. Variance Bound

For any non-degenerate Binomial, $V(X) = npq < np = E(X)$ strictly! In Year Final 2024 Q1(a), $\mu = 100, \sigma = 50 \implies \sigma^2 = 2500 > 100 \implies q = 25 > 1$ (impossible!). In 2025 Q7(b), $\mu = \sigma^2 \implies q = 1 \implies p = 0$ (degenerate).

Trap 2: Stopping Early Breaks "N"

In Year Final 2025 Q7(d), trying keys with replacement until the treasure chest opens (or up to 6 attempts) is NOT Binomial because the number of trials $n$ is random, not fixed!

Trap 3: $X_1 - X_2$ is NOT Binomial

For independent $X_1 \sim B(n_1, p)$ and $X_2 \sim B(n_2, p)$, the sum $X_1 + X_2 \sim B(n_1 + n_2, p)$, but the difference $X_1 - X_2$ can take negative values and is never Binomial!

Proof 1 · ISRT Year Final 2025 Q7(a)
Sum of Binomial Probabilities Equals 1

For $X \sim B(n, p)$, clearly $P(X=x) = \binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x} \ge 0$ for all $x \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$. By the Binomial Theorem $(a + b)^n = \sum_{x=0}^n \binom{n}{x} a^x b^{n-x}$ with $a = p$ and $b = 1 - p$:

$$\sum_{x=0}^{n} P(X = x) = \sum_{x=0}^{n} \binom{n}{x} p^x (1 - p)^{n-x} = \big(p + (1 - p)\big)^n = 1^n = 1 \quad \blacksquare$$
Proof 2 · ISRT Year Final 2022 Q5(c) & 2024 Q6(b)
Mean & Variance of $B(n, p)$ via MGF & Indicator Variables

Method 1 (Via MGF): $M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}] = \sum_{x=0}^n \binom{n}{x}(pe^t)^x q^{n-x} = (q + pe^t)^n$. Differentiate at $t = 0$:

$$M'_X(t) = n(q + pe^t)^{n-1}pe^t \implies E(X) = M'_X(0) = n(q+p)^{n-1}p = np$$ $$M''_X(t) = n(n-1)(q+pe^t)^{n-2}(pe^t)^2 + n(q+pe^t)^{n-1}pe^t \implies E(X^2) = n(n-1)p^2 + np$$ $$V(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2 = n^2p^2 - np^2 + np - n^2p^2 = np(1-p) = npq$$

Method 2 (Sleek Indicator Shortcut): Write $X = \sum_{i=1}^n I_i$ where $I_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(p)$ with $E(I_i) = p$ and $V(I_i) = pq$. By linearity and independence:

$$E(X) = \sum_{i=1}^n E(I_i) = np, \qquad V(X) = \sum_{i=1}^n V(I_i) = npq \quad \blacksquare$$
Proof 3 · ISRT Year Final Q6(c)
Binomial Recurrence Relation & Mode Formula

For $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$, form the ratio $c = \dfrac{P(X = i)}{P(X = i - 1)}$:

$$c = \frac{\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}\,p^i q^{n-i}}{\frac{n!}{(i-1)!(n-i+1)!}\,p^{i-1} q^{n-i+1}} = \frac{(i-1)!(n-i+1)!}{i!(n-i)!}\cdot\frac{p}{q} = \mathbf{\frac{n - i + 1}{i}\cdot\frac{p}{1 - p}}$$

Deriving the Mode: $P(X = i) \ge P(X = i - 1) \iff c \ge 1$:

$$(n - i + 1)p \ge i(1 - p) \iff (n + 1)p - ip \ge i - ip \iff i \le (n + 1)p$$

Thus $P(X=i)$ climbs until $i = \lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$ and decreases afterward. If $(n+1)p$ is an exact integer, equality holds at $i = (n+1)p$, yielding two equal peaks (bimodal) at $(n+1)p - 1$ and $(n+1)p$. $\blacksquare$

Proof 4 · ISRT Year Final 2022 Q7(c) & 2024 Q6(c)
Reproductive Property of Binomial ($X_1 + X_2$ vs. $X_1 - X_2$)

Let $X_1 \sim B(n_1, p)$ and $X_2 \sim B(n_2, p)$ be independent. Their MGFs are $M_{X_1}(t) = (q+pe^t)^{n_1}$ and $M_{X_2}(t) = (q+pe^t)^{n_2}$.

$$M_{X_1 + X_2}(t) = M_{X_1}(t)M_{X_2}(t) = (q + pe^t)^{n_1}(q + pe^t)^{n_2} = (q + pe^t)^{n_1 + n_2}$$

By the MGF Uniqueness Theorem, $X_1 + X_2 \sim B(n_1 + n_2, p)$. Conversely, for the difference $X_1 - X_2$: $M_{X_1 - X_2}(t) = (q+pe^t)^{n_1}(q+pe^{-t})^{n_2}$, which cannot take the Binomial form $(q^* + p^*e^t)^N$ (and $X_1 - X_2$ can be negative). $\blacksquare$

Proof 5 · Hypergeometric Mean & FPC Variance
Mean & Variance of $\text{Hyp}(N, K, n)$ via Indicator Variables

Let $X = \sum_{i=1}^n I_i$, where $I_i = 1$ if the $i$-th drawn item is a success and $0$ otherwise. By symmetry of sampling without replacement, $P(I_i = 1) = \frac{K}{N} = p$, so $E(I_i) = p$ and $V(I_i) = pq$.

Mean: $E(X) = \sum_{i=1}^n E(I_i) = n\frac{K}{N} = np$.

Covariance for $i \neq j$: $E(I_i I_j) = P(I_i=1, I_j=1) = \frac{K}{N}\cdot\frac{K-1}{N-1}$. Therefore:

$$\text{Cov}(I_i, I_j) = \frac{K(K-1)}{N(N-1)} - \frac{K^2}{N^2} = -\frac{K(N - K)}{N^2(N - 1)} = -\frac{pq}{N - 1}$$

Summing all $n$ variances and $n(n-1)$ ordered covariance pairs:

$$V(X) = n(pq) + n(n-1)\left(-\frac{pq}{N-1}\right) = npq\left(1 - \frac{n - 1}{N - 1}\right) = npq\left(\frac{N - n}{N - 1}\right) \quad \blacksquare$$

ISRT Year Final 2025 · Q7(a, b, c, d) [12 Marks] AST 102 · University of Dhaka

(a) State the four conditions of a Binomial experiment (BINS) and show that $\sum_{x=0}^n P(X=x) = 1$.

(b) Show that for $X \sim B(n, p)$, $\mu = \sigma^2$ occurs only in the degenerate case $p = 0$. Briefly describe the degenerate cases $p = 0$ and $p = 1$.

(c) A marksman shoots $n = 10$ times with $V(X) = 1.44$. Find the possible values of $p$. Taking the higher $p$, find $P(X > 8)$ and the minimum shots $n$ needed so $P(X \ge 1) > 0.995$.

(d) On a game show, 1 of 8 keys opens a treasure chest. A contestant tries keys with replacement, stopping as soon as the chest opens (or after 6 tries). Is the number of tries Binomial? Find $P(\text{chest opens in fewer than 4 tries})$.

Toggle Step-by-Step Solution

(a) BINS: Binary outcomes, Independent trials, Fixed $n$, Same $p$. Sum equals $(p + (1-p))^n = 1^n = 1$.

(b) $\mu = \sigma^2 \implies np = np(1-p) \implies np^2 = 0 \implies p = 0$ (since $n \ge 1$). At $p = 0$, $P(X=0)=1$ (always 0 successes); at $p = 1$, $P(X=n)=1$ (always $n$ successes); both have $V(X)=0$.

(c) $10p(1-p) = 1.44 \implies p^2 - p + 0.144 = 0 \implies p = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 0.576}}{2} = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{0.424}}{2} \approx \mathbf{0.8256}$ or $\mathbf{0.1744}$. With $p = 0.8256$: $P(X > 8) = P(X=9) + P(X=10) = 10(0.8256)^9(0.1744) + (0.8256)^{10} \approx 0.3110 + 0.1468 = \mathbf{0.4578}$. For $P(X \ge 1) > 0.995$: $1 - (0.1744)^n > 0.995 \implies (0.1744)^n < 0.005 \implies n > \dfrac{\ln(0.005)}{\ln(0.1744)} \approx 3.034 \implies \mathbf{n \ge 4}$.

(d) Not Binomial — stopping early when the chest opens violates the fixed-$n$ condition ("N" in BINS); it is a truncated Geometric model. $$P(\text{opens in } < 4 \text{ tries}) = 1 - P(\text{first 3 fail}) = 1 - \left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^3 = 1 - \frac{343}{512} = \mathbf{\frac{169}{512} \approx 0.3301}$$

ISRT Year Final 2022 · Q2(c) [5 Marks] Hypergeometric PMF Table · AST 102

A box of 10 flashbulbs contains 3 defective bulbs. A random sample of 3 is selected without replacement and tested. (i) Define the random variable $X$ for this problem. (ii) Find the probability distribution of $X$, verify it sums to 1, and compute $E(X)$ and $V(X)$.

Toggle Step-by-Step Solution

(i) Let $X$ = number of defective bulbs in the sample of 3. Since sampling is without replacement, $X \sim \text{Hyp}(N=10, K=3, n=3)$, with $\binom{10}{3} = 120$.

(ii) PMF Table:

  • P(X = 0) = C(3,0)·C(7,3) / 120 = 35 / 120 ≈ 0.2917
  • P(X = 1) = C(3,1)·C(7,2) / 120 = 63 / 120 = 0.5250
  • P(X = 2) = C(3,2)·C(7,1) / 120 = 21 / 120 = 0.1750
  • P(X = 3) = C(3,3)·C(7,0) / 120 = 1 / 120 ≈ 0.0083

Check Sum: $\frac{35 + 63 + 21 + 1}{120} = \frac{120}{120} = 1$ ✓.   Mean & Variance: $E(X) = 3\left(\frac{3}{10}\right) = \mathbf{0.9}$;   $V(X) = 3(0.3)(0.7)\left(\frac{10-3}{10-1}\right) = 0.63 \times \frac{7}{9} = \mathbf{0.49}$.

ISRT Year Final 2024 · Q1(a) [2 Marks] Classic Parameter Trap · AST 102

"The mean and standard deviation of a binomial distribution are 100 and 50. Find the binomial distribution."

Toggle Step-by-Step Solution

Mathematically Impossible! For $X \sim B(n, p)$, $\mu = np = 100$ and $\sigma = 50 \implies \sigma^2 = npq = 2500$.

$$q = \frac{npq}{np} = \frac{2500}{100} = 25 > 1 \implies p = 1 - 25 = -24 < 0 \quad (\text{Contradiction!})$$

Exam-Hall Note: State clearly that no Binomial distribution exists as written because $V(X) = npq \le np = E(X)$ always holds. Then add: "If 'standard deviation = 50' was a misprint for 'variance = 50', then $q = 50/100 = 0.5$, $p = 0.5$, and $n = 100/0.5 = 200$, giving $B(200, 0.5)$."

ISRT Year Final 2022 · Q2(b) & Q3(b) [8 Marks] Applied Binomial Problems · AST 102

Q2(b) [4 Marks]: 5% of drivers show traces of alcohol and 10% do not wear seat belts (independent). An officer stops $n = 4$ drivers at random. Find (i) $P(\text{exactly 3 committed an offense})$ and (ii) $P(\text{at least 1 committed at least one offense})$.

Q3(b) [4 Marks]: A box has 10 red and 20 blue balls. A ball is drawn, noted whether red, and returned 10 times. Calculate the expected value and standard deviation.

Toggle Step-by-Step Solution

Q2(b): For a single driver, $p = P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A)P(B) = 0.05 + 0.10 - (0.05)(0.10) = \mathbf{0.145}$. Thus $X \sim B(4, 0.145)$:

  • (i) $P(X = 3) = \binom{4}{3}(0.145)^3(0.855)^1 \approx \mathbf{0.0104}$.
  • (ii) $P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - (0.855)^4 \approx 1 - 0.5344 = \mathbf{0.4656}$.

Q3(b): With replacement, $X \sim B(n=10, p = 10/30 = 1/3)$: $$E(X) = 10\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = \mathbf{\frac{10}{3} \approx 3.3333}, \qquad SD(X) = \sqrt{10\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{20}{9}} = \mathbf{\frac{\sqrt{20}}{3} \approx 1.4907}$$

ISRT Year Final · Q6(c) [4 Marks] Binomial Recurrence Relation · AST 102

If $X$ has a Binomial distribution $B(n, p)$ and $P[X = i] = c\,P[X = i - 1]$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$, find the constant $c$. Demonstrate computing $P(X = 2)$ from $P(X = 1)$ when $n = 5$ and $p = 0.4$.

Toggle Step-by-Step Solution

Derivation of $c$: $$c = \frac{P(X = i)}{P(X = i - 1)} = \frac{\binom{n}{i} p^i (1-p)^{n-i}}{\binom{n}{i-1} p^{i-1} (1-p)^{n-i+1}} = \mathbf{\frac{n - i + 1}{i}\cdot\frac{p}{1 - p}}$$

Numerical Demo ($n = 5, p = 0.4, i = 2$): First, $P(X = 1) = \binom{5}{1}(0.4)^1(0.6)^4 = 5(0.4)(0.1296) = 0.2592$. The recurrence multiplier at $i = 2$ is $c = \frac{5 - 2 + 1}{2}\cdot\frac{0.4}{0.6} = 2 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{3}$. Thus: $$P(X = 2) = \frac{4}{3}(0.2592) = \mathbf{0.3456}$$ (Direct check: $\binom{5}{2}(0.4)^2(0.6)^3 = 10(0.16)(0.216) = 0.3456$ ✓)
