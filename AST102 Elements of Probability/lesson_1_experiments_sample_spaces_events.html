Section 01

Core Concepts & Definitions

Sources: AST 102 Lecture 02–03 Notes & Sheldon Ross, Ch. 2.2

01 • Foundation Lecture 02

Experiment vs. Random Experiment

Experiment

A process or procedure by which an observation (or measurement) is obtained. It may be deterministic (outcome known in advance under identical conditions, e.g., heating water to 100°C at 1 atm).

Random Experiment

An experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance, even when repeated under identical conditions—yet the set of all possible outcomes is known beforehand.

Examples: Tossing a coin, rolling a six-sided die, selecting a card from an urn containing cards numbered 1 to 100, or testing a blood sample for Dengue.

02 • Building Blocks Lecture 02

Trial, Outcome & Equally Likely

  • Trial: A single unit or execution of a random experiment (e.g., rolling a die once).
  • Outcome (Sample Point): The observed result of a single trial (e.g., getting a 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6 on a die).
  • Equally Likely Outcomes: Outcomes of a trial are equally likely if we have no reason to expect any one outcome rather than another (e.g., Head and Tail on a fair coin; all 6 faces of a balanced die).
Shortcut when equally likely: If $S$ is finite and all outcomes are equally likely, then $P(E) = \dfrac{|E|}{|S|}$.
03 • Core Structure Ross Ch. 2.2 & Lecture 02

Sample Space ($S$ or $\Omega$)

The collection (set) of all possible outcomes of a random experiment is called its sample space, usually denoted by $S$ or $\Omega$.

04 • Subsets of S Lecture 02

Events & Special Event Types

An event $E$ is any subset of the sample space ($E \subseteq S$), denoted by capital letters ($A, B, E$). Event $E$ occurs if the experiment's outcome belongs to $E$.

Impossible (Null) Event ($\emptyset$): Contains no outcomes; can never occur. $P(\emptyset) = 0$.
Sure (Certain) Event ($S$): The entire sample space; always occurs. $P(S) = 1$.
Complementary Event ($A^c$ or $A'$): All outcomes in $S$ not in $A$. One occurs if and only if the other does not.
Exhaustive Events: Events whose union covers the entire sample space ($A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \dots \cup A_k = S$).

Mutually Exclusive Events

Events $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive (disjoint) if the occurrence of one precludes the other: $A \cap B = \emptyset$. Example on a die: $A = \{1,3,5\}$ (odd) and $B = \{2,4,6\}$ (even).

05 • Visual Tool Lecture 02 & 03

Tree Diagrams for Multi-Stage Experiments

A tree diagram organizes multi-stage experiments into sequential branches so you can systematically list every outcome in $S$ without missing or double-counting any path.

Sample Space vs. Arbitrary Set

Key Principle: Every sample space is a set, but not every set is a sample space. A set only becomes a sample space when it is tied to a specific random experiment and satisfies two strict rules: its elements must be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.

General Mathematical Set

  • Any well-defined collection of distinct objects (numbers, letters, symbols).
  • Requires no connection to a random experiment or chance process.
  • Example: $\{2, 4, \text{apple}, \pi\}$ is a valid set, not tied to any trial.
  • Need not exhaust all possibilities of an experiment (e.g., $\{1, 2, 3\}$ for a die is just an event, not the sample space).

Sample Space ($S$ or $\Omega$)

  • Specifically the set of all possible outcomes of a defined random experiment.
  • Mutually exclusive: No two elementary outcomes can occur simultaneously on a single trial.
  • Collectively exhaustive: At least one element of $S$ must occur on every trial ($P(S) = 1$).
  • Example: For rolling a die, $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ is the unique sample space.

Section 03

Classifying Sample Spaces & Key Proofs

Essential for discrete vs. continuous probability theory

1

Finite Sample Space

Contains a fixed, countable number of outcomes $N$. Easy to list or count with combinatorics.

Examples: Rolling 2 dice ($|S|=36$), 7-horse race finish orders ($|S|=7! = 5040$).
2

Countably Infinite

Contains infinitely many outcomes that can be put into a 1-to-1 correspondence with the natural numbers $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.

Examples: Rolling a die until a 3 appears; tossing a coin until the first Head appears.
3

Uncountable (Continuous)

Contains an entire interval of real numbers; outcomes cannot be listed in a sequence.

Examples: Lifetime (in hours) of a transistor $S = \{x : 0 \le x < \infty\} = [0, \infty)$.

Step-by-Step Exam Derivations

Let $E_k$ be the elementary event that the first 3 appears on exactly the $k$-th roll ($k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$). This requires $(k-1)$ consecutive non-3s (each with probability $q = \frac{5}{6}$) followed by a 3 on the $k$-th roll (probability $p = \frac{1}{6}$).

By independence of successive die rolls:

$$P(E_k) = \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-1}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right), \quad k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Summing over all mutually exclusive outcomes in the countably infinite sample space $S$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(E_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-1}$$

This is an infinite geometric series with first term $a = \frac{1}{6}$ and common ratio $r = \frac{5}{6}$ (where $|r| < 1$). Using $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a r^{k-1} = \frac{a}{1-r}$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(E_k) = \frac{1/6}{1 - 5/6} = \frac{1/6}{1/6} = 1 \quad \blacksquare$$

When a fair coin is tossed until the first Head ($H$) appears, the countably infinite sample space is:

$$S = \{H,\; TH,\; TTH,\; TTTH,\; \dots\}$$

Let $E_n$ denote the outcome that the first Head occurs on toss $n$. Since the coin is fair ($p = 1/2$):

$$P(E_n) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Let $A$ be the event that the first Head appears on an odd-numbered toss: $A = \{E_1, E_3, E_5, E_7, \dots\}$. Because these outcomes are mutually exclusive, by Axiom 3 (countable additivity):

$$P(A) = P(E_1) + P(E_3) + P(E_5) + \dots = \frac{1}{2} + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^5 + \dots = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{32} + \dots$$

This is a geometric series with $a = \frac{1}{2}$ and common ratio $r = \frac{1}{4}$:

$$P(A) = \frac{1/2}{1 - 1/4} = \frac{1/2}{3/4} = \frac{2}{3} \quad \blacksquare$$

1st Incourse 2025 • Q1(a) & Q5 ISRT, University of Dhaka

Q1(a): Distinguish between an experiment and a random experiment with examples.

Q5: A fair coin is tossed repeatedly until the first head appears. Write down the sample space, assign probabilities to each outcome, and find the probability that the first head appears on an odd-numbered toss.

Solution to Q1(a):

An experiment is any process that yields an observation or measurement. In a deterministic experiment, the outcome is uniquely determined by the conditions (e.g., measuring the boiling point of pure water at sea level). A random experiment is an experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty beforehand, even under identical conditions, though all possible outcomes are known (e.g., tossing a coin or rolling a die).

Solution to Q5:

  • Sample Space: $S = \{H, TH, TTH, TTTH, \dots\}$ (countably infinite).
  • Probability Assignment: Let $E_k$ be getting $(k-1)$ tails followed by a head on toss $k$. Then $P(E_k) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^k$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$
  • Odd Toss Probability: $P(\text{odd}) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2m+1} = \frac{1/2}{1 - 1/4} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$.
1st Incourse 2022 • Q1 [2 pts] & Q5 [3 pts] ISRT, University of Dhaka

Q1: Consider an experiment of rolling one die. Define trial, outcome, sample space, and event for this experiment.

Q5: A medical technologist conducted tests on three samples for possible Dengue infection. S/he categorizes each test result as being positive or negative. Write down the sample space. What is the probability that none of the results is positive? What is the probability that at least one of the results is positive?

Solution to Q1 (Rolling One Die):

  • Trial: Rolling the die once.
  • Outcome: The face number observed on top (e.g., 4).
  • Sample Space: $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.
  • Event: Any subset of $S$, e.g., $E = \{2, 4, 6\}$ (getting an even number).

Solution to Q5 (Dengue Tests):

Let $P$ = Positive and $N$ = Negative. The sample space has $2^3 = 8$ equally likely outcomes:

S = { PPP, PPN, PNP, NPP, PNN, NPN, NNP, NNN }

  • Let $A$ = none of the results is positive $= \{NNN\}$. Then $P(A) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{8}} = 0.125$.
  • Let $B$ = at least one result is positive $= A^c$. Then $P(B) = 1 - P(A) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = \mathbf{\frac{7}{8}} = 0.875$.
1st Incourse 2020 • Q5 [3 pts] ISRT, University of Dhaka

Q5: What do you mean by a sample space? Distinguish between a sample space and a set. An experiment consists of rolling a die until a 3 appears. Construct a sample space for this experiment.

Sample Space: The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment, denoted by $S$ or $\Omega$.

Sample Space vs. Set: A set is any well-defined collection of objects with no requirement of randomness. A sample space is a specialized set whose elements represent all mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive outcomes of a specific random experiment. Hence, every sample space is a set, but not every set is a sample space.

Constructing $S$ (Rolling a die until a 3 appears): Let 3 denote rolling a three, and N denote rolling any other face ($\{1,2,4,5,6\}$). Every terminating sequence has zero or more Ns followed by a single 3:

$$S = \{(3),\; (N, 3),\; (N, N, 3),\; (N, N, N, 3),\; \dots\}$$

Note: This sample space is countably infinite.

Year Final 2022 Q1(a, d) & Year Final 2023 Q2(a) ISRT, University of Dhaka

Final 2022 Q1(a) [3 pts]: Define random experiment, sample space, and event. Give an example to explain the concept of mutually exclusive events using a Venn diagram.

Final 2022 Q1(d) [2 pts]: A coin is tossed three times. Calculate the probability of obtaining more heads than tails.

Solution to Q1(a):

State standard definitions of random experiment, sample space $S$, and event $E \subseteq S$. For mutually exclusive events, let a fair die be rolled ($S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$), with $A = \{1, 3, 5\}$ (odd) and $B = \{2, 4, 6\}$ (even). Since $A \cap B = \emptyset$, they cannot occur together—shown by two non-overlapping circles $A$ and $B$ inside rectangle $S$.

Solution to Q1(d):

For 3 tosses of a fair coin, $|S| = 2^3 = 8$:

S = { HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT }

Let $E$ be the event of obtaining more heads than tails (i.e., 2 or 3 heads):

E = { HHH, HHT, HTH, THH }  ⇒  |E| = 4

Therefore, $P(E) = \frac{|E|}{|S|} = \frac{4}{8} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}} = 0.5$.
