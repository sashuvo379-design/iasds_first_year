Lesson 4: Probability Definitions, Axioms & Inequalities

How do we rigorously assign numbers to uncertainty? In this lesson, we move from classical counting and long-run relative frequencies to Kolmogorov's three axioms—and derive the exact laws, odds formulas, and Boole's & Bonferroni's inequalities tested repeatedly in ISRT exams.

Section 01 • Theoretical Foundations

Core Concepts & Intuition

Aligned with AST 102 Lectures 05–09 & Sheldon Ross Ch. 2.3–2.5

1.1 Three Approaches to Probability

1. Classical (A Priori)

Applies when a random experiment has a finite sample space $S$ whose elementary outcomes are equally likely and mutually exclusive. Determined logically before running trials.

$$P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)} = \frac{\text{Favorable Outcomes}}{\text{Total Outcomes}}$$
2. Empirical (A Posteriori)

Also called the Relative Frequency approach. Based on repeating an experiment $n$ times under identical conditions—dividing past occurrences $n(E)$ by total trials $n$.

$$P(E) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n(E)}{n}$$
3. Axiomatic (Kolmogorov)

The modern mathematical foundation. Defines probability as any real-valued set function $P$ mapping events of $S$ to $[0,1]$ that satisfies Kolmogorov's three axioms—free of circularity or finite-space limits.

Valid for finite, countably infinite, & continuous sample spaces
!
ISRT Exam Favorite • 1st Incourse 2020 Q3 & Year Final

3 Classic Limitations of the Classical Definition

  1. 1. Unequally Likely Outcomes

    Fails whenever outcomes do not have equal symmetry (e.g., a loaded/weighted die or biased coin where $P(H) = 2/3$).

  2. 2. Infinite Sample Spaces

    Fails when $n(S) = \infty$, whether countably infinite (rolling a die until a 3 appears) or uncountably infinite (transistor lifetime $x \ge 0$).

  3. 3. Circular Reasoning

    Defining probability using the phrase "equally likely" is circular—because "equally likely" implicitly means "having equal probability."

1.2 Kolmogorov's Three Axioms of Probability

Axiom 1 • Non-Negativity
$$P(E) \ge 0 \quad (\text{or } 0 \le P(E) \le 1)$$

Every event $E \subseteq S$ is assigned a non-negative real number. Negative probabilities are strictly impossible.

Axiom 2 • Normalization
$$P(S) = 1$$

The probability of the entire sample space (the sure/certain event $S$) is exactly $1$. Some outcome in $S$ must occur.

Axiom 3 • Countable Additivity
$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} E_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(E_i)$$

Holds for any sequence of pairwise mutually exclusive events $E_1, E_2, \dots$ where $E_i \cap E_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$.

1.3 Essential Derived Laws & Odds

Complement Rule & Null Event
$$P(A') = 1 - P(A) \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(\emptyset) = 0$$

Since $A \cup A' = S$ and $A \cap A' = \emptyset$, Axioms 2 and 3 yield $P(A) + P(A') = 1$. Setting $A = S$ immediately gives $P(\emptyset) = 0$.

Monotonicity Property
$$A \subseteq B \implies P(A) \le P(B) \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$$

A smaller event can never have a larger probability than an event that contains it. Because $A \cap B \subseteq A$, we always have $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$.

Addition Law & 3-Event Inclusion-Exclusion
$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$ $$P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(AB) - P(BC) - P(AC) + P(ABC)$$

Subtracting $P(A \cap B)$ corrects for double-counting the overlap. For mutually exclusive events, $P(A \cap B) = 0$.

Odds in Favor (Lecture 06)
$$\text{Odds}(A) = \frac{P(A)}{P(A')} = \frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)}$$

Lecture 06 Example (Matt's Lottery): Out of 200 tickets sold, Matt bought 10 tickets. His odds in favor of winning are $\frac{10}{200 - 10} = \frac{10}{190} = \mathbf{1 : 19}$ (1 chance to win vs. 19 chances to lose).

1.4 Boole's & Bonferroni's Inequalities

Bonferroni's Inequality
$$P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1$$

Provides a guaranteed lower bound on the probability that two events occur simultaneously. Whenever $P(A) + P(B) > 1$, events $A$ and $B$ cannot be mutually exclusive—they must overlap by at least $P(A) + P(B) - 1$.

Boole's Inequality (Union Bound & ISRT Complement Form)
$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)$$
ISRT Lecture 09 / Final Exam Equivalent Form:
$$P(A \cap B) \ge 1 - P(A') - P(B')$$

Notice that $1 - P(A') - P(B') = 1 - (1 - P(A)) - (1 - P(B)) = P(A) + P(B) - 1$—both forms give the exact same lower bound!

✕
ISRT Exam Trap • 1st Incourse 2024 Q2(iv)

Watch the Direction of the Inequality Sign!

The 1st Incourse 2024 exam asked: "Do you agree or disagree: For any events $A$ and $B$ on $\Omega$, $P(A \cap B) \le 1 - P(\bar{A}) - P(\bar{B})$?"

Verdict: DISAGREE! The inequality sign is reversed. Boole's inequality requires $\ge$, NOT $\le$. (Counterexample: if $P(A) = 0.8, P(B) = 0.9, P(A \cap B) = 0.75$, then $1 - 0.2 - 0.1 = 0.70$, and $0.75 \not\le 0.70$.)

Section 03 • Exam Proofs

Proofs & Derivations Drawer

5 high-frequency AST 102 theorem proofs — click to expand

Part (a): Prove $P(\emptyset) = 0$.

Consider the sample space $S$ and the null event $\emptyset$. They are mutually exclusive ($S \cap \emptyset = \emptyset$) and satisfy $S \cup \emptyset = S$. By Axiom 3:

$$P(S) = P(S \cup \emptyset) = P(S) + P(\emptyset) \implies 1 = 1 + P(\emptyset) \implies P(\emptyset) = 0$$

Part (b): Prove $0 \le P(A) \le 1$.

By Axiom 1 (non-negativity), $P(A) \ge 0$ and $P(A') \ge 0$. Furthermore, $A$ and $A'$ are mutually exclusive ($A \cap A' = \emptyset$) with $A \cup A' = S$. By Axioms 2 and 3:

$$P(A) + P(A') = P(S) = 1 \implies P(A) = 1 - P(A') \le 1 \quad (\text{since } P(A') \ge 0)$$ $$\therefore \; 0 \le P(A) \le 1 \quad \blacksquare$$

Suppose $A \subseteq B$. We can express $B$ as the union of two mutually exclusive events:

$$B = A \cup (B \cap A'), \qquad A \cap (B \cap A') = \emptyset$$

Applying Axiom 3 (additivity for disjoint events):

$$P(B) = P(A) + P(B \cap A')$$

By Axiom 1, $P(B \cap A') \ge 0$. Therefore $P(B) \ge P(A)$, i.e., $P(A) \le P(B)$.

Corollary ($P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$): For any two events $A$ and $B$, every outcome in $A \cap B$ also belongs to $A$, so $A \cap B \subseteq A$. By monotonicity, $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$. $\blacksquare$

Write $A \cup B$, $A$, and $B$ as unions of pairwise disjoint sets:

$$A \cup B = (A \cap B') \cup (A \cap B) \cup (B \cap A')$$ $$A = (A \cap B') \cup (A \cap B), \qquad B = (A \cap B) \cup (B \cap A')$$

Applying Axiom 3 to $A$ and $B$ gives $P(A) = P(A \cap B') + P(A \cap B)$ and $P(B \cap A') = P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.

Now apply Axiom 3 to the three disjoint components of $A \cup B$:

$$P(A \cup B) = \underbrace{P(A \cap B') + P(A \cap B)}_{P(A)} + \underbrace{P(B \cap A')}_{P(B) - P(A \cap B)} = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \quad \blacksquare$$

Bonferroni's Form: Since $A \cup B \subseteq S$, Axiom 1 & Monotonicity give $P(A \cup B) \le 1$. Substituting the Addition Law:

$$P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = P(A \cup B) \le 1 \implies P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1$$

Boole's Complement Form (Lecture 09): Substitute $P(A) = 1 - P(A')$ and $P(B) = 1 - P(B')$ into $P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B)$:

$$P(A \cap B) = \big(1 - P(A')\big) + \big(1 - P(B')\big) - P(A \cup B) = 1 - P(A') - P(B') + \underbrace{\big(1 - P(A \cup B)\big)}_{\ge\, 0}$$ $$\therefore \; P(A \cap B) \ge 1 - P(A') - P(B') \quad \blacksquare$$

Group $(A \cup B)$ as a single event $D = A \cup B$ and apply the 2-event Addition Law:

$$P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(D \cup C) = P(A \cup B) + P(C) - P\big((A \cup B) \cap C\big)$$

Expand $P(A \cup B)$ and use the Distributive Law $(A \cup B) \cap C = (A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C)$:

$$P\big((A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C)\big) = P(A \cap C) + P(B \cap C) - P(A \cap B \cap C)$$

Substituting back yields the 3-event Inclusion-Exclusion identity:

$$P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(B \cap C) - P(A \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \quad \blacksquare$$

1st Incourse 2025 • Q1 & Q2 AST 102 • ISRT, DU

Q1: Distinguish between (a) experiment and random experiment, and (b) probability and proportion. State the three axioms of probability.

Q2: Given $P(A) = \dfrac{1}{3}$, $P(B) = \dfrac{1}{2}$, and $P(A \cup B) = \dfrac{3}{4}$, find (i) $P(A \cap B)$, (ii) $P(A' \cup B')$, and (iii) $P(A' \cap B)$.

Q1 Summary: A random experiment has an unpredictable outcome even under identical conditions (unlike a deterministic experiment). Probability is a theoretical likelihood of future events, whereas proportion is an observed fraction in an existing dataset. Axioms: (1) $0 \le P(E) \le 1$, (2) $P(S)=1$, (3) $P(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(E_i)$ for pairwise disjoint $E_i$.

Q2 Calculations:

(i) $P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{4 + 6 - 9}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{12}}$.

(ii) By De Morgan's Law, $P(A' \cup B') = P((A \cap B)') = 1 - P(A \cap B) = 1 - \frac{1}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{11}{12}}$.

(iii) $P(A' \cap B) = P(B) - P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{5}{12}}$.

1st Incourse 2024 • Q1 & Q2(iv) AST 102 • ISRT, DU

Q1 [2]: Give classical and empirical definitions of probability.

Q2(iv) [1]: Do you agree or disagree? "For any events $A$ and $B$ on the sample space $\Omega$, $P(A \cap B) \le 1 - P(\bar{A}) - P(\bar{B})$."

Q1: Classical: $P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)}$ for finite, equally likely outcomes. Empirical: $P(E) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n(E)}{n}$ where $n(E)$ is the count of occurrences in $n$ repeated trials.

Q2(iv): DISAGREE. By Boole's/Bonferroni's inequality, the true universal relation is $P(A \cap B) \ge 1 - P(\bar{A}) - P(\bar{B})$ (the sign in the statement is flipped).

Counterexample: Let $P(A) = 0.8$, $P(B) = 0.9$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.75$. Then $1 - P(\bar{A}) - P(\bar{B}) = 1 - 0.2 - 0.1 = 0.70$. Clearly $0.75 > 0.70$, disproving the $\le$ claim.

Year Final 2025 • Q1(a) & Q2(c) AST 102 • ISRT, DU

Q1(a) [1]: The number of times a particular event occurred in the past is divided by the number of occurrences (trials). What is this approach to probability called?

Q2(c) [4]: At the production of a certain item, defects $A$ and $B$ can occur with $P(A)=0.1$, $P(B)=0.2$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.05$. Find the probability of: (i) at least one defect, (ii) defect $A$ but not $B$, (iii) none of the defects, and (iv) precisely one of the defects.

Q1(a): The Empirical (Relative Frequency / A Posteriori) approach to probability.

Q2(c):

  • (i) At least one: $P(A \cup B) = 0.1 + 0.2 - 0.05 = \mathbf{0.25}$.
  • (ii) $A$ but not $B$: $P(A \cap B') = P(A) - P(A \cap B) = 0.1 - 0.05 = \mathbf{0.05}$.
  • (iii) None: $P(A' \cap B') = 1 - P(A \cup B) = 1 - 0.25 = \mathbf{0.75}$.
  • (iv) Precisely one: $P(A \cap B') + P(A' \cap B) = 0.05 + (0.2 - 0.05) = \mathbf{0.20}$.
1st Incourse 2017 • Q4 [2 Marks] AST 102 • ISRT, DU

A die is such that an even number is twice as likely to occur as an odd number. If $E$ is the event that a number less than 4 occurs on a single toss of the die, find $P(E)$.

Let $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. Let each odd face have probability $p$, so each even face has probability $2p$:

$P(\{1\}) = P(\{3\}) = P(\{5\}) = p$ and $P(\{2\}) = P(\{4\}) = P(\{6\}) = 2p$.

By Axioms 2 and 3, the probabilities over $S$ must sum to 1:

$$3p + 3(2p) = 1 \implies 9p = 1 \implies p = \frac{1}{9}$$

For $E = \{1, 2, 3\}$ (number less than 4):

$$P(E) = P(\{1\}) + P(\{2\}) + P(\{3\}) = \frac{1}{9} + \frac{2}{9} + \frac{1}{9} = \frac{4}{9} \approx 0.4444$$
1st Incourse 2020 • Q1, Q2, Q3 [6 Marks] AST 102 • ISRT, DU

Q1 [2]: Prove that $0 \le P(A) \le 1$.

Q2 [2]: Prove or disprove $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$.

Q3 [2]: Give the classical definition of probability and state its limitations.

Q1: From Axiom 1, $P(A) \ge 0$ and $P(A') \ge 0$. Since $A \cup A' = S$ and $A \cap A' = \emptyset$, $P(A) + P(A') = P(S) = 1 \implies P(A) = 1 - P(A') \le 1$. Thus $0 \le P(A) \le 1$.

Q2 (TRUE — Prove): Write $A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B')$, a disjoint union. By Axiom 3, $P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B')$. Since $P(A \cap B') \ge 0$, it follows that $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$.

Q3: Classical definition: $P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)}$ when $S$ has finitely many equally likely outcomes. Limitations: (1) fails for unequally likely outcomes, (2) fails for infinite sample spaces, and (3) relies on circular reasoning ("equally likely").
