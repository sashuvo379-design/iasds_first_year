01 Conditional Probability & Reduced Sample Space

Ross Ch. 3.2 · 1st Incourse 2024 Q4

For two events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is the probability that $A$ occurs once we know for certain that $B$ has already occurred:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0$$

Intuition (Reduced Sample Space): Knowing that $B$ occurred discards every outcome outside $B$. The sample space shrinks from $\Omega$ to $B$, and we measure what fraction of $B$ also lies inside $A$ (namely $A \cap B$).

Unconditional vs. Conditional
Unconditional $P(A)$

Evaluated over the entire original sample space $\Omega$ with no prior information about other events.

Conditional $P(A \mid B)$

Evaluated over the reduced sample space $B$, rescaling probabilities by dividing by $P(B)$ so $P(B \mid B) = 1$.

! ISRT Exam Trap Callout · Year Final 2020 Q1(ii) & Final Q1a(iv) FALSE / DISPROVED

"Prove or Disprove: Conditional probability is always less than unconditional probability."

Disproved! Conditional probability $P(A \mid B)$ can be greater than, equal to, or less than $P(A)$. Consider rolling a fair six-sided die ($\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$) with conditioning event $B = \{2, 4, 6\}$ ($P(B) = 1/2$):

Case 1: $P(A \mid B) > P(A)$

Let $A = \{2\}$ ($P(A) = \frac{1}{6}$).

$P(A \mid B) = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3} > \frac{1}{6}$

Case 2: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$

Let $A = \{1, 2\}$ ($P(A) = \frac{1}{3}$).

$P(A \mid B) = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$

Case 3: $P(A \mid B) < P(A)$

Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ ($P(A) = \frac{1}{2}$).

$P(A \mid B) = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3} < \frac{1}{2}$

02 General Multiplication Rule

Year Final 2025 Q3(a)
Two Events ($A$ and $B$)
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) = P(B)P(A \mid B)$$

Rearranging the conditional probability definition lets us find joint probabilities sequentially—essential for sampling without replacement.

Three Events ($A, B, C$) · Final 2025 Q3(a)
$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\,P(C \mid A \cap B)$$

Under Mutual Independence: $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$ and $P(C \mid A \cap B) = P(C)$, so the chain rule simplifies cleanly to: $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)$.

03 Independence vs. Mutual Exclusivity

1st Incourse 2024 Q2(ii) · Year Final 2020 Q1(i)

Two events $A$ and $B$ are statistically independent if and only if knowing one occurred does not change the probability of the other: $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad P(A \mid B) = P(A) \;\text{ (when } P(B) > 0\text{)}$$

Property Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Independent
Defining Equation $A \cap B = \emptyset \implies P(A \cap B) = 0$ $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$
Conditional Effect Maximal dependence: If $B$ occurs, $A$ becomes impossible ($P(A \mid B) = 0$). Zero dependence: Knowing $B$ leaves $A$'s probability unchanged ($P(A \mid B) = P(A)$).
Venn Diagram Two separated, non-overlapping circles. Circles MUST overlap (if $P(A)>0, P(B)>0$) with overlap area equal to $P(A)P(B)$.
Classic Exam Trap If $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$, then $P(A)P(B) > 0 \neq 0$, so mutually exclusive events can NEVER be independent!
Complement Shortcut · "At Least One Succeeds"

When $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ are independent (e.g., multiple students solving a problem), compute the probability that all fail and subtract from 1:

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^n \big(1 - P(A_i)\big)$$

04 Law of Total Probability & Bayes' Theorem

Ross Ch. 3.3 · Year Finals 2023–2025

Partition $\{B_1, B_2, B_3\}$: mutually exclusive ($B_i \cap B_j = \emptyset$) and exhaustive ($\bigcup B_i = \Omega$).

Law of Total Probability
$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\,P(A \mid B_i)$$
Bayes' Theorem (Inverse Probability)
$$P(B_j \mid A) = \frac{P(B_j)\,P(A \mid B_j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\,P(A \mid B_i)}$$
Prior: $P(B_j)$ Likelihood: $P(A \mid B_j)$ Total Evidence: $P(A)$ Posterior: $P(B_j \mid A)$

Given that $A$ and $B$ are independent, we know $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. We want to show $P(A' \cap B') = P(A')P(B')$.

By De Morgan's Law, $A' \cap B' = (A \cup B)'$. Therefore:

$$P(A' \cap B') = P\big((A \cup B)'\big) = 1 - P(A \cup B)$$ $$= 1 - \big[P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\big] = 1 - P(A) - P(B) + P(A)P(B)$$

Factor the right-hand side by grouping:

$$P(A' \cap B') = \big(1 - P(A)\big) - P(B)\big(1 - P(A)\big) = \big(1 - P(A)\big)\big(1 - P(B)\big) = P(A')P(B') \quad \blacksquare$$

Hence $A'$ and $B'$ are independent.

DISPROVED (False). Let a fair six-sided die be rolled ($\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$), and fix $B = \{2, 4, 6\}$ so $P(B) = \frac{1}{2}$.

  • Greater than ($>$): Let $A = \{2\}$. Then $P(A) = \frac{1}{6}$, while $P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(\{2\})}{P(\{2,4,6\})} = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3} > \frac{1}{6}$. (Directly disproves the claim!)
  • Equal to ($=$): Let $A = \{1, 2\}$. Then $P(A) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$, and $P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(\{2\})}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3} = P(A)$ (independent events).
  • Less than ($<$): Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Then $P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$, while $P(A \mid B) = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3} < \frac{1}{2}$.

Thus $P(A \mid B)$ may be larger than, equal to, or smaller than $P(A)$. $\blacksquare$

Let $P(A) = p_1 > 0$ and $P(B) = p_2 > 0$. By Bonferroni's inequality (since $P(A \cup B) \le 1$):

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1 = p_1 + p_2 - 1$$

Divide both sides by $P(A) = p_1 > 0$ (preserving the inequality direction):

$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \ge \frac{p_1 + p_2 - 1}{p_1} = \frac{p_1 - (1 - p_2)}{p_1} = 1 - \frac{1 - p_2}{p_1} \quad \blacksquare$$

1st Incourse 2017 Q1a(ii) Equivalent Form: Setting $P(A)=a$ and $P(B)=b > 0$ and dividing $P(A \cap B) \ge a + b - 1$ by $b$ yields $P(A \mid B) \ge \dfrac{a + b - 1}{b}$.

Part (a): Prove $P(A' \mid B) + P(A \mid B) = 1$ (for $P(B) > 0$).

Notice that $(A' \cap B)$ and $(A \cap B)$ are mutually exclusive with $(A' \cap B) \cup (A \cap B) = B$. Hence $P(A' \cap B) + P(A \cap B) = P(B)$:

$$P(A' \mid B) + P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A' \cap B)}{P(B)} + \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(A' \cap B) + P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B)}{P(B)} = 1 \quad \blacksquare$$

Part (b): Prove that if $B \subset A$ (with $P(B) > 0$), then $P(A \mid B) = 1$.

Since $B \subset A$, every element of $B$ is in $A$, so $A \cap B = B$. Therefore:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B)}{P(B)} = 1 \quad \blacksquare$$

DISPROVED (False in general).

Suppose $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive ($A \cap B = \emptyset$), which forces $P(A \cap B) = 0$. For $A$ and $B$ to also be independent, we would need:

$$P(A)P(B) = P(A \cap B) = 0$$
  • Positive-Probability Case ($P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$): Then $P(A)P(B) > 0 \neq 0$, so $A$ and $B$ can NEVER be independent! (Example on a fair die: $A = \{1,3,5\}$ and $B = \{2,4,6\}$ are mutually exclusive, yet $P(A \cap B) = 0 \neq \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$.)
  • Zero-Probability Edge Case: The only trivial exception occurs if $P(A) = 0$ or $P(B) = 0$ (e.g., $A = \emptyset$), in which case $P(A \cap B) = 0 = P(A)P(B)$ holds vacuously.

Hence the blanket claim "mutually exclusive events are also independent" is FALSE. $\blacksquare$

Year Final 2025 · Q3(c) [4 Marks] & Q3(e) AST 102 · ISRT, University of Dhaka

Q3(c): The probability that it rains on a given day is $0.6$. If it rains, the probability that friends play football is $0.2$; if it does not rain, the probability rises to $0.8$. Find $P(\text{Rain} \cap \text{Football})$, $P(\text{No Rain} \cap \text{Football})$, and $P(\text{Football})$.

Q3(e): Three urns contain white and black balls: Urn 1 has (3W, 2B), Urn 2 has (2W, 3B), and Urn 3 has (4W, 1B). One urn is chosen at random and a drawn ball turns out to be white. What is the probability it came from the third urn?

Solution to Q3(c) — Rain & Football:

  • $P(R \cap F) = P(R)P(F \mid R) = 0.6 \times 0.2 = \mathbf{0.12}$
  • $P(R' \cap F) = P(R')P(F \mid R') = 0.4 \times 0.8 = \mathbf{0.32}$
  • By Law of Total Probability: $P(F) = 0.12 + 0.32 = \mathbf{0.44}$

Solution to Q3(e) — Three Urns:

Priors: $P(U_1) = P(U_2) = P(U_3) = \frac{1}{3}$. Likelihoods of white: $P(W \mid U_1) = \frac{3}{5}$, $P(W \mid U_2) = \frac{2}{5}$, $P(W \mid U_3) = \frac{4}{5}$.

$$P(W) = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5} + \frac{4}{5}\right) = \frac{9}{15} = \frac{3}{5}$$ $$P(U_3 \mid W) = \frac{P(U_3)P(W \mid U_3)}{P(W)} = \frac{\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{4}{5}}{\frac{3}{5}} = \mathbf{\frac{4}{9} \approx 0.4444}$$
1st Incourse 2025 · Q6 [4 Marks] Bertrand's Box Paradox · AST 102

Three identical boxes contain two coins each: Box I contains 2 Gold coins, Box II contains 2 Silver coins, and Box III contains 1 Gold and 1 Silver coin. A box is selected at random and one coin is drawn at random. If the drawn coin is Gold, what is the probability that the other coin in the same box is also Gold?

The other coin is also Gold if and only if we selected Box I ($B_1$). Let $G$ be the event that the drawn coin is Gold.

Priors: $P(B_1) = P(B_2) = P(B_3) = \frac{1}{3}$. Likelihoods: $P(G \mid B_1) = 1$, $P(G \mid B_2) = 0$, $P(G \mid B_3) = \frac{1}{2}$.

$$P(G) = \frac{1}{3}(1) + \frac{1}{3}(0) + \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}$$ $$P(B_1 \mid G) = \frac{P(B_1)P(G \mid B_1)}{P(G)} = \frac{\frac{1}{3}\cdot 1}{\frac{1}{2}} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667}$$

Intuition: There are 3 Gold coins in total—2 of them sit in Box I, so seeing Gold makes Box I twice as likely as Box III!

Year Final 2023 · Q4(d) [5 Marks] AST 102 · ISRT, University of Dhaka

A VCR manufacturer buys LS-24 microchips from three suppliers: 30% from Hall Electronics (3% defective), 20% from Schuller Sales (5% defective), and 50% from Crawford Components (4% defective). A randomly selected chip from the bin is found to be defective. What is the probability that it was manufactured by Schuller Sales?

Let $H, S, C$ denote Hall, Schuller, and Crawford, and $D$ denote defective. Given $P(H)=0.30, P(S)=0.20, P(C)=0.50$ and $P(D \mid H)=0.03, P(D \mid S)=0.05, P(D \mid C)=0.04$.

$$P(D) = (0.30)(0.03) + (0.20)(0.05) + (0.50)(0.04) = 0.009 + 0.010 + 0.020 = 0.039$$ $$P(S \mid D) = \frac{(0.20)(0.05)}{0.039} = \frac{0.010}{0.039} = \mathbf{\frac{10}{39} \approx 0.2564}$$
Year Final 2024 · Q3(c) [3 Marks] & Q3(e) [3 Marks] AST 102 · ISRT, University of Dhaka

Q3(c): In a college, 25% of boys and 10% of girls study mathematics. Girls constitute 60% of the student body. (i) Find $P(\text{Math})$. (ii) If a randomly selected student studies math, find $P(\text{Girl} \mid \text{Math})$.

Q3(e): An insurance company insured 4,000 doctors, 8,000 teachers, and 12,000 businessmen. Probabilities of dying before age 58 are $0.01, 0.03,$ and $0.05$, respectively. If one insured individual dies before 58, find the probability he is a doctor.

Solution to Q3(c) — College Math:

$P(G)=0.60, P(B)=0.40, P(M \mid G)=0.10, P(M \mid B)=0.25$.

(i) $P(M) = (0.60)(0.10) + (0.40)(0.25) = 0.06 + 0.10 = \mathbf{0.16}$.

(ii) $P(G \mid M) = \dfrac{0.06}{0.16} = \mathbf{\dfrac{3}{8} = 0.375}$.

Solution to Q3(e) — Insurance Policyholders:

Total insured $= 4000 + 8000 + 12000 = 24{,}000$. Priors: $P(Doc)=\frac{1}{6}$, $P(T)=\frac{2}{6}$, $P(Bus)=\frac{3}{6}$.

$$P(\text{Dies}) = \frac{1}{6}(0.01) + \frac{2}{6}(0.03) + \frac{3}{6}(0.05) = \frac{0.01 + 0.06 + 0.15}{6} = \frac{0.22}{6}$$ $$P(\text{Doctor} \mid \text{Dies}) = \frac{0.01/6}{0.22/6} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{22} \approx 0.0455}$$
1st Incourse 2024 · Q4 [4], Q5 [3] & Q7 [3] AST 102 · ISRT, University of Dhaka

Q4: Among 100 sports car buyers, 40 bought alarm systems, 30 bought bucket seats, and 20 bought both. If a randomly chosen buyer bought an alarm system, what is the probability they also bought bucket seats?

Q5: An Avon salesperson sells on the 1st visit with probability $0.4$. If she fails on the 1st visit, she sells on the 2nd visit with probability $0.65$ (never visits more than twice). Find $P(\text{makes a sale})$.

Q7: A problem is given to three persons $P, Q, R$ whose chances of solving it are $\frac{2}{7}, \frac{4}{7}, \frac{4}{9}$ respectively. Find the probability that the problem is solved.

Q4: $P(\text{Bucket} \mid \text{Alarm}) = \dfrac{P(\text{Bucket} \cap \text{Alarm})}{P(\text{Alarm})} = \dfrac{20/100}{40/100} = \dfrac{20}{40} = \mathbf{0.50}$.

Q5: $P(\text{Sale}) = P(S_1) + P(S_1' \cap S_2) = 0.4 + (1 - 0.4)(0.65) = 0.4 + 0.39 = \mathbf{0.79}$.

Q7: Assuming independence, $P(\text{None solve}) = \left(1 - \frac{2}{7}\right)\left(1 - \frac{4}{7}\right)\left(1 - \frac{4}{9}\right) = \frac{5}{7}\cdot\frac{3}{7}\cdot\frac{5}{9} = \frac{75}{441} = \frac{25}{147}$. Therefore: $$P(\text{Solved}) = 1 - \frac{25}{147} = \mathbf{\frac{122}{147} \approx 0.8299}$$
