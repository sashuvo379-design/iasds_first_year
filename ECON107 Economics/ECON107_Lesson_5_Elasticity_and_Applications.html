Section 01 • Core Definitions

Price Elasticity of Demand (ED) & Determinants

Definition (ECON107_L5 / Samuelson & Nordhaus)
The price elasticity of demand (ED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price: ED = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price). By convention, economists drop the minus sign and report ED as a positive number.
Value of ED Category Meaning Effect on Total Revenue (TR = P × Q)
ED > 1 Price-Elastic %ΔQ > %ΔP (e.g., cars, luxuries, restaurant meals) P ↓ ⇒ TR ↑  |  P ↑ ⇒ TR ↓
ED = 1 Unit-Elastic %ΔQ = %ΔP (borderline case; rectangular hyperbola) P change ⇒ TR unchanged (Max TR)
ED < 1 Price-Inelastic %ΔQ < %ΔP (e.g., rice, salt, cooking oil, insulin) P ↓ ⇒ TR ↓  |  P ↑ ⇒ TR ↑
ED = ∞ Perfectly Elastic Horizontal demand curve; tiny price rise cuts Q to zero Firm is a pure price taker
ED = 0 Perfectly Inelastic Vertical demand curve; Q does not respond to price at all TR moves strictly in proportion to P

What Determines Price Elasticity?

💎 Luxuries vs. Necessities

Luxuries (cars, vacations, perfume) have high elasticity; necessities (rice, electricity, cooking oil, life-saving medicine) have low elasticity.

🔄 Availability of Substitutes

Goods with many close substitutes (e.g., a specific brand of soda) are highly elastic; goods with few substitutes (e.g., gasoline or addictive tobacco for heavy smokers) are inelastic.

⏳ Time Horizon

Demand is more inelastic in the short run and more elastic in the long run (e.g., when gas prices spike, drivers eventually buy fuel-efficient cars or switch to transit).

Section 02 • Exact Numerical Method

Midpoint (Arc) Elasticity Calculator with ISRT PYQ Presets

To avoid ambiguity over whether to divide by the initial or final price/quantity, economists use the Midpoint Formula: dividing $\Delta Q$ by average quantity $(Q_1 + Q_2)/2$ and $\Delta P$ by average price $(P_1 + P_2)/2$.

Why Elasticity ≠ Slope & The Total Revenue (TR) Box

Consider the linear demand curve Q = 100 − 2P (Final 2017 Q2b & Samuelson Ch. 4 Q11). Its slope is constant ($dQ/dP = -2$), yet its point elasticity $E_D = |dQ/dP| \times (P/Q) = 2P/Q$ changes at every single point! Geometric Shortcut (Segment Ratio):
At any point on a straight-line demand curve, ED = (Length below point) ÷ (Length above point). That's why above the midpoint $E_D > 1$, at the midpoint $E_D = 1$, and below the midpoint $E_D < 1$!
Section 04 • Beyond Own-Price Demand

Price Elasticity of Supply (ES) & Income Elasticity (EI)

🏭 Price Elasticity of Supply (ES)

ES = (% change in quantity supplied) ÷ (% change in price).

  • Momentary Run: Supply is fixed (vertical, $E_S = 0$ — e.g., fresh fish at morning auction).
  • Short Run: Firms can adjust labor/materials but not plant size (moderately elastic).
  • Long Run: New factories can be built and firms can enter/exit (highly elastic).

💰 Income Elasticity of Demand (EI)

EI = (%ΔQ ÷ %ΔI) = (∂Q / ∂I) × (I / Q).

  • EI > 1: Luxury good (demand grows faster than income).
  • 0 < EI < 1: Normal necessity (demand grows slower than income).
  • EI < 0: Inferior good (demand falls when income rises).
  • Growth Forecast (Final 2023 Q3b): %ΔQ ≈ E_I × (%ΔI).
Section 05 • Interactive Policy Lab

Tax Incidence, Agriculture Paradox & Minimum Wages

Golden Rule of Tax Incidence (ECON107_L5)
Tax burden does not depend on who legally writes the check to the government—it depends on relative elasticities. The more inelastic side bears the larger share of the tax!
Consumer Share = ES / (ED + ES)  |  Producer Share = ED / (ED + ES)
Demand Elasticity (ED) 0.20 (Inelastic)
Supply Elasticity (ES) 1.80 (Elastic)
Per-Unit Excise Tax (t) $2.00

🌾 The Paradox of the Bumper Harvest

If one farmer has a bumper crop, their income rises. If all farmers have a bumper crop, market supply shifts sharply rightward (S → S'). Because demand for basic food (wheat, rice, corn) is price-inelastic (ED < 1), the percentage drop in price is far larger than the percentage rise in quantity sold—so total farm revenue (P × Q) FALLS!

🛢️ Crop / Import Restrictions (PYQ 2023 & 2025)

The flip side of the paradox! When government restricts corn acreage (Final 2023 Q3c) or restricts cooking-oil imports (1st In-course 2025 Q5), market supply shifts leftward (S → S'). Because household demand for staple food/oil is inelastic, price rises steeply while quantity falls only slightly—causing total domestic producer revenue to RISE!

⚖️ Price Floor Above Equilibrium (Fig 4-12)

A binding minimum wage (Wmin > Wmarket) creates a surplus of unskilled labor (QS > QD), generating unemployment equal to the gap between workers seeking jobs and employers willing to hire.

📊 Why Elasticity Matters in the Debate

Empirical studies show that the demand for low-skilled/teenage labor is relatively inelastic ($E_D \approx 0.1 \text{ to } 0.3$). Thus a moderate minimum wage hike causes relatively small job losses while raising total wage income of low-skilled workers as a group—explaining why economists still debate its net social impact.

ISRT Past Exam Question (PYQ) Vault (2017–2025)

Q2(b) Calculation: %ΔQ = (270 − 150) / 210 = 120 / 210 = 0.5714 (57.14%). %ΔP = (15 − 8) / 11.5 = 7 / 11.5 = 0.6087 (60.87%). Therefore ED = 0.5714 / 0.6087 = 0.939 (< 1). Interpretation: Demand is slightly price-inelastic (a 1% price drop raises quantity demanded by only 0.94%).

Q5 Cooking Oil Import Restriction: Blocking foreign oil shifts overall market supply leftward (S → S'), raising domestic price from P₁ to P₂. Because cooking oil is an essential household staple, its demand is price-inelastic (ED < 1). Consumers cannot easily cut usage, so the large percentage increase in price far outweighs the small percentage drop in quantity bought—causing total revenue of domestic producers to increase.

Step 1 (Evaluate Q): Q = 300 − 5(25) + 2(4000) = 300 − 125 + 8000 = 8,175.

(i) Price Elasticity: ED = |∂Q/∂P| × (P/Q) = 5 × (25 / 8175) = 0.0153 (highly price-inelastic).

(ii) Income Elasticity: EI = (∂Q/∂I) × (I/Q) = 2 × (4000 / 8175) = 0.9786 (normal necessity; 1% rise in income raises Q by 0.98%).

(iii) Growth Potential (3% income rise): %ΔQ = EI × 3% = 0.9786 × 3% = 2.94% per year (sales expand nearly in lockstep with income). (c) Corn Restriction: Shifts supply left against inelastic food demand → price jumps steeply, raising total farm revenue.

(b) Q = 100 − 2P: Slope = dQ/dP = −2 (constant everywhere). Point elasticity ED = |−2| × (P/Q) = 2P / (100 − 2P).

  • At P = 1: Q = 98 ⇒ ED = 2(1)/98 = 0.0204 (highly inelastic).
  • At P = 25: Q = 50 ⇒ ED = 2(25)/50 = 1.00 (unit-elastic midpoint).

(c) Cigarette Tax: Heavy smokers are addicted → highly inelastic demand → a tobacco tax shifts almost entirely forward onto consumers (steep price hike, tiny drop in smoking). Casual smokers have elastic demand → tax causes a large drop in quantity smoked and a smaller retail price hike (more burden falls on producers).

Rice (Necessity → Inelastic, ED < 1): Essential staple with few substitutes. When price rises (P₁ → P₂), quantity falls only slightly → Total Revenue INCREASES (gained price rectangle > lost quantity rectangle).

Car (Luxury → Elastic, ED > 1): Expensive discretionary item with substitutes (public transit, used cars). When price rises, quantity drops sharply → Total Revenue DECREASES.

Slope measures absolute rate of change ($\Delta Q / \Delta P = -b$), which is constant along any straight line $Q = a - bP$. Elasticity measures percentage responsiveness: $E_D = |\Delta Q / \Delta P| \times (P/Q) = b \cdot (P/Q)$. Even though $b$ is constant, the ratio $P/Q$ starts at $\infty$ at the vertical intercept ($Q=0$), equals $1/b$ at the midpoint ($E_D = 1$), and falls to $0$ at the horizontal intercept ($P=0$). Hence elasticity falls continuously as you move down a linear demand curve.
