Section 01 • Orientation

What Is Economics & Why Study It?

Core Definition (Samuelson & Nordhaus / ECON107_L1)
Economics is the study of how societies use scarce resources to produce valuable goods and services and distribute them among different individuals.

Economics covers a broad spectrum of real-world phenomena—from interest rates and stock markets to why some nations grow rich while others remain poor, business cycles (unemployment and inflation), globalization and free trade, and government policies aimed at growth, price stability, and fair income distribution.

🌍 Understanding Global Events

Helps interpret everyday shocks: geopolitical conflicts (e.g., Russia–Ukraine war, Middle East tensions), pandemics like COVID-19, inflation spikes, and government policy shifts.

🧭 Making Wiser Decisions

Equips individuals and firms to optimize choices under constraints—choosing a career, deciding whether to buy a house, or allocating limited time and budgets.

📊 Value for Data Scientists (ISRT)

Connects statistical modeling with human incentives—essential for econometrics, causal inference (avoiding spurious correlations), pricing algorithms, and public policy analysis.

Section 02 • Twin Themes

Scarcity & Efficiency: The Twin Themes of Economics

Strip away all technical details and two fundamental ideas run through the entire discipline: goods are scarce and society must use its resources efficiently.

1. Scarcity (The Constraint)

  • Definition: A situation in which goods and productive resources (land, labor, capital) are limited relative to unlimited human desires.
  • Economic Goods: Goods that are scarce and must be rationed (usually via positive prices).
  • Free Goods: Goods available in abundance at zero price (e.g., sand in a desert, seawater at a beach).
  • A World Without Scarcity? If infinite quantities of every good existed, all prices would be zero, markets would be unnecessary, and economics would cease to be a useful subject.

2. Efficiency (The Goal)

  • Definition: The most effective use of a society's scarce resources in satisfying people's wants and needs—the absence of waste.
  • Economic (Pareto) Efficiency: An economy produces efficiently when it achieves the highest combination of quantity and quality given its technology and resources—meaning no individual's economic welfare can be improved without making someone else worse off.
  • Sources of Inefficiency: Unchecked monopolies, pollution (externalities), or government corruption push output below potential.
Exam-Ready Synthesis
Without scarcity, there would be no need to economize or allocate resources. Without the pursuit of efficiency, scarce resources would be wasted, leaving society unnecessarily impoverished. Economics exists precisely at the intersection of these two themes.
Section 03 • Two Major Subfields

Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics

Dimension Microeconomics Macroeconomics
Focus Behavior of individual entities: markets, firms, and households. Performance of the economy as a whole (aggregate variables).
Founder & Classic Work Adam Smith — The Wealth of Nations (1776). John Maynard Keynes — The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936).
Key Questions How are individual prices and wages set? How do consumers maximize utility? How do monopolies vs. competitive firms behave? What causes recessions and business cycles? How do we measure GDP, tame inflation, reduce unemployment, and foster long-run growth?
Core Insight Self-interest working through competitive markets can generate societal benefit ("Invisible Hand"). During deep slumps (like the 1930s Great Depression), free markets may not automatically restore full employment; fiscal & monetary policies matter.
Section 04 • Scientific Reasoning

The Logic of Economics & Three Common Fallacies

Economists use the scientific approach—observing economic affairs, analyzing statistics, and studying historical records. Because economic systems involve many interacting variables at once, analysts must guard against three classic reasoning traps:

1. The Post Hoc Fallacy

Post hoc, ergo propter hoc ("after this, therefore because of this"): assuming that because Event A occurred before Event B, Event A caused Event B.

  • Great Depression Example: Rising prices often preceded business recoveries, so policymakers wrongly tried to end the Depression by artificially propping up wages and prices.
  • Key Lesson: Temporal sequence or correlation does not prove causation.

2. Failure to Hold Other Things Constant

Violating ceteris paribus: failing to keep other influencing factors unchanged while isolating the effect of a single variable.

  • Tax Cut Example: After the 1964 Kennedy–Johnson tax cuts, tax revenues rose in 1965. Concluding "tax cuts always raise revenue" ignores that overall economic growth was simultaneously expanding incomes.
  • Demand Curve Example: To observe that higher price lowers quantity demanded, income and tastes must be held constant.

3. Fallacy of Composition

Assuming that what is true for a part (one individual or firm) is automatically true for the whole (the entire market or economy).

  • Bumper Harvest: One farmer with a record crop earns more; if all farmers have a bumper crop, prices plunge and total farm income falls.
  • More Money: One person receiving extra cash is richer; if everyone gets twice as much cash, inflation erodes purchasing power.
  • Tariffs: Protecting one industry helps it; tariffs on all goods hurt national welfare.
Section 05 • Facts vs. Values

Positive vs. Normative Economics

🔬 Positive Economics ("What Is")

  • Describes the facts and cause-and-effect relationships of an economy.
  • Objective and testable: can be confirmed or refuted by theoretical analysis and empirical evidence.
  • Example 1: "Do higher interest rates slow the economy and lower inflation?"
  • Example 2: "Minimum wage laws cause higher unemployment among unskilled workers."

⚖️ Normative Economics ("What Ought to Be")

  • Prescriptive statements involving value judgments, ethical precepts, and norms of fairness.
  • No purely scientific right or wrong answer; resolved through societal debate and political choices.
  • Example 1: "Should unemployment be raised to ensure price inflation does not become too rapid?"
  • Example 2: "The government should raise the minimum wage to help low-income families."
Cool Heads at the Service of Warm Hearts
Positive economics provides the objective, analytical tools ("cool heads") to evaluate the likely consequences of alternative policies, so that society can better achieve its ethical and compassionate goals ("warm hearts").
Section 06 • Economic Organization

The Three Fundamental Problems & Economic Systems

Every human society—whether industrial, developing, or centrally planned—must answer three universal questions:

1. WHAT to produce?

Which goods and services, and in what quantities? More consumption goods today (pizzas, shirts) or more investment goods (machines) for tomorrow?

2. HOW to produce?

Who does the production, with what resources, and which techniques? Labor-intensive vs. capital-intensive; solar vs. coal electricity.

3. FOR WHOM to produce?

How are national income and consumption distributed among households and classes? Market factor incomes vs. social safety nets.

Scarcity means productive resources (land, labor, capital) and goods are limited relative to unlimited human wants. Because of scarcity, society cannot satisfy every desire and must make choices about what, how, and for whom to produce.

Efficiency means using those limited resources as effectively as possible without waste—producing the maximum combination of quantity and quality of goods given available technology, such that no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

Combined Role: Scarcity creates the economic problem of choice, while efficiency guides the optimal solution. Together they form the twin themes of economics.

(a) Importance of Studying Economics: Yes, studying economics is vital because it helps us (1) understand real-world events such as inflation, recessions, geopolitical wars, and trade policy; (2) make wiser personal and business decisions under budget/time constraints (e.g., career or investment choices); and (3) evaluate government policies targeting growth, price stability, and equity.

(b) Twin Themes: Without scarcity, goods would be free and infinite, making allocation irrelevant. Without efficiency, scarce resources would be wasted, lowering living standards. Thus economics revolves around acknowledging scarcity and organizing society for efficient resource use.

(a) Positive vs. Normative: Positive economics describes objective facts and cause-and-effect relationships that can be tested using empirical data (e.g., "Higher interest rates reduce inflation"). Normative economics involves value judgments and ethical prescriptions about what ought to be (e.g., "The government should raise the minimum wage to support low-income families").

(b) Post-Hoc vs. Composition: The post-hoc fallacy assumes that because Event A occurred before Event B, A caused B (e.g., concluding that raising wages/prices cures a depression simply because rising prices preceded past recoveries). The fallacy of composition assumes what is true for an individual part is true for the whole (e.g., one farmer with a bumper harvest earns higher income, but if all farmers have a bumper harvest, prices drop and total farm income falls).

(a) Definition & Benefits for Data Science: Economics is the study of how societies use scarce resources to produce valuable goods and distribute them. For Applied Statistics & Data Science students, it provides domain theory for econometrics, helps distinguish causal effects from correlation, guides pricing/demand forecasting models, and informs evidence-based policy evaluation.

(b) Holding Other Things Constant (Ceteris Paribus): Real economies have many variables changing simultaneously. To isolate the true relationship between two variables—such as tax rates and tax revenue, or price and quantity demanded—all other confounding factors (like income growth or consumer tastes) must be held constant; otherwise, we draw biased conclusions.

A demand curve isolates the inverse relationship between a good's own price and quantity demanded. If other factors—such as consumer income, population, or the price of substitutes—change at the same time, quantity purchased might rise even when price rises (e.g., people buying more cars despite higher prices during an economic boom when incomes surge). Holding other things constant (ceteris paribus) ensures we measure only the pure price effect along a downward-sloping demand curve.

The fallacy of composition is the mistaken assumption that what holds true for an individual part of a system also holds true for the entire system. Example: If one person stands up at a crowded cricket stadium, they get a better view; if everyone stands up, nobody sees better. Economically, if one farmer has a bumper crop, their income rises; if all farmers have a bumper crop, market prices fall sharply and total farm income declines.
