Section 01 • Core Production Concepts

The Production Function & Product Measures

Definition: The Production Function (ECON107_L8 / Samuelson Ch. 6)
The production function specifies the maximum output that can be produced with a given quantity of inputs (land, labor, capital, materials), defined for a given state of engineering and technical knowledge: Q = f(K, L, …).

🏭 Capital-Intensive vs. Labor-Intensive (ECON107_L8)

The same good can be produced using different points on the production function. In the ready-made garment (RMG) industry, developed countries rely on automated expensive machinery (capital-intensive), whereas developing economies like Bangladesh employ many human workers (labor-intensive).

📐 Three Product Concepts (Holding Other Inputs Fixed)

  • Total Product (TP): Total physical output produced (e.g., tons of wheat, cakes baked).
  • Marginal Product (MP): Extra output from 1 additional unit of an input while other inputs stay constant: MP_L = ΔTP / ΔL.
  • Average Product (AP): Output per unit of input: AP_L = TP / L.

• Whenever MP > AP, the average AP is pulled upward.
• Whenever MP < AP, the average AP is pulled downward.

Section 03 • Most Tested Exam Contrast (2023, 2024, 2025 PYQs)

Law of Diminishing Returns vs. Returns to Scale

⚠️ Never Confuse These Two! (2nd In-course 2023 Q1a — False!)
Law of Diminishing Returns (Short Run / 1 Variable Input): Increase ONE input (e.g., labor) while holding all other inputs (land, ovens, needles) fixed → extra output (MP) eventually declines.
Returns to Scale (Long Run / ALL Inputs): Increase ALL inputs together in the exact same proportion (e.g., double land, labor, and capital simultaneously) → see whether output rises more than, equal to, or less than proportionally.
Returns to ScaleDefinition (Balanced Increase in ALL Inputs by Factor t)Real-World Examples (ECON107_L8)
Increasing (IRS) Output increases more than proportionally ($f(tK, tL) > t \cdot f(K, L)$). Also called economies of scale. Chemical plants (10% more inputs → >10% output), automated factories, mass-production assembly lines.
Constant (CRS) Output increases exactly proportionally ($f(tK, tL) = t \cdot f(K, L)$). Duplicating identical textile/handicraft workshops or retail franchises.
Decreasing (DRS) Output increases less than proportionally ($f(tK, tL) < t \cdot f(K, L)$). Oversized power plants (failure/coordination risks), wine grape growing, city drinking water supply (natural resource limits).

Section 04 • Time Horizons & Innovation

Short Run vs. Long Run, Technological Change & Productivity

⏱️ Short Run

A period in which only variable inputs (workers, raw materials, overtime hours) can be adjusted. Fixed factors (factory buildings, ovens, leases, machinery) cannot be modified.
Example (Final 2024 Burger Stall): If demand drops, cut burger buns/meat orders and reduce student-worker hours.

📅 Long Run

A period long enough for the firm to adjust ALL factors of production, including capital stock, plant size, equipment, and market entry/exit.
Example (Final 2024 Burger Stall): Sell extra grills/fryers, downsize stall space, or exit the burger business.

💡 Technological Change & Productivity

  • Process vs. Product Innovation: Improving production methods (e.g., automated assembly) or inventing new/better products (smartphones).
  • Graph Effect: Technological change shifts the entire production function upward (more output from the same inputs, or same output with fewer inputs)—unlike adding more labor, which is a movement along the curve.
  • Productivity: Ratio of output to inputs. Labor productivity = Output / Labor; Total Factor Productivity (TFP) = Output / Weighted index of all inputs.

🤖 Data Science Link: ML-Driven Production (Final 2025 Q4)

Traditional production functions assume static technology, rival physical inputs, and diminishing marginal returns. Machine Learning–driven production challenges these assumptions because: (1) models continuously self-improve with more data (endogenous tech shift); (2) data and trained weights are non-rival (near-zero marginal cost to replicate); and (3) network/data feedback loops can generate persistent increasing returns rather than diminishing returns.

Section 05 • The Nature of the Firm

Why Firms Exist & Forms of Business Organization

Business firms are specialized organizations devoted to managing production. Production takes place inside firms (rather than individuals working in basements) for three core reasons: (1) Economies of specialization & mass production, (2) Raising large financial capital, and (3) Managing & coordinating ongoing activities.

Business Form Ownership & Scale Key Advantages Key Limitations
Individual Proprietorship Owned by 1 person (most numerous; small shops, freelancers). Easy to start, flexible, owner keeps all profits and control. Unlimited liability (personal assets at risk); hard to raise large capital.
Partnership Owned by 2 to ~200 partners (law firms, clinics, accounting). Pools skills and moderate capital; shared management. Unlimited liability (each partner liable for firm's debts); continuity risk if a partner leaves.
Corporation Separate legal entity owned by stockholders (bulk of modern GDP). Limited liability (owners risk only their investment); raises billions via stocks/bonds; perpetual life. Double taxation of dividends; divorce of ownership from control (principal-agent problem between shareholders & executives).
Section 06 • Exam Preparation

ISRT Past Exam Question (PYQ) Vault (2023–2025)

1. Inputs: Capital: grills, fryers, tables, refrigerator. Labor: student cooks, cashiers, servers. Materials: burger buns, patties, potatoes, cooking oil, sauces, packaging.

2. Declining Demand: Short Run: Reduce variable inputs—buy fewer buns/potatoes and schedule fewer worker shifts (grills/stall lease remain fixed). Long Run: Adjust fixed capital—sell extra fryers/grills, rent a smaller stall, or switch to a different menu.

3. MP & AP of Labor: $MP_L$ is the extra burgers produced by hiring 1 additional student worker (holding grills/materials constant). $AP_L$ is total burgers divided by total student workers ($TP/L$).

4. Diminishing Returns & Returns to Scale: Adding more cooks to a fixed small grill eventually crowds the workspace so each extra cook adds fewer burgers (diminishing returns). By contrast, increasing/decreasing returns to scale describe whether doubling ALL inputs (stalls, grills, cooks, ingredients) yields more than or less than double the output.

(a) FALSE: The law of diminishing returns is a short-run concept where only one input (e.g., needles or labor) increases while cotton, thread, and cloth are held fixed. Decreasing returns to scale is a long-run concept where all inputs (cotton, thread, needles, cloth) increase proportionally together, yet toy output rises less than proportionally.

(b) FALSE: Marginal product of needle ($MP_N = \Delta TP / \Delta N$) is the extra soft toys produced by adding 1 more needle (other inputs fixed), whereas average product of needle ($AP_N = TP / N$) is the total toys produced divided by the total number of needles used.

(a) Flour Definitions: TP: Total cakes baked with a given kg of flour (other inputs fixed). MP of flour: Extra cakes from 1 extra kg of flour ($\Delta TP / \Delta F$). AP of flour: Total cakes per kg of flour ($TP / F$).

(b) IRS vs. DRS: If doubling flour, butter, eggs, sugar, ovens, and bakers yields >2× cakes, that's IRS; if it yields <2× cakes, that's DRS.

(c) Cobb-Douglas Proof (e.g., $Q = A K^\alpha L^\beta$ with $\alpha + \beta < 1$, say $0.4 + 0.4 = 0.8$): Increase both $K$ and $L$ by 50% ($t = 1.5$): $Q' = A(1.5K)^\alpha(1.5L)^\beta = 1.5^{\alpha+\beta} Q$. Since $\alpha+\beta < 1$, $1.5^{\alpha+\beta} < 1.5$ (for $\alpha+\beta=0.8$, $1.5^{0.8} \approx 1.383$, a 38.3% increase < 50%).

(1) Diminishing Returns vs. DRS: Adding extra flour/sugar while holding oven/eggs fixed yields smaller and smaller extra cakes (diminishing returns). Increasing flour, sugar, eggs, and ovens together by 10% and getting <10% more cakes is decreasing returns to scale.

(2) MP of Sugar & MC: $MP_{\text{sugar}}$ is the additional cake output gained from 1 extra kg of sugar (holding flour & eggs constant). Marginal Cost (MC) is the additional dollar/taka expense required to bake 1 extra cake ($\Delta TC / \Delta Q$).
