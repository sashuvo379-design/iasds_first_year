Analysis of Perfectly Competitive Markets

Discover how atomistic price-taking firms decide how much to produce ($P = MC$), why a firm losing money should still stay open in the short run as long as $P > \min AVC$, how free entry and exit drive long-run economic profits to zero, and why competitive markets achieve Pareto efficiency ($MU = P = MC$).

P = MR = MC (Rising MC)
Zero-Profit Point

Shutdown Point

Efficiency Benchmark
MU = P = MC (Max Surplus)
Section 01 • Supply Behavior of the Competitive Firm

Price-Takers, Horizontal Demand & The P = MC Rule

Core Features of Perfect Competition (ECON107_L10 / Samuelson Ch. 8)
1. Many small firms selling a homogeneous (identical) product (e.g., wheat, financial assets).
2. Price-takers: Each firm is too tiny relative to the total market to affect price—so to the firm's "lilliputian eye," its own demand curve dd is completely horizontal (infinitely elastic) at the market price $P$.
3. Marginal Revenue = Price: Selling 1 extra unit brings in exactly $P$, so P = AR = MR.
4. Profit-Maximization Rule: Produce where P = MC (on the rising portion of MC). Thus the firm's rising MC curve (above min AVC) is its short-run supply curve!

The Short-Run Shutdown Rule (ECON107_L10):
  • If P ≥ min AVC: Revenue covers all variable costs and pays part of fixed costs → Operating loses less than shutting down (e.g., heavily indebted airlines).
  • If P < min AVC: Revenue doesn't even cover variable costs → Shutting down (q = 0) minimizes loss to just −FC (e.g., oil rigs idling in 1999).
Section 03 • Industry Dynamics (Figures 8-5, 8-6 & 8-7)

Market Supply, Short-Run vs. Long-Run Equilibrium & Constant Cost

📊 Market Supply & Short-Run vs. Long-Run

  • Market Supply ($SS$): Obtained by horizontally summing the individual MC (supply) curves of all competitive firms at each price.
  • Short-Run Equilibrium: Number of firms and plants is fixed; supply ($S_S$) is steeper, so a demand surge causes a larger price jump and smaller quantity rise.
  • Long-Run Equilibrium: Free entry and exit allow new firms to enter when profits > 0 (pushing P down) and exit when profits < 0 (pushing P up) until:
    P = MC = min LRAC = Zero-Profit Price

🏭 Constant-Cost vs. Increasing-Cost Industries

  • Constant-Cost Case (Fig 8-7): Output can be expanded simply by duplicating identical factories, machinery, and labor (e.g., textiles/shirts: 100k → 200k/day) without bidding up input prices → Horizontal Long-Run Supply ($S_L$) at min AC.
  • Increasing-Cost Case: Industry relies on a scarce specific factor (e.g., fertile wine land or beachfront property) → expanding output drives up factor prices → Upward-Sloping $S_L$.
Ch. 8 Q8 Insight: "How can competitive profits be ZERO in the long run? Who will work for nothing?"
Zero economic profit does NOT mean zero accounting profit! Costs in economics include all opportunity costs—managers are paid competitive salaries and owners earn a normal return on their capital. Competition wipes out only excess (supernormal) economic profits.
Section 04 • Welfare & Exam Traps

Allocative Efficiency (MU = P = MC) & 5 Classic Fallacies

Why Perfectly Competitive Equilibrium Is Pareto (Allocatively) Efficient (Fig 8-11)
1. Consumers maximize utility where MU = P.
2. Competitive firms maximize profit where MC = P.
3. Therefore MU = P = MC: the marginal benefit to society from the last unit consumed exactly equals the marginal cost to society of producing it! This maximizes Economic Surplus (= Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus).
Qualifications (Two Cheers, Not Three): Fails under market failures (imperfect competition, externalities/pollution, imperfect information) and says nothing about equity (fair income distribution).

Debunking the 5 Classic Competitive Firm Fallacies (Samuelson Ch. 8 Q1)

False! A competitive firm maximizes profit by producing where price equals marginal cost ($P = MC$)—not $P = AVC$. ($P = \min AVC$ is merely the shutdown threshold.)
False! The shutdown point comes where price falls below minimum Average Variable Cost ($P < \min AVC$). When $\min AVC < P < \min AC$, the firm suffers a loss but still covers all variable costs and part of fixed costs—so operating minimizes its short-run loss.
False! You also need AVC to know where the short-run supply curve starts (the shutdown point at min AVC), and AC to determine profitability and long-run entry/exit!
False! $P = MC$ maximizes profit only on the upward-sloping (rising) portion of MC (second-order condition: $d^2\pi/dq^2 = -d(MC)/dq < 0 \Rightarrow d(MC)/dq > 0$). Where MC is falling, $P = MC$ actually minimizes profit!
False (subtle wording)! A competitive firm is a price-taker—it cannot "set" price. Instead, it takes market price $P$ as given and chooses its output level $q$ so that $MC(q) = P$.

(3) Both Maximize Profit at MR = MC — TRUE: Any profit-maximizing firm ($\pi = TR - TC$) maximizes profit where $d\pi/dq = MR - MC = 0 \Rightarrow MR = MC$. For a monopoly, $MR < P$, so it sets $MR = MC < P$. For a perfect competitor, since price is constant, $MR = P$, so the universal $MR = MC$ rule simply reduces to the special case $P = MC$!

(4) Shutdown Condition: Draw U-shaped MC cutting AVC at $M'$ ($P_S = \min AVC$) and AC at $M$ (zero-profit point). Explain that for $P < P_S$, $TR < VC$ (losses exceed FC), so the firm shuts down ($q = 0$); for $P_S < P < \min AC$, the firm operates at a loss to minimize total loss below FC.

FALSE. The shutdown point occurs where price falls below minimum Average Variable Cost ($P < \min AVC$), not minimum Average Cost ($AC$). Between $\min AVC$ and $\min AC$, the firm's revenues cover all variable costs and contribute toward fixed costs, so staying open yields a smaller loss than shutting down.

(a) Zero-Profit & Shutdown Points: Sketch Figure 8-3 showing MC cutting AC at its minimum $M$ (Zero-Profit Point, where $P = \min AC \Rightarrow \pi = 0$) and cutting AVC at its minimum $M'$ (Shutdown Point, where $P_S = \min AVC \Rightarrow TR = VC$ and $\pi = -FC$).

(b) Parallel Slopes of TR and TC: Profit is $\pi(q) = TR(q) - TC(q)$. At maximum profit, $d\pi/dq = d(TR)/dq - d(TC)/dq = 0 \Rightarrow d(TR)/dq = d(TC)/dq$ (i.e., $MR = MC$). Geometrically, the slope of the TR curve equals the slope of the TC curve—meaning their tangent lines are parallel at the profit-maximizing output!
