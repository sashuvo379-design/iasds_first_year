Why Does Demand Slope Downward?

Law of Downward-Sloping Demand: When price rises (holding other things constant), buyers buy less; when price falls, quantity demanded increases. Two forces drive this:

1. Substitution Effect

When the price of coffee (or fish) rises, it becomes relatively more expensive compared to alternatives (tea, chicken), so consumers switch away to cheaper substitutes.

2. Income Effect

When a good's price falls, your real purchasing power increases—your fixed money income can now buy more goods, encouraging extra purchases.

5 Forces Behind the Demand Curve (Demand Shifters)

Force / DeterminantEffect on Demand Curve (Example: Automobiles)
1. Average IncomeAs household incomes rise, people buy more normal goods like cars (shifts right).
2. Population (Market Size)Population growth increases the number of buyers, shifting demand right.
3. Prices of Related GoodsSubstitutes: Higher price of bus fares or tea raises demand for cars or coffee.
Complements: Lower gasoline prices raise demand for cars.
4. Tastes / PreferencesCultural shifts (e.g., cars as status symbols or health trends) shift demand.
5. Special InfluencesWeather (umbrellas in monsoon), safety features, expectations of future price hikes, availability of alternatives.
Section 02 • The Seller's Side

Supply Schedule, Upward Slope & Forces Behind Supply

Supply Schedule / Supply Curve
Shows the relationship between a commodity's market price and the quantity producers are willing to produce and sell, holding other things constant (input prices, technology, related goods, government policies).

📈 Why Is the Supply Curve Upward-Sloping?

  • Profit Motive: Producers supply goods for profit. Higher market prices make production more profitable, attracting more output and new firms.
  • Law of Diminishing Returns: Expanding output (e.g., orange juice or wine) requires adding more labor to limited suitable land. Each extra worker adds less extra product, pushing marginal production cost up—so a higher price is needed to coax out additional output.

⚡ Forces Behind the Supply Curve (Shifters)

  • Cost of Production (Input Prices & Tech): Lower wages, cheaper raw materials (e.g., watch batteries, microchips), or better tech lower costs and shift supply rightward.
  • Prices of Related Goods: If corn prices surge, farmers switch farmland from soybeans to corn, reducing soybean supply.
  • Government Policy: Taxes, minimum wages, and import quotas reduce supply; subsidies and free-trade agreements expand supply.
  • Special Influences: Weather (agriculture), number of firms, expectations.
Crucial Exam Distinction (1st In-course 2019 Q3): Direction of "Increase" Shifts
Increase in Demand: Shifts the demand curve Rightward & Upward (at each price, buyers want more; for each quantity, buyers are willing to pay more).
Increase in Supply: Shifts the supply curve Rightward & Downward (at each price, firms supply more; for each quantity, lower costs allow firms to accept a lower price).

The Backward-Bending Labor Supply Curve

While most product supply curves slope steadily upward, the supply curve of labor can bend backward at very high wage rates (Samuelson & Nordhaus, Figure 8-10). Drag the wage slider below to see how the Substitution Effect and Income Effect compete as wages rise:

Hourly Wage Rate (W) $30 / hr
Exam Justification:
  • Low-to-Mid Wages (Upward Slope): Higher wage raises the opportunity cost of leisure → Substitution Effect dominates → workers supply more hours.
  • High Wages (Backward Bend): Workers earn enough to afford more leisure (a normal good) → Income Effect dominates → workers choose fewer hours (e.g., working 3 days instead of 6, or retiring earlier).
Section 05 • Mathematical Economics (1st In-course 2019 PYQ)

Slopes & Curvature of Inverse Demand Curves

In mathematical economics, an inverse demand curve expresses price as a function of quantity: $P = f(X)$ for $P \ge 0, X \ge 0$. The slope at any output $X$ is the first derivative $ rac{dP}{dX}$, and curvature is determined by the second derivative $ rac{d^2P}{dX^2}$.

(a) Upward Slope: Firms produce for profit. Higher market prices make extra output profitable, and due to the law of diminishing returns, producing additional units with fixed plant/land incurs rising marginal costs—so a higher price is required to coax out greater quantity supplied.

(b) Three Factors for Wrist Watches: (1) Input Prices: Higher cost of quartz movements, stainless steel, or watchmaker wages increases production cost and shifts supply left. (2) Technology: Automated micro-assembly lowers per-unit cost and shifts supply right. (3) Government Policy / Trade: Higher import tariffs on watch components shift supply left, while tax breaks or free-trade deals shift supply right.

Backward-Bending Supply: The labor supply curve bends backward at high wages. Initially higher wages increase hours worked (substitution effect: leisure is costlier), but above a high wage threshold the income effect dominates—workers afford more leisure and cut back hours.

Coffee & Substitution Effect: When coffee's price rises (other things constant), coffee becomes relatively more expensive than close substitutes like tea or energy drinks. Consumers substitute away from coffee toward tea, lowering quantity demanded of coffee.

Ice Cream (Movement vs. Shift): A change in the own price of ice cream causes a movement along the same demand curve (change in quantity demanded). A change in a non-price factor—such as a hot summer heatwave, higher consumer incomes, or lower price of cones—causes a shift of the entire demand curve.

Demand Schedule for Fish (per week): Price 600 BDT/kg → 5 kg; 500 BDT/kg → 8 kg; 400 BDT/kg → 12 kg; 300 BDT/kg → 18 kg. Comment: Quantity demanded varies inversely with price, holding income, tastes, and meat prices constant.

Substitution & Income Effects: When fish prices fall, fish becomes cheaper relative to chicken/beef, so households substitute fish for meat (substitution effect). Simultaneously, paying less per kg increases the household's real purchasing power, allowing them to buy more fish with the same budget (income effect).

(a) Price of Tea Rises: Tea is a substitute for coffee. Higher tea prices make consumers switch to coffee at every coffee price—causing a rightward shift of the coffee demand curve ($D o D'$).

(b) Price of Coffee Rises: This is a change in the good's own price (holding other things constant), causing an upward/leftward movement along the existing coffee demand curve (a decrease in quantity demanded, not a shift).

Curve (a) P = 100 − 5X: Slope $dP/dX = -5$. Thus at $X=0$, slope $= -5$; at $X=1$, slope $= -5$. For any linear inverse demand curve $P = a + bX$, the law of downward-sloping demand holds when $b < 0$.

Curve (b) P = 100 − 20X + X² (valid for $0 \le X \le 10$ where $P \ge 0$): Slope $dP/dX = -20 + 2X$. At $X=0$, slope $= -20$; at $X=1$, slope $= -18$. Second derivative $d^2P/dX^2 = +2 > 0$. Because the second derivative is strictly positive (slope becomes less negative as $X$ rises), curve (b) is convex (like a cup), not concave.
