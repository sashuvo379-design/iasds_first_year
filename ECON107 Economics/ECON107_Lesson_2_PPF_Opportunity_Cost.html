Section 01 • Building Blocks

Inputs, Outputs & Factors of Production

Because goods are scarce relative to wants, every economy must combine its limited resources using existing engineering and technical knowledge to produce useful goods and services.

⚙️ Inputs (Resources)

Commodities or services used by firms in their production processes (e.g., flour, ovens, chefs' time, and electricity for making pizza).

📦 Outputs (Goods & Services)

Useful goods or services resulting from production—either consumed directly (rice, haircuts) or employed in further production (tractors, computers).

The Three Broad Factors of Production

🌱 1. Land (Natural Resources)

Gifts of nature used in production: farmland, forests, minerals, oil/gas reserves, water resources, and urban building sites.

👷 2. Labor (Human Time)

Human time and effort spent in production—factory workers, farmers, teachers, software engineers, and doctors.

🏭 3. Capital (Durable Produced Goods)

Durable goods produced by the economy to produce other goods: machines, roads, computers, trucks, factories, and software. (Note: In economics, capital means physical tools, not cash!)

Section 02 • Core Model

The Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

Exact Definition (Samuelson & Nordhaus / ECON107_L2)
The Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) shows the maximum quantity of goods that can be efficiently produced by an economy, given its technological knowledge and the quantity of available inputs.

Classic Numerical Schedule: Guns vs. Butter (Table 1-1)

Notice how each additional million pounds of butter requires sacrificing a larger number of guns—this is the Law of Increasing Opportunity Cost, which gives a typical PPF its bowed-out (concave) shape.

Possibility Butter (millions of lbs) Guns (thousands) Opportunity Cost of +1 Butter
A015— (All resources in guns)
B1141 thousand guns (15 → 14)
C2122 thousand guns (14 → 12)
D393 thousand guns (12 → 9)
E454 thousand guns (9 → 5)
F505 thousand guns (5 → 0)

Three Zones on Any PPF Graph

✅ On the Curve (Efficient)

Full employment of resources and best available technology. To get more of one good, society must give up some of the other.

⚠️ Inside the Curve (Inefficient)

Feasible, but resources are idle or misallocated (e.g., high unemployment in recessions, outdated methods, or pollution market failures).

🚫 Outside the Curve (Infeasible)

Unattainable with current inputs and technology. Can only be reached in the future through economic growth (outward shift).

Section 04 • Trade-Offs & Real Costs

Opportunity Cost & Productive Efficiency

Opportunity Cost
In a world of scarcity, choosing one thing means giving up something else. The opportunity cost of a decision is the value of the next best good or service forgone.
Productive Efficiency
Occurs when an economy cannot produce more of one good without producing less of another good. Graphically, this means the economy is operating directly on its PPF.

Section 05 • Real-World Applications

Applying the PPF to Society's Choices & Growth

Samuelson & Nordhaus (Figures 1-3, 1-4, 1-5) and ISRT exams frequently test three major macro applications of the PPF, plus the crucial distinction between a movement along vs. a shift of the PPF.

Exam Insight (1st In-course 2023 & 2025): A high-income nation has more capital, skilled labor, and advanced technology, so its PPF lies far outward compared to a low-income nation. A poor nation must devote most resources to basic necessities (point A), whereas a rich nation can enjoy abundant necessities and luxuries like cars or perfume (point B).

Economies trade off private goods (purchased individually) against public goods (funded by taxes: highways, public health, defense, clean air). As nations grow richer, they allocate a larger share of output to public goods and environmental quality.

Sacrificing Today for Tomorrow: A nation that devotes more resources to capital investment (machines, factories, education) instead of immediate consumption shifts its future PPF much further outward, enjoying higher consumption and capital in the future.

Movement Along vs. Shift of the PPF
Movement Along the PPF (e.g., B → D) means reallocating fixed resources from one good to another.
Outward Shift of the PPF means economic growth caused by more inputs (labor/capital growth) or technological innovation. Inward Shift occurs from natural resource depletion, war, or disaster.
Section 06 • Exam Preparation

ISRT Past Exam Question (PYQ) Vault (2017–2025)

(a) Definition & Points: The PPF shows the maximum combinations of rice and perfume that an economy can efficiently produce given its available inputs and technology.

  • Inefficient point (inside PPF): Resources are underutilized or misallocated; the economy could produce more rice, more perfume, or both without any sacrifice.
  • Infeasible/Unattainable point (outside PPF): Cannot be produced with current resources and technology.

(b) High-Income vs. Low-Income Country: Draw Rice (necessity) on the horizontal axis and Perfume (luxury) on the vertical axis. The high-income country's PPF lies further outward (to the northeast) than the low-income country's PPF because it possesses larger capital stock, higher productivity, and superior technology. Consequently, a low-income country devotes most of its capacity to rice (necessity), whereas a high-income country can produce abundant rice and substantial perfume (luxury).

Let $H$ = number of haircuts and $F$ = number of facials. The labor constraint is:

0.5H + 2F = 10 ⇒ H = 20 - 4F

Production Possibilities Schedule: (F=0, H=20), (F=1, H=16), (F=2, H=12), (F=3, H=8), (F=4, H=4), (F=5, H=0).

Shape & Opportunity Cost: Because labor is equally productive across both tasks, the PPF is a straight downward-sloping line connecting (0, 20) and (5, 0) with a constant opportunity cost: 1 facial costs 4 haircuts (and 1 haircut costs 0.25 facials). Opportunity Cost of Education: Yes. Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone. Attending university involves not only direct out-of-pocket costs (tuition, books, fees) but also the forgone earnings (salary) the student could have earned by working full-time during those years—which is typically the largest part of the opportunity cost.

PPF Graph: Plot a concave curve between two goods (e.g., Guns and Butter). Mark a point on the curve as Efficient, a point inside the curve (toward the origin) as Inefficient, and a point outside the curve (northeast) as Unattainable/Infeasible.

Numeric Example: Use the Guns & Butter table: (0,15), (1,14), (2,12), (3,9), (4,5), (5,0).

Change in Technology: Technological improvement allows more output from the same inputs, shifting the PPF outward (if it improves only one good, the PPF pivots outward on that good's axis).

Change in National Income: Higher national income reflects greater productive capacity/capital accumulation (outward PPF) and also changes the chosen point on the PPF—shifting society's choice away from purely necessities toward a higher share of luxuries and public goods.

Combine the four crisp definitions from Sections 1–4: (1) Scarcity: limited resources vs. unlimited wants; (2) PPF: maximum efficient output combinations of Rice and Cars given inputs & technology; (3) Productive Efficiency: being on the PPF where producing more cars requires producing less rice; (4) Opportunity Cost: the tons of rice forgone to produce one additional car.
