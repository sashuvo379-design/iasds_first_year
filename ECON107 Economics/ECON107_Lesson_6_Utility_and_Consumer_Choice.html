Section 01 • Foundations of Satisfaction

Utility, Marginal Utility & Law of Diminishing MU

Core Definitions (ECON107_L6 / Samuelson & Nordhaus Ch. 5)
Utility (U or TU): Denotes satisfaction—more precisely, a scientific construct describing how consumers rank different bundles of goods and services.
Marginal Utility (MU): The additional (extra) utility gained from consuming one more unit of a commodity: MU = ΔTU / ΔQ.
Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility: As the quantity of a good consumed increases, the extra (marginal) utility from each additional unit tends to decline (e.g., the 1st glass of water on a hot day is glorious; the 5th is a chore).
⚠️ Classic Exam Caution (Mihad's Notes)
Falling Marginal Utility (MU) does NOT mean Total Utility (TU) is falling!
• While MU > 0 (even if falling), TU is still rising (at a decreasing rate, concave dome).
• When MU = 0, TU reaches its maximum (saturation point).
• Only when MU < 0 (e.g., eating so much ice cream you feel sick) does TU begin to fall.

Section 03 • Consumer Equilibrium

The Equi-Marginal Principle & Utility Optimization

The Equi-Marginal Principle (Fundamental Rule of Rational Choice)
A consumer achieves maximum utility when the marginal utility of the last dollar/taka spent on every good is equalized:
MU₁ / P₁ = MU₂ / P₂ = MU₃ / P₃ = … = MU per $ of income

🚫 Common Exam Fallacy (2nd In-course 2022 Q2)

Wrong: "Utility is maximized when the marginal utilities of all goods are equal ($MU_1 = MU_2$)."
Why wrong? Prices differ! A 500-taka pair of shoes should deliver far more marginal utility than a 10-taka egg. What must be equalized is marginal utility per taka spent ($MU/P$).

Section 04 • Measurement Scales (1st In-course 2025 Q4a & Final 2017 Q1b)

Cardinal vs. Ordinal Utility

Feature Cardinal Utility (Measurable) Ordinal Utility (Preference Ranking)
Core Idea Assumes satisfaction can be measured in exact numerical units ("utils"), like liters on a gasoline pump. Requires only a preference ranking of bundles ("I prefer A over B") without measuring how much more.
Scale Property Dimensional: quantitative differences and ratios matter (e.g., "10 utils is twice as much pleasure as 5 utils"). Dimensionless (rubber-band scale): any monotonic stretch preserves rankings and the equi-marginal condition.
Modern Status Generally rejected for standard demand theory (subjective pleasure isn't directly observable), though useful for risk & uncertainty. Preferred by modern economics because it relies on weaker, realistic assumptions and still derives all demand curve properties.
Section 05 • Behavioral & Market Applications

Related Goods, The Paradox of Value & Economics of Addiction

🔄 Substitutes

A rise in price of Good A increases demand for Good B (shifts right).
Example (2022 PYQ): Pen & Pencil; Tea & Coffee.

🤝 Complements

A rise in price of Good A decreases demand for Good B (shifts left).
Example (2022 PYQ): Pen & Paper; Cars & Gasoline.

➖ Independent Goods

A price change in Good A has no effect on demand for Good B.
Example (2022 PYQ): Pen & Glue; Rice & Umbrellas.

💧💎 The Paradox of Value (Water vs. Diamonds)

Why is water—essential for life—almost free, while non-essential diamonds cost a fortune?
Resolution: Market price is determined by marginal utility, NOT total utility. Because water is abundant, the last glass has very low marginal utility (used for watering lawns), so its price is tiny. Diamonds are scarce, so the last diamond has high marginal utility and a high price. ("The tail of marginal utility wags the dog of prices.")

🚫 Economics of Addiction (Final 2017 & 2nd In-course 2021)

Addictive substances (e.g., heroin, nicotine) have two distinct buyer groups: casual users (price-elastic demand) and hardcore addicts (highly price-inelastic demand). Strict prohibition shifts supply leftward ($S \to S'$), raising street prices sharply. While high prices deter casual users, hardcore addicts buy nearly the same quantity at much higher prices—raising total drug expenditure and often fueling property crime.

Section 06 • Exam Preparation

ISRT Past Exam Question (PYQ) Vault (2017–2025)

Principle: Consumer maximizes utility when $MU_x / P_x = MU_y / P_y$.

Step 1 (Partial Derivatives): $MU_x = \partial u/\partial x = 0.8 x^{-0.2} y^{0.2}$ and $MU_y = \partial u/\partial y = 0.2 x^{0.8} y^{-0.8}$.

Step 2 (Apply Rule): $(0.8 x^{-0.2} y^{0.2}) / 5 = (0.2 x^{0.8} y^{-0.8}) / 3 \Rightarrow (0.8/0.2)(y/x) = 5/3 \Rightarrow 4y/x = 5/3 \Rightarrow 5x = 12y \Rightarrow x = 2.4y$.

Step 3 (Budget Constraint): $5x + 3y = 75 \Rightarrow 5(2.4y) + 3y = 75 \Rightarrow 12y + 3y = 75 \Rightarrow 15y = 75 \Rightarrow \mathbf{y^* = 5}$ and $\mathbf{x^* = 12}$.

(a) Scale: Use an ordinal scale. Satisfaction from rice, umbrellas, and perfume is subjective and cannot be measured in physical cardinal units; consumers only need to rank bundles in order of preference, which is sufficient for modern demand theory.

(b) Equi-Marginal Principle & Umbrella Price Drop: $MU_R/P_R = MU_U/P_U = MU_P/P_P$. If $P_U$ falls, $MU_U/P_U$ becomes greater than the marginal utility per taka of rice and perfume. To maximize utility, the consumer buys more umbrellas (and reallocates budget from rice/perfume), which drives $MU_U$ down via diminishing marginal utility until all three ratios are equal again.

Q2 (Milk Price Rises): $MU_{tea}/P_{tea} = MU_{milk}/P_{milk} = MU_{sugar}/P_{sugar}$. When $P_{milk}$ rises, $MU_{milk}/P_{milk}$ drops below the other ratios. The consumer reduces milk consumption—raising $MU_{milk}$ via diminishing MU—until equality is restored (explaining why milk's demand curve slopes downward).

Q2: The statement ignores prices. A consumer will not require the last expensive shoe to give the same MU as a cheap candy; utility is maximized when marginal utility per dollar/taka spent ($MU_i / P_i$) is equal across all goods.

By the equi-marginal principle, for all goods purchased in positive amounts: $MU_H / 2 = MU_C / 0.50 = MU_P / 1 = \lambda$ (the common MU per dollar). Hence $MU_H = 2\lambda$ (twice the MU of pizza and 4× the MU of coke), $MU_P = 1\lambda$, and $MU_C = 0.5\lambda$. Because he buys zero hot dogs at $1.50, even the 1st hot dog gives less marginal utility per dollar than his other purchases: $MU_{hotdog} / 1.50 < \lambda \Rightarrow MU_{hotdog} < 1.5\lambda$.

(a) TU rises at a decreasing rate while MU > 0, peaks when MU = 0, and falls if MU < 0. (b) Ordinal utility is preferred because subjective satisfaction cannot be measured cardinally, yet ordinal rankings alone suffice to derive demand curves. (c) Prohibition shifts addictive drug supply left; casual users (elastic) cut back strongly, while hardcore addicts (inelastic) pay sharply higher prices for nearly the same quantity, raising total spending and crime.
