Section 01 • Welfare Measurement

Consumer Surplus & Public Cost-Benefit Analysis

Definition of Consumer Surplus (ECON107_L7 / Samuelson Ch. 5)
Consumer Surplus (CS) is the gap between the total utility (maximum amount a consumer is willing to pay) of a good and its total market value (actual amount paid). It arises because we pay the same market price (what the last unit is worth) for every unit bought, while earlier units have higher marginal utility due to the law of diminishing marginal utility.

💧 Classic Water Example (Fig 5-5 & 5-6)

  • Market price of water = $1 per gallon.
  • 1st gallon worth $9 (surplus $8), 2nd worth $8 ($7), …, 8th worth $2 ($1). 9th worth $0.50 (not bought).
  • Total Value (Utility): $9 + $8 + … + $2 = $44.
  • Total Market Expenditure: 8 × $1 = $8.
  • Consumer Surplus: $44 − $8 = $36 (the area below the demand curve and above the price line).

🛣️ Cost-Benefit Analysis (Highways & Parks)

  • Public goods like a free highway or children's park (2nd In-course 2025 Q2c) earn zero ticket revenue, yet create massive welfare via time saved, safety, or recreation.
  • Highway Example: 10,000 users × $350 individual CS = $3.5 million total CS.
  • Decision Rule: A free public project increases social welfare and should be built whenever Total Consumer Surplus > Total Construction & Maintenance Cost.

Section 03 • Ordinal Preferences

Indifference Curves, Law of Substitution & Indifference Map

Indifference Curve & Marginal Rate of Substitution (ECON107_L7)
An indifference curve joins all combinations of two goods (e.g., Food and Clothing) among which the consumer is indifferent—every point yields the exact same level of total satisfaction.
Its absolute slope is the Substitution Ratio / Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS), which equals the ratio of marginal utilities: MRS = |ΔC / ΔF| = MUF / MUC.

🥣 Why Is an Indifference Curve Convex (Bowl-Shaped)?

By the Law of Substitution: the scarcer a good, the greater its relative substitution value.

  • At Bundle A, clothing is plentiful (6) and food is scarce (1), so the consumer happily swaps 3 units of clothing for 1 extra unit of food.
  • By Bundle C, clothing is scarce (2) and food is plentiful (3), so the consumer will only sacrifice 0.5 clothing for another unit of food.
  • This diminishing MRS makes the indifference curve flatten out as you move rightward—producing a convex bowl shape!
  • Indifference Map: Higher curves to the northeast ($U_4 > U_3 > U_2 > U_1$) represent strictly higher satisfaction.
Section 04 • Core Properties & Exam Contrasts

Special Shapes, Why Curves Never Intersect & IC vs. Isoquant

Logical Proof by Contradiction

  1. Suppose $U_1$ and $U_2$ intersect at bundle A.
  2. Since A and B both lie on $U_1$, the consumer is indifferent: A ~ B.
  3. Since A and C both lie on $U_2$, the consumer is indifferent: A ~ C.
  4. By transitivity of preferences, B ~ C.
  5. Contradiction! Bundle C contains the same Bread as B but strictly more Butter—so a rational consumer must strictly prefer C over B (C > B). Therefore, indifference curves can never intersect!

🥤 Perfect Substitutes (Straight Lines)

Example: Two identical bottles of cola sitting next to each other (or 10-Tk notes vs. two 5-Tk notes). The consumer is always willing to trade them at a constant 1-for-1 ratio (constant MRS), so indifference curves are parallel straight downward-sloping lines.

👞 Perfect Complements (L-Shaped / Right Angles)

Example: Left shoes and right shoes. Having 1 left shoe and 5 right shoes gives no more satisfaction than 1 left shoe and 1 right shoe! Extra units of one without the other add zero utility, forming right-angled L-shaped curves.

FeatureIndifference Curve (Consumer)Isoquant / Equal-Product Curve (Firm)
Decision Maker A consumer choosing consumption bundles (e.g., milk & sugar for tea). A producer / firm choosing input bundles (e.g., milk & sugar to make sweets).
What Stays Constant? Equal level of subjective utility / satisfaction ($U_0$). Equal level of physical output (e.g., $q = 100$ kg of sweets).

Section 06 • Exam Preparation

ISRT Past Exam Question (PYQ) Vault (2021–2025)

(a) Definition: Consumer surplus is the excess of total willingness to pay (total utility) over actual market expenditure.

(b) Numerical & Graphical Value: With 8 pieces dropping linearly from 20 to 6, the step drop is $(20-6)/7 = 2$ Tk/piece: MUs are 20, 18, 16, 14, 12, 10, 8, 6. Total Utility $= 8 \times (20+6)/2 = \mathbf{104\text{ Tk}}$. Total Paid $= 8 \times 5 = \mathbf{40\text{ Tk}}$. Exact Discrete Consumer Surplus $= 104 - 40 = \mathbf{64\text{ Tk}}$! (Check the Cake 2025 preset in Section 2 to see the step-bar graph.)

(c) Children's Park Decision: Although a public children's park is free (zero direct revenue), families derive real consumer surplus from recreation, health, and safety. Using cost-benefit analysis, the local government should estimate total consumer surplus across all neighborhood users and build the park if Total Consumer Surplus > Total Cost of land, construction, and upkeep.

Q4 (Cake CS): Willingness to pay for 7 pieces: 50, 45, 40, 35, 30, 25, 20. Total Willingness to Pay $= 245\text{ Tk}$. Total Expenditure $= 7 \times 15 = 105\text{ Tk}$. Consumer Surplus $= 245 - 105 = \mathbf{140\text{ Tk}}$.

Q5 (Equilibrium Point): Given fixed budget and indifference map for rice and coffee, equilibrium occurs at the point of tangency where the budget line just touches the highest attainable indifference curve ($P_R/P_C = MU_R/MU_C$). It is called an equilibrium because any other affordable point on the budget line lies on a lower indifference curve, while higher indifference curves are unaffordable—so the consumer has no incentive to change bundles.

Budget Line Equation: $1.5 R + 2 F = 12$.

Intercepts & Slope: If all budget spent on Rice ($F=0$): $R = 12/1.5 = \mathbf{8}$. If all spent on Fish ($R=0$): $F = 12/2 = \mathbf{6}$. Slope $= -P_R / P_F = -1.5 / 2 = \mathbf{-0.75}$.

Equilibrium: Plot the straight line from $(0, 6)$ to $(8, 0)$ and superimpose a family of convex indifference curves. The utility-maximizing equilibrium is the tangency point $E$ (e.g., $R=4, F=3$ for symmetric preferences) where $MU_R / MU_F = 1.5 / 2 = 0.75$.

Choke Price ($Y=0$): $P_{\max} = 1,000,000 / 75,000 = \$13.333$ ($40/3$).

  • At $P = \$2$: $Y = 850,000 \Rightarrow CS = \frac{1}{2}(850,000)(13.333 - 2) = \mathbf{\$4,816,667}$.
  • At $P = \$5$: $Y = 625,000 \Rightarrow CS = \frac{1}{2}(625,000)(13.333 - 5) = \mathbf{\$2,604,167}$.
  • At $P = \$20$: Since $\$20 > \$13.33$, nobody crosses ($Y = 0$) $\Rightarrow CS = \mathbf{\$0}$.

(b) Why Build the $5M Bridge? Maximum possible toll revenue occurs at the midpoint ($P = \$6.67, Y = 500,000$), giving only $3.33 million < $5M—so no single toll can cover the cost! However, if built free ($P = \$0$), $Y = 1,000,000$ trips yield a total Consumer Surplus of $\frac{1}{2}(1,000,000)(13.333) = \mathbf{\$6.67\text{ million}} > \$5\text{M}$. Because social benefit exceeds cost by $1.67M, society gains by building the bridge free of toll! (Test this live in the Section 2 Bridge Lab!)

Non-Intersection: Two intersecting curves imply a bundle with strictly more of both goods yields the same satisfaction as a smaller bundle (violating transitivity and nonsatiation—see Section 4 Proof).

IC vs. Isoquant (Milk & Sugar): For a consumer drinking tea, an indifference curve plots milk & sugar combinations yielding equal ordinal satisfaction. For a sweet-shop firm, an isoquant plots milk & sugar input combinations producing an equal cardinal quantity of sweets (e.g., 50 kg).
