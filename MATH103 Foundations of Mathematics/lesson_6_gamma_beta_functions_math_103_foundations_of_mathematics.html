Section 01

The Gamma Function $\Gamma(x)$ & Fundamental Properties

Reading…

Definition 1.1 · Euler's Gamma Function

For any real number $x > 0$, the Gamma function $\Gamma(x)$ is defined by the convergent improper integral: $$\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{x-1}\,dt \qquad (x > 0)$$

Why Gamma & Beta Matter in Applied Statistics & Data Science

ISRT Final 2023 Q3a
  • Continuous Factorials: Generalizes $n!$ to non-integer real numbers (e.g., $\Gamma(3.5)$, $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$).
  • Probability Distributions: Provides the exact normalizing constants for the Gamma, Chi-square ($\chi^2$), Student's $t$, $F$, Weibull, and Beta probability density functions (PDFs) in AST 102.
  • Tough Improper Integrals: Converts seemingly impossible definite and trigonometric integrals into 2-line algebraic evaluations.
Property Formula / Behavior Key Remark
Base Value $\Gamma(1) = 1$ Matches $0! = 1$
Recurrence Relation $\Gamma(x+1) = x\,\Gamma(x)$ Holds for all $x \notin \{0, -1, -2, \dots\}$
Positive Integers $\Gamma(n) = (n - 1)! \;\;(n \in \mathbb{Z}^+)$ Off-by-one: $\Gamma(6) = 5! = 120$!
Half-Integer Anchor $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$ Unlocks all $\Gamma(n + 1/2)$ values
Non-Positive Integers $\Gamma(0), \Gamma(-1), \Gamma(-2), \dots = \pm\infty$ Simple poles (undefined)
Negative Non-Integers $\Gamma(x) = \dfrac{\Gamma(x+1)}{x}$ (finite!) Alternates sign between negative integers

(a) Proof of $\Gamma(1) = 1$: $$\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{1-1}\,dt = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dt = \Big[-e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty = 0 - (-1) = 1 \quad \blacksquare$$

(b) Proof of $\Gamma(x+1) = x\Gamma(x)$ ($x > 0$): Start from $\Gamma(x+1) = \int_0^\infty t^x e^{-t}\,dt$. Integrate by parts with $u = t^x \implies du = x t^{x-1}\,dt$ and $dv = e^{-t}\,dt \implies v = -e^{-t}$: $$\Gamma(x+1) = \Big[-t^x e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty + x\int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{x-1}\,dt$$ At $t = 0$, $0^x e^0 = 0$ (since $x > 0$). As $t \to \infty$, $\lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{t^x}{e^t} = 0$ by repeated L'Hôpital's Rule. Therefore, the boundary term vanishes and $\Gamma(x+1) = x\Gamma(x)$. By induction, $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. $\blacksquare$

Let $u = tx$ ($t > 0$). Then $x = \frac{u}{t}$ and $dx = \frac{du}{t}$. As $x$ runs from $0$ to $\infty$, $u$ also runs from $0$ to $\infty$:

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-tx} x^{m-1}\,dx = \int_0^\infty e^{-u}\left(\frac{u}{t}\right)^{m-1}\frac{du}{t} = \frac{1}{t^m}\int_0^\infty e^{-u} u^{m-1}\,du = \frac{\Gamma(m)}{t^m} \quad \blacksquare$$
⚠️

Exam Trap Alert: Evaluating $\Gamma(x)$ for Negative Non-Integers!

ISRT 2nd Incourse 2024 Q4a

NEVER plug $x \le 0$ directly into $\int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{x-1}\,dt$—that integral diverges at $t = 0$! Instead, extend $\Gamma(x)$ analytically using the inverted recurrence $\Gamma(x) = \dfrac{\Gamma(x+1)}{x}$ until the argument becomes positive:

$$\Gamma(-2.5) = \Gamma\!\left(-\frac{5}{2}\right) = \frac{\Gamma(-3/2)}{-5/2} = \frac{\Gamma(-1/2)}{(-5/2)(-3/2)} = \frac{\Gamma(1/2)}{(-5/2)(-3/2)(-1/2)} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{-15/8} = \mathbf{-\frac{8}{15}\sqrt{\pi}}$$

Section 02

The Beta Function $\beta(x,y)$ & Equivalent Forms

Reading…

Definition 2.1 · Euler's Beta Function

For any positive real numbers $x > 0$ and $y > 0$, the Beta function $\beta(x, y)$ (also written $B(x, y)$) is defined by: $$\beta(x, y) = \int_0^1 t^{x-1}(1 - t)^{y-1}\,dt \qquad (x > 0,\; y > 0)$$ Key quick values: Symmetry $\beta(x, y) = \beta(y, x)$, $\beta(1, x) = \frac{1}{x}$, and $\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \pi$.

Three Essential Forms of $\beta(x, y)$

Form 1 · Standard $[0, 1]$
$$\int_0^1 t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}\,dt$$

Direct definition; powers of $t$ and $(1-t)$.

Form 2 · Trig ($t = \sin^2\theta$)
$$2\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^{2x-1}\theta \cos^{2y-1}\theta\,d\theta$$

Unlocks $\beta(1/2,1/2)=\pi$ & Wallis integrals.

Form 3 · Improper ($t = \frac{u}{1+u}$)
$$\int_0^\infty \frac{u^{x-1}}{(1+u)^{x+y}}\,du$$

Key bridge to the Beta–Gamma theorem.

Start with $\beta(x, y) = \int_0^1 t^{x-1}(1 - t)^{y-1}\,dt$. Substitute $t = 1 - u$, so $1 - t = u$ and $dt = -du$.

When $t = 0 \implies u = 1$, and when $t = 1 \implies u = 0$. Substituting gives:

$$\beta(x, y) = \int_1^0 (1 - u)^{x-1} u^{y-1}(-du) = \int_0^1 u^{y-1}(1 - u)^{x-1}\,du = \beta(y, x) \quad \blacksquare$$

In $\beta(x,y) = \int_0^1 t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}\,dt$, substitute $t = \sin^2\theta \implies 1 - t = \cos^2\theta$ and $dt = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta$.

Limits: $t = 0 \implies \theta = 0$, and $t = 1 \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$. Thus:

$$\beta(x, y) = \int_0^{\pi/2} (\sin^2\theta)^{x-1}(\cos^2\theta)^{y-1}(2\sin\theta\cos\theta)\,d\theta = 2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2x-1}\theta\cos^{2y-1}\theta\,d\theta$$

Setting $x = \frac{1}{2}$ and $y = \frac{1}{2}$ gives $2x-1 = 0$ and $2y-1 = 0$, so: $$\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^0\theta \cos^0\theta\,d\theta = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} 1\,d\theta = 2\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \pi \quad \blacksquare$$

Section 03 ⭐ ISRT Final 2017, 2019, 2020 (5–7 Marks)

The Master Bridge — Relation Between Beta & Gamma Functions

Reading…
Theorem 3.1 · The Beta–Gamma Master Identity
$$\beta(x, y) = \frac{\Gamma(x)\,\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x + y)} \qquad (x > 0,\; y > 0)$$

Step 1 (Scale $\Gamma(x)$): Start from $\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{x-1}\,dt$. Substitute $t = \lambda u$ ($\lambda > 0$ constant, $dt = \lambda\,du$): $$\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda u} (\lambda u)^{x-1} \lambda\,du = \lambda^x \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda u} u^{x-1}\,du$$

Step 2 (Multiply by $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{y-1}$ & Integrate $\lambda$ from $0$ to $\infty$): $$\Gamma(x) \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda} \lambda^{y-1}\,d\lambda = \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda(1+u)} \lambda^{x+y-1} u^{x-1}\,d\lambda\,du$$ The left-hand side is simply $\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)$. On the right-hand side, evaluate the inner $\lambda$-integral using the scaling identity (Proof 1.3) with parameter $1 + u$: $$\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda(1+u)} \lambda^{(x+y)-1}\,d\lambda = \frac{\Gamma(x+y)}{(1+u)^{x+y}}$$

Step 3 (Isolate the $u$-integral): Substituting back gives: $$\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y) = \Gamma(x+y)\int_0^\infty \frac{u^{x-1}}{(1+u)^{x+y}}\,du \implies \frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)} = \int_0^\infty \frac{u^{x-1}}{(1+u)^{x+y}}\,du \quad \text{(Eq. 1)}$$

Step 4 (Match with $\beta(x,y)$ via $t = \frac{u}{1+u}$): In $\beta(x,y) = \int_0^1 t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}\,dt$, substitute $t = \frac{u}{1+u}$, so $1 - t = \frac{1}{1+u}$ and $dt = \frac{du}{(1+u)^2}$. As $t: 0 \to 1$, $u: 0 \to \infty$: $$\beta(x, y) = \int_0^\infty \left(\frac{u}{1+u}\right)^{x-1}\left(\frac{1}{1+u}\right)^{y-1}\frac{du}{(1+u)^2} = \int_0^\infty \frac{u^{x-1}}{(1+u)^{x+y}}\,du \quad \text{(Eq. 2)}$$

Comparing (Eq. 1) and (Eq. 2) yields $\beta(x, y) = \dfrac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}$. $\blacksquare$

Set $x = \frac{1}{2}$ and $y = \frac{1}{2}$ in $\beta(x, y) = \dfrac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}$: $$\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\Gamma(1/2)\,\Gamma(1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2 + 1/2)} = \frac{\big[\Gamma(1/2)\big]^2}{\Gamma(1)} = \big[\Gamma(1/2)\big]^2 \qquad (\text{since } \Gamma(1) = 1)$$

From Proof 2.2, we know $\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta = \pi$. Hence $\big[\Gamma(1/2)\big]^2 = \pi$. Since the integrand $e^{-t} t^{-1/2} > 0$ on $(0, \infty)$, $\Gamma(1/2) > 0$, so $\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \sqrt{\pi}$. $\blacksquare$

Section 04

Trigonometric Integrals, Wallis's Formula & Euler's Reflection Formula

Reading…
Theorem 4.1 · Sine–Cosine Gamma Formula ISRT Final 2023 Q3b (4 Marks)
$$\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^p\theta \cos^q\theta\,d\theta = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{q+1}{2}\right)}{2\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p+q+2}{2}\right)} \qquad (p > -1,\; q > -1)$$

From the trigonometric form of the Beta function (Section 2), $\beta(x, y) = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^{2x-1}\theta \cos^{2y-1}\theta\,d\theta$.

Set $2x - 1 = p \implies x = \frac{p+1}{2}$ and $2y - 1 = q \implies y = \frac{q+1}{2}$ (note $p, q > -1 \iff x, y > 0$).

Dividing by $2$ and applying $\beta(x, y) = \frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}$ with $x + y = \frac{p+q+2}{2}$ gives: $$\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^p\theta \cos^q\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2}\beta\!\left(\frac{p+1}{2}, \frac{q+1}{2}\right) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{q+1}{2}\right)}{2\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p+q+2}{2}\right)} \quad \blacksquare$$

Euler's Reflection Formula (RJT Slides)
$$\Gamma(x)\,\Gamma(1 - x) = \frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi x)} \qquad (0 < x < 1)$$

Worked RJT Exercise: Set $x = \frac{1}{4}$: $$\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi/4)} = \frac{\pi}{1/\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{\pi\sqrt{2}}$$

Dirichlet's Theorem ISRT Final 2019 Q4c (2 Marks)

Over the 3D simplex $V = \{(x,y,z) : x, y, z \ge 0,\; x + y + z \le 1\}$ ($l, m, n > 0$):

$$\iiint_V x^{l-1} y^{m-1} z^{n-1}\,dx\,dy\,dz = \frac{\Gamma(l)\,\Gamma(m)\,\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(l + m + n + 1)}$$

Section 06 · Applied Statistics Bridge

Statistical Connections (Preview for AST 102 / Probability)

Reading…

Every valid continuous probability density function (PDF) $f(x)$ must integrate to $1$ over its support. At ISRT, Gamma and Beta functions serve as the exact normalizing constants for two of the most important probability families in data science:

Gamma Distribution · $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)$
$$f(x) = \frac{\lambda^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x > 0$$

Why it integrates to $1$: By the scaling identity (Section 1), $\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\lambda^\alpha}$. Models waiting times, rainfall, and insurance claims (and gives the $\chi^2_\nu$ distribution when $\alpha = \nu/2, \lambda = 1/2$).

Beta Distribution · $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\beta(\alpha, \beta)} x^{\alpha-1}(1 - x)^{\beta-1}, \qquad 0 < x < 1$$

Why it integrates to $1$: Directly by Definition 2.1, $\int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,dx = \beta(\alpha,\beta)$. The backbone of Bayesian statistics for modeling unknown probabilities, proportions, and click-through rates!

ISRT 2nd Incourse 2024 (AST 103) · Q4(a) [3 Marks]

1. Define the Gamma function and evaluate $\Gamma(-2.5)$.

Hint: Write $-2.5 = -\frac{5}{2}$ and repeatedly apply $\Gamma(x) = \frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{x}$ three times until you reach $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$.

Definition: For $x > 0$, $\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{x-1}\,dt$. For negative non-integers, it is extended via $\Gamma(x) = \frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{x}$.

Evaluation of $\Gamma(-2.5) = \Gamma(-5/2)$: $$\Gamma\!\left(-\frac{5}{2}\right) = \frac{\Gamma(-3/2)}{-5/2} = \frac{\Gamma(-1/2)}{(-5/2)(-3/2)} = \frac{\Gamma(1/2)}{(-5/2)(-3/2)(-1/2)} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{-15/8} = \mathbf{-\frac{8}{15}\sqrt{\pi}} \approx -0.9453 \quad \blacksquare$$

ISRT 2nd Incourse 2024 (AST 103) · Q4(b) & Q5(b) [2 + 2 = 4 Marks]

2. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2} x^{2n-1}\,dx$ ($n > 1$) and express $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^m(1 - x^2)^n\,dx$ in terms of the Beta function.

Hint: Use the exact same substitution $t = x^2$ ($x = t^{1/2}$, $dx = \frac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}\,dt$) for both integrals!

Part (a): Let $t = x^2 \implies x = t^{1/2}$ and $2x\,dx = dt \implies x^{2n-1}\,dx = (x^2)^{n-1} x\,dx = \frac{1}{2} t^{n-1}\,dt$. Limits remain $0$ to $\infty$: $$\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2} x^{2n-1}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{n-1}\,dt = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(n)}$$

Part (b): Let $t = x^2 \implies x = t^{1/2}$ and $dx = \frac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}\,dt$. Limits remain $0$ to $1$: $$\int_0^1 x^m(1 - x^2)^n\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 t^{\frac{m}{2}}(1-t)^n t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,dt = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 t^{\frac{m+1}{2} - 1}(1-t)^{(n+1)-1}\,dt = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}\beta\!\left(\frac{m+1}{2},\; n+1\right)} \quad \blacksquare$$

ISRT Final 2023 (MATH 103) · Q3(b) & Q3(d) [4 + 3 = 7 Marks]

3. Express $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^p\theta \cos^q\theta\,d\theta$ in terms of the Gamma function with proof, and evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-\sqrt{x}} x^{\frac{1}{2}n - 1}\,dx$ ($n > 1$).

Hint: For Q3(b), set $2x-1=p, 2y-1=q$ in the trig Beta form. For Q3(d), substitute $t = \sqrt{x} \implies x = t^2,\; dx = 2t\,dt$.

Q3(b): Since $\beta(x,y) = 2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2x-1}\theta\cos^{2y-1}\theta\,d\theta$, substituting $x = \frac{p+1}{2}, y = \frac{q+1}{2}$ gives: $$\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^p\theta\cos^q\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2}\beta\!\left(\frac{p+1}{2}, \frac{q+1}{2}\right) = \mathbf{\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{q+1}{2}\right)}{2\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p+q+2}{2}\right)}}$$

Q3(d): Let $t = \sqrt{x} \implies x = t^2$ and $dx = 2t\,dt$. Limits stay $0$ to $\infty$: $$\int_0^\infty e^{-\sqrt{x}} x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,dx = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} (t^2)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(2t\,dt) = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{n-2+1}\,dt = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{n-1}\,dt = \mathbf{2\Gamma(n)} \quad \blacksquare$$

ISRT Final 2017 (AST 104) · Q4(b) & Q4(c) [3 + 4 = 7 Marks]

4. If $m$ and $t$ are positive integers, show that $\dfrac{\Gamma(m)}{t^m} = \displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-tx} x^{m-1}\,dx$, and prove that $B(m, n) = \dfrac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}$.

Hint: In 4(b), substitute $u = tx$. In 4(c), apply 4(b) with $t = 1 + u$ inside the double integral for $\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)$!

4(b): Substitute $u = tx \implies x = u/t,\; dx = du/t$: $\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}x^{m-1}\,dx = \frac{1}{t^m}\int_0^\infty e^{-u}u^{m-1}\,du = \frac{\Gamma(m)}{t^m}$.

4(c): From 4(b), $\Gamma(m) = \lambda^m \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda u} u^{m-1}\,du$. Multiply both sides by $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{n-1}$ and integrate $\lambda \in [0,\infty)$: $$\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n) = \int_0^\infty u^{m-1}\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda(1+u)}\lambda^{m+n-1}\,d\lambda\right)du = \Gamma(m+n)\int_0^\infty \frac{u^{m-1}}{(1+u)^{m+n}}\,du = \Gamma(m+n)B(m,n)$$ Dividing by $\Gamma(m+n)$ yields $B(m, n) = \dfrac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}$. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2024 / 2023 Q4(c) / RJT Slides [3–4 Marks]

5. Prove that $\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right) = \sqrt{\pi}$ starting from the Beta function.

Hint: Evaluate $\beta(1/2, 1/2)$ in two ways: (1) via $t = \sin^2\theta$ to get $\pi$, and (2) via $\frac{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(1/2)}{\Gamma(1)}$ to get $[\Gamma(1/2)]^2$.

Using $\beta(x, y) = \int_0^1 t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}\,dt$ with $x = y = \frac{1}{2}$ and substituting $t = \sin^2\theta$: $$\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \int_0^{\pi/2} (\sin\theta)^{-1}(\cos\theta)^{-1}(2\sin\theta\cos\theta)\,d\theta = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta = \pi$$

On the other hand, by the Beta–Gamma relation: $\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \dfrac{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(1/2)}{\Gamma(1)} = \big[\Gamma(1/2)\big]^2$.

Since $\Gamma(1/2) > 0$, taking the positive square root yields $\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \sqrt{\pi}$. $\blacksquare$

RJT Lecture Slides · Exercises [4 Marks]

6. Prove that: (i) $\beta\!\left(3, \dfrac{5}{2}\right) = \dfrac{16}{315}$  and  (ii) $\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right) = \pi\sqrt{2}$.

Hint: For (i), expand $\Gamma(11/2)$ down to $\Gamma(5/2)$ so $\Gamma(5/2)$ cancels cleanly! For (ii), apply Euler's reflection formula with $x = 1/4$.

(i) $\beta\!\left(3, \frac{5}{2}\right) = \dfrac{\Gamma(3)\Gamma(5/2)}{\Gamma(11/2)} = \dfrac{2!\cdot \Gamma(5/2)}{\frac{9}{2}\cdot\frac{7}{2}\cdot\frac{5}{2}\cdot\Gamma(5/2)} = \dfrac{2}{315/8} = \mathbf{\dfrac{16}{315}}$.

(ii) By Euler's Reflection Formula $\Gamma(x)\Gamma(1-x) = \frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi x)}$ at $x = \frac{1}{4}$: $$\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi/4)} = \frac{\pi}{1/\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{\pi\sqrt{2}} \quad \blacksquare$$
