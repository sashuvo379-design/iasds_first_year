Section 7.1

What Is a Differential Equation? ODE vs. PDE

Reading…

Definition 7.1 · Differential Equation

A differential equation (DE) is any equation that involves one or more derivatives (ordinary or partial) of an unknown dependent variable (e.g., $y$ or $u$) with respect to one or more independent variables (e.g., $x, t$, or $x, y$).

Feature Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) Partial Differential Equation (PDE)
Independent Variables Single independent variable (e.g., $x$ or $t$) Two or more independent variables (e.g., $x$ and $y$)
Derivative Notation Ordinary derivatives: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}, \dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}, y', \dot{x}$ Partial derivatives: $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}, \dfrac{\partial^4 u}{\partial x^2 \partial y^2}$
ISRT Exam Examples $\dfrac{d^3y}{dx^3} + 4\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} - 5\dfrac{dy}{dx} + 3y = \sin x$
$x^2 dy + y^2 dx = 0$		 $\dfrac{\partial^4 u}{\partial x^2 \partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} + u = 0$
(ISRT Incourse-1 2022 & 2024)
Section 7.2

Order, Degree & Undefined-Degree Traps

Reading…
Definition 7.2 · Order

The order of a differential equation is the order of the highest-order derivative appearing in the equation. Every differential equation has a well-defined positive integer order.

Definition 7.3 · Degree

The degree is the positive integer power (exponent) of the highest-order derivative after the equation has been cleared of all radicals and fractional powers in its derivatives—provided it is a polynomial in all derivatives.

⚠️

ISRT Exam-Trap Callouts: Radicals & Undefined Degrees

Trap 1 · The Radical Trap (ISRT 2020, 2022, 2024)
$$\left(\frac{dr}{ds}\right)^3 = \sqrt{\frac{d^2r}{ds^2} + 1}$$

Squaring both sides clears the square root: $\left(\frac{dr}{ds}\right)^6 = \frac{d^2r}{ds^2} + 1$. The highest derivative is $\frac{d^2r}{ds^2}$, which has exponent $1$. Therefore: Order = 2, Degree = 1 (NOT $6$ and NOT $1/2$!).

Trap 2 · Undefined Degree (Transcendental Derivatives)
$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \sin\!\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad e^{y'} + y = x$$

Because $\sin(y')$ and $e^{y'}$ expand into infinite power series in $y'$, these equations cannot be written as a finite polynomial in their derivatives. Thus Order = 2 (or 1), but Degree is NOT defined!

Section 7.3

Linear vs. Nonlinear Differential Equations (The 3-Check Rule)

Reading…

Standard $n$-th Order Linear ODE

$$a_n(x)\frac{d^ny}{dx^n} + a_{n-1}(x)\frac{d^{n-1}y}{dx^{n-1}} + \dots + a_1(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + a_0(x)y = g(x)$$

Notice that the coefficient functions $a_k(x)$ and the forcing term $g(x)$ may be arbitrarily nonlinear functions of the independent variable $x$ (like $\sin x, e^x, x^5$)!

CHECK 1 · POWER 1

The dependent variable $y$ and every derivative $y^{(k)}$ appear only to the 1st power (so every linear DE has Degree 1).

CHECK 2 · NO PRODUCTS

No products of $y$ and/or its derivatives (such as $y\cdot y'$ or $y^{(4)}\cdot y^{(3)}$).

CHECK 3 · NO NONLINEAR $f(y)$

No nonlinear functions of $y$ or its derivatives (such as $\sin y$, $y^2$, $e^y$, $\sqrt{y'}$).

ISRT Exam Equation Linearity Exact Reason (3-Check Rule)
$\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y\sin x = 0$ Linear $\sin x$ depends only on the independent variable $x$; $y$ and $y''$ are power 1.
$x^2\,dy + y^2\,dx = 0$ Nonlinear Rewriting as $x^2\frac{dy}{dx} + y^2 = 0$ reveals the $y^2$ term (fails Check 1).
$\dfrac{d^6x}{dt^6} + \left(\dfrac{d^4x}{dt^4}\right)\left(\dfrac{d^3x}{dt^3}\right) + x = t$ Nonlinear Contains the product of two derivatives $\left(\frac{d^4x}{dt^4}\right)\left(\frac{d^3x}{dt^3}\right)$ (fails Check 2).

Section 7.4

Types of Solutions & Initial Value Problems (IVP)

Reading…
General Solution

A solution of an $n$-th order ODE containing exactly $n$ essential arbitrary constants (e.g., $y = A\cos x + B\sin x$ for $y'' + y = 0$). Represents an entire family of integral curves.

Particular Solution

Obtained from the general solution by assigning specific numeric values to the arbitrary constants using initial conditions (at a single point $x_0$) or boundary conditions.

Singular Solution

A valid solution (often an envelope) that cannot be obtained for any choice of constants in the general solution. Example: Clairaut's DE $y = xy' + (y')^2$ has general solution $y = cx + c^2$ and singular solution $y = -x^2/4$.

Step 1 (Separate & Integrate): $\dfrac{dx}{5x - 3} = dt \implies \frac{1}{5}\ln|5x - 3| = t + C_1 \implies 5x - 3 = Ce^{5t} \implies x(t) = \dfrac{3}{5} + Ke^{5t}$.

Step 2 (Apply $x(2) = 1$): $1 = \frac{3}{5} + Ke^{10} \implies Ke^{10} = \frac{2}{5} \implies K = \frac{2}{5}e^{-10}$. Hence the particular solution is: $$x(t) = \frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5}e^{5(t - 2)}$$

Step 3 (Verify Both the ODE & Initial Condition):

  • ODE Check: $\text{LHS} = \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{2}{5}\cdot 5 e^{5(t-2)} = 2e^{5(t-2)}$. Meanwhile $\text{RHS} = 5x - 3 = 5\left(\frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5}e^{5(t-2)}\right) - 3 = 3 + 2e^{5(t-2)} - 3 = 2e^{5(t-2)} = \text{LHS}$ ✓
  • IC Check: $x(2) = \frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5}e^{5(2-2)} = \frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5}(1) = 1$ ✓ $\blacksquare$
Section 7.5

Formation of Differential Equations (Eliminating Arbitrary Constants)

Reading…

The Golden Rule of Formation

If a family of curves $F(x, y, c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n) = 0$ contains $n$ essential (independent) arbitrary constants, differentiate the equation $n$ times with respect to $x$, producing $n + 1$ equations in total, and algebraically eliminate $c_1, \dots, c_n$. The resulting differential equation has order $n$.

⚠️

Watch Out for Redundant Constants!

Always simplify constants before counting! For example, $y = c_1 e^{x + c_2}$ looks like it has 2 constants, but $y = (c_1 e^{c_2}) e^x = C e^x$ has only 1 essential constant $C$! Therefore its DE is 1st-order: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = y$ (NOT 2nd-order).

1. Count: 1 arbitrary constant ($a$) $\implies$ differentiate once.

2. Differentiate: $2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 4a \implies 4a = 2y\frac{dy}{dx}$.

3. Eliminate $4a$: Substitute into $y^2 = 4ax$: $y^2 = \left(2y\frac{dy}{dx}\right)x \implies \mathbf{2x\dfrac{dy}{dx} = y}$ (for $y \neq 0$). $\blacksquare$

1. Expand: $x^2 - 2ax + a^2 + y^2 = a^2 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$ (1 constant $a$).

2. Differentiate: $2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 2a \implies 2a = 2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx}$.

3. Eliminate $2a$: Substitute into $x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$: $x^2 + y^2 = \left(2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx}\right)x = 2x^2 + 2xy\frac{dy}{dx}$. Rearranging gives $2xy\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - x^2$, or $\mathbf{(x^2 - y^2)dx + 2xy\,dy = 0}$. $\blacksquare$

1. Count: 2 essential arbitrary constants ($A, B$) $\implies$ differentiate twice.

2. Differentiate Twice: $\frac{dy}{dx} = Ae^x - Be^{-x}$, and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = Ae^x + Be^{-x}$.

3. Eliminate $A, B$: Recognize the right-hand side is $y$ itself! Thus $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = y \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} - y = 0}$. $\blacksquare$

1. Count: 2 essential arbitrary constants ($A, B$) $\implies$ differentiate twice.

2. Differentiate Twice: $\frac{dy}{dx} = -A\sin x + B\cos x$, and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -A\cos x - B\sin x = -(A\cos x + B\sin x)$.

3. Eliminate $A, B$: Since $A\cos x + B\sin x = y$, we get $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -y \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0}$. $\blacksquare$

Section 7.6 ISRT Final 2023 Q1(c) & Q6(b)

Applications of Differential Equations in Statistics & Science

Reading…
1. Population Dynamics

Malthusian: $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = rP$ (unlimited growth).
Verhulst Logistic: $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{K}\right)$ with carrying capacity $K$.

2. Radioactive Decay

Rate of decay is proportional to remaining mass $N(t)$: $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N \implies N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ (carbon dating).

3. Newton's Law of Cooling

Temperature $T(t)$ approaches ambient temperature $T_a$: $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_a)$ ($k > 0$).

4. Continuous Compounding

Account balance $A(t)$ with interest rate $r$: $\dfrac{dA}{dt} = rA \implies A(t) = A_0 e^{rt}$ (financial math).

5. Statistical Distributions (Pearson System & Normal PDF)

In Applied Statistics, the Normal density $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$ satisfies the 1st-order linear ODE $f'(x) = -\dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma^2}f(x)$—a special case of Karl Pearson's differential equation generating the Beta, Gamma, $t$, and Normal families!

ISRT Final 2019 Q5(a) & Final 2024 Q1(b) [3 Marks]

1. Define differential equation. What do you understand by the terms order and degree of a differential equation?

Hint: For full marks on degree, always mention clearing radicals and fractional powers in derivatives and the polynomial requirement.

Differential Equation: An equation involving derivatives (ordinary or partial) of one or more dependent variables with respect to one or more independent variables.

Order: The order of the highest derivative present in the differential equation.

Degree: The power (positive integer exponent) of the highest-order derivative present in the equation after it has been made rational and integral (free from radicals and fractional powers) as far as derivatives are concerned, provided it is a polynomial in all its derivatives.

ISRT Incourse-1 2024 Q1 & Incourse-1 2022 Q2 [4–8 Marks]

2. Classify each equation (ODE/PDE, Order, Degree, Linear/Nonlinear): (i) $\left(\frac{dr}{ds}\right)^3 = \sqrt{\frac{d^2r}{ds^2}+1}$,  (ii) $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y\sin x = 0$,  (iii) $x^2 dy + y^2 dx = 0$,  (iv) $\frac{d^6x}{dt^6} + \left(\frac{d^4x}{dt^4}\right)\left(\frac{d^3x}{dt^3}\right) + x = t$.

Hint: Square (i) first; check which variable is inside $\sin x$ in (ii); divide (iii) by $dx$; spot the derivative product in (iv).

(i) Squaring gives $(dr/ds)^6 = d^2r/ds^2 + 1 \implies$ ODE, Order 2, Degree 1, Nonlinear (due to $(dr/ds)^6$).

(ii) $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + (\sin x)y = 0 \implies$ ODE, Order 2, Degree 1, Linear ($\sin x$ depends on independent variable $x$).

(iii) $x^2 \frac{dy}{dx} + y^2 = 0 \implies$ ODE, Order 1, Degree 1, Nonlinear (due to $y^2$).

(iv) Highest derivative is $d^6x/dt^6$ to power 1 $\implies$ ODE, Order 6, Degree 1, Nonlinear (product of $x^{(4)}$ and $x^{(3)}$).

ISRT Final 2020 Q1(a)(i) & Final 2024 [3 Marks]

3. Form the differential equation of the family of all circles passing through the origin and having their centers on the $x$-axis.

Hint: A circle centered at $(a, 0)$ passing through $(0, 0)$ has radius $|a|$, giving $(x - a)^2 + y^2 = a^2 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$.

Equation of the family: $(x - a)^2 + y^2 = a^2 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$ (1 arbitrary constant $a$).

Differentiating with respect to $x$: $2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 2a$.

Substituting $2a$ back into $x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$: $x^2 + y^2 = \left(2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx}\right)x = 2x^2 + 2xy\frac{dy}{dx}$.

Answer: $2xy\dfrac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - x^2$  or  $(x^2 - y^2)dx + 2xy\,dy = 0$. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2020 Q1(a)(ii), Final 2024 & Incourse-1 2025 Q4 [3 Marks]

4. Form the differential equation of $y = A\cos x + B\sin x$ (and contrast with $y = Ae^x + Be^{-x}$).

Hint: Both have 2 essential constants ($A, B$), so differentiate twice and express $y''$ directly in terms of $y$.

For $y = A\cos x + B\sin x$: $y' = -A\sin x + B\cos x$ and $y'' = -A\cos x - B\sin x = -y \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0}$.

Contrast with $y = Ae^x + Be^{-x}$: $y' = Ae^x - Be^{-x}$ and $y'' = Ae^x + Be^{-x} = +y \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} - y = 0}$. (Only the sign before $y$ flips!) $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2023 (MATH 103) · Q6(a) [4 Marks]

5. Solve the ODE $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 5x - 3$ combined with initial condition $x(2) = 1$, and verify the solution.

Hint: Separate variables $\int \frac{dx}{5x-3} = \int dt$, solve for $x(t)$, plug in $t=2, x=1$ to find the constant, then differentiate to verify.

Separating variables: $\frac{dx}{5x-3} = dt \implies \frac{1}{5}\ln|5x-3| = t + C_1 \implies x(t) = \frac{3}{5} + Ke^{5t}$.

Applying $x(2) = 1$: $1 = \frac{3}{5} + Ke^{10} \implies K = \frac{2}{5}e^{-10}$, giving $x(t) = \dfrac{3}{5} + \dfrac{2}{5}e^{5(t-2)}$.

Verification: $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2e^{5(t-2)} = 5\left(\frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5}e^{5(t-2)}\right) - 3 = 5x - 3$, and $x(2) = \frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{5}e^0 = 1$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2023 (MATH 103) · Q6(b) [4 Marks]

6. A population of rabbits in a forest is modeled by the Verhulst equation $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{K}\right)$ with $r = 0.1$/month, $K = 5000$, and $P(0) = 100$. Find $P(t)$ and the population after $6$ months.

Hint: Use partial fractions $\frac{1}{P(1-P/K)} = \frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{K-P}$ to derive $P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\frac{K-P_0}{P_0}\right)e^{-rt}}$, then evaluate at $t = 6$.

Separating variables and applying partial fractions: $$\left(\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{K-P}\right)dP = r\,dt \implies \ln\left|\frac{P}{K-P}\right| = rt + C \implies \frac{K-P}{P} = Ae^{-rt}$$ With $P(0) = P_0 = 100$ and $K = 5000$, we get $A = \frac{K - P_0}{P_0} = \frac{4900}{100} = 49$.

Thus the population function is: $P(t) = \dfrac{5000}{1 + 49e^{-0.1t}}$.

After $t = 6$ months: $P(6) = \dfrac{5000}{1 + 49e^{-0.6}} = \dfrac{5000}{1 + 49(0.54881)} \approx \mathbf{178.98 \approx 179\text{ rabbits}}$. $\blacksquare$
