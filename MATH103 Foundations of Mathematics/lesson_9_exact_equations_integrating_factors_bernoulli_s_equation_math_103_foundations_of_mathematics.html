Section 9.1

Exact Differential Equations & Necessary/Sufficient Condition

Reading…

Definition 9.1 · Total Differential & Exact DE

For a smooth scalar potential function $u(x, y)$, its total differential is: $$du = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,dy$$ A first-order differential equation $M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$ is called exact if there exists a function $u(x, y)$ such that $M\,dx + N\,dy = du$ identically—that is, $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} = M$ and $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = N$. Its general solution is then simply $u(x, y) = C$.

Theorem 9.1 · Necessary & Sufficient Condition for Exactness

Let $M(x, y)$ and $N(x, y)$ have continuous first partial derivatives on a simply connected domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. Then $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ is exact if and only if:

$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$$
Part 1 · Necessity ($\text{Exact} \implies \partial M/\partial y = \partial N/\partial x$)

Suppose $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ is exact. Then there exists $u(x, y)$ with $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = M$ and $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = N$.

Differentiating the first with respect to $y$ and the second with respect to $x$: $$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y\,\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y}$$ Since $M_y$ and $N_x$ are continuous, Clairaut's (Schwarz's) Theorem guarantees $u_{yx} = u_{xy}$, hence $\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}$.

Part 2 · Sufficiency ($\partial M/\partial y = \partial N/\partial x \implies \text{Exact}$)

Assume $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$. Define a candidate potential by integrating $M$ with respect to $x$ (holding $y$ constant) plus an unknown function $\phi(y)$: $$u(x, y) = \int M(x, y)\,dx + \phi(y)$$ Clearly $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = M(x, y)$. We need $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = N(x, y)$, which requires: $$\phi'(y) = N(x, y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int M(x, y)\,dx$$ To confirm $\phi'(y)$ depends only on $y$ (so we can integrate it to find $\phi(y)$), differentiate the right-hand side with respect to $x$: $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[N - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int M\,dx\right] = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int M\,dx\right) = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 0$$ Since its $x$-derivative is identically $0$, $\phi'(y)$ is independent of $x$! Integrating $\phi'(y)$ yields $\phi(y)$ and constructs $u(x, y)$ with $du = M\,dx + N\,dy$. $\blacksquare$

Fast Exam Working Rule

Once $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$ is verified, write the general solution directly in one line:

$$\int_{\substack{y\text{ held}\\\text{constant}}} M(x, y)\,dx \;+\; \int \big(\text{terms of } N \text{ free from } x\big)\,dy = C$$
⚠️

Exam Trap: Double-Counting Mixed $xy$ Terms!

In $(3x + 2y)\,dx + (2x + y)\,dy = 0$, the integral $\int (3x+2y)\,dx = \frac{3}{2}x^2 + 2xy$ already captures the $2x$ term from $N$! Only integrate the pure-$y$ term $y$ from $N$ ($\int y\,dy = \frac{1}{2}y^2$). Never add $2xy$ twice!

Inverse Exactness & Quick Verification

Reading…

Inverse Exactness Technique (ISRT Final 2025 Q6a)

3 Marks

Given $M(x, y) = x^3 + xy^2$, find the most general $N(x, y)$ making $(x^3 + xy^2)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$ exact:

Require $\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 2xy$. Integrating with respect to $x$ (holding $y$ constant) introduces an arbitrary function of $y$ (not just a constant $C$!): $$N(x, y) = \int 2xy\,dx + g(y) = \mathbf{x^2 y + g(y)}$$ where $g(y)$ is any differentiable function of $y$ alone (e.g., $g(y) = y^3$ recovers ISRT 2022/2024's exact DE!).

Section 9.3

Integrating Factors (IF) & The 5 Standard Rules

Reading…

Definition 9.2 · Integrating Factor $\mu(x, y)$

If $M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$ is not exact ($\partial M/\partial y \neq \partial N/\partial x$), a nonzero function $\mu(x, y)$ is called an Integrating Factor (IF) if multiplying the equation by $\mu$ makes $\mu M\,dx + \mu N\,dy = 0$ exact: $$\frac{\partial(\mu M)}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial(\mu N)}{\partial x}$$

Rule Condition / Structure Integrating Factor $\mu$ ISRT Exam Citation
Rule 1 · Inspection Recognize exact blocks: $d(xy) = ydx+xdy$, $d(y/x) = \frac{xdy-ydx}{x^2}$, $d(x/y) = \frac{ydx-xdy}{y^2}$, $d(\ln(xy))$, $d(\tan^{-1}(y/x)) = \frac{xdy-ydx}{x^2+y^2}$ $\frac{1}{x^2},\; \frac{1}{y^2},\; \frac{1}{xy},\; \frac{1}{x^2+y^2}$ Quick mental shortcut
Rule 2 · Homogeneous $M, N$ homogeneous of same degree and $Mx + Ny \neq 0$ $\mu = \dfrac{1}{Mx + Ny}$ ISRT 1st Incourse 2025 Q2: $(x^2y - 2xy^2)dx - (x^3 - 3x^2y)dy = 0 \implies \mu = \frac{1}{x^2y^2}$
Rule 3 · $f(xy)$ Form $y f_1(xy)\,dx + x f_2(xy)\,dy = 0$ and $Mx - Ny \neq 0$ $\mu = \dfrac{1}{Mx - Ny}$ Abdul Matin Ch. 2
Rule 4 · Function of $x$ $\dfrac{1}{N}\left(\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} - \dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}\right) = f(x)$ (free of $y$) $\mu(x) = e^{\int f(x)\,dx}$ ISRT Final 2024: $(x^2+y^2+2x)dx + 2y\,dy = 0 \implies f(x)=1 \implies \mu = e^x$
Rule 5 · Function of $y$ $\dfrac{1}{M}\left(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right) = g(y)$ (free of $x$) $\mu(y) = e^{\int g(y)\,dy}$ Dual of Rule 4

Bonus Rule · Undetermined Exponent Method ($\mu = x^n$ or $\mu = x^a y^b$)

ISRT Final 2025 Q6(b)

When an exam explicitly asks for an IF of the form $\mu = x^n$ (or $x^a y^b$), multiply the DE by $x^n$, enforce $\frac{\partial(x^n M)}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial(x^n N)}{\partial x}$, and equate coefficients to solve directly for $n$! For $(4x + 3y^2)dx + 2xy\,dy = 0$, this gives $6 x^n y = 2(n+1)x^n y \implies n = 2 \implies \mu = x^2$.

Section 9.4

First-Order Linear Differential Equations (Leibniz Form)

Reading…
Standard Linear Form in $y$
$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)$$

Integrating Factor: $\mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$. Multiplying gives $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[\mu(x)\,y\big] = \mu(x)Q(x)$, so: $$y\cdot \mu(x) = \int Q(x)\,\mu(x)\,dx + C$$

Swapped Linear Form in $x$
$$\frac{dx}{dy} + P(y)x = Q(y)$$

If an equation is nonlinear in $y$ (e.g., contains $y^2$ or $e^y$) but linear in $x$, flip to $\frac{dx}{dy}$, compute $\mu(y) = e^{\int P(y)\,dy}$, and solve: $$x\cdot \mu(y) = \int Q(y)\,\mu(y)\,dy + C$$

⚠️

Two Classic Linear-DE Exam Traps

1. Normalize First: Always divide through so the coefficient of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is strictly $1$ before reading off $P(x)$! For $(1 - x^2)\frac{dy}{dx} + xy = Q$, $P(x) = \frac{x}{1-x^2}$ (NOT $x$).

2. Logarithmic Sign Care: Remember $e^{-\ln|u|} = e^{\ln|u|^{-1}} = \dfrac{1}{|u|}$ (NOT $-u$!), and $e^{-\frac{1}{4}\ln|1-x^2|} = (1 - x^2)^{-1/4}$.

Section 9.5 ★ ISRT Incourse 2024 Q4, 2025 Q3 & Final 2024

Bernoulli's Differential Equation & Trigonometric Reducible Forms

Reading…

Definition 9.3 · Bernoulli's Differential Equation

A nonlinear first-order ODE of the form: $$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,y = Q(x)\,y^n \qquad (n \neq 0,\; n \neq 1)$$ (Note: if $n = 0$ it is already linear; if $n = 1$ it is variable separable!)

Step 1 (Divide by $y^n$): $y^{-n}\dfrac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y^{1-n} = Q(x)$ (noting $y = 0$ is a trivial solution if $n > 0$).

Step 2 (Substitute $v = y^{1-n}$): Differentiate with respect to $x$: $\dfrac{dv}{dx} = (1 - n)y^{-n}\dfrac{dy}{dx} \implies y^{-n}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{1-n}\dfrac{dv}{dx}$.

Step 3 (Linear ODE in $v$): Substitute into Step 1 and multiply through by $(1 - n)$: $$\frac{dv}{dx} + (1 - n)P(x)\,v = (1 - n)Q(x)$$

Step 4 (Solve & Back-Substitute): Solve this standard Leibniz linear DE with $\mu(x) = e^{\int (1-n)P(x)\,dx}$, then replace $v$ with $y^{1-n}$. $\blacksquare$

Generalized / Trigonometric Bernoulli Form (ISRT 2024 Favorite!)

ISRT Incourse 2024 Q4 & Final 2024

Any equation of the form $f'(y)\dfrac{dy}{dx} + P(x)f(y) = Q(x)$ linearizes immediately via $v = f(y) \implies \frac{dv}{dx} + P(x)v = Q(x)$!

Example: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + x\sin(2y) = x^3\cos^2 y$. Dividing by $\cos^2 y$ and using $\sin(2y) = 2\sin y\cos y$ yields: $$\sec^2 y\,\frac{dy}{dx} + 2x\tan y = x^3 \;\xrightarrow{\;v = \tan y\;}\; \frac{dv}{dx} + 2x\,v = x^3$$

ISRT 1st Incourse 2022 Q4(a) & 2024 Q3(a) [4–6 Marks]

1. Show that the differential equation $(x^3 + xy^2)\,dx + (y^3 + x^2y)\,dy = 0$ is exact, and find its general solution.

Hint: Compute $\partial M/\partial y = 2xy$ and $\partial N/\partial x = 2xy$. Remember to drop $x^2y$ from $N$ when applying the working rule!

Here $M = x^3 + xy^2$ and $N = y^3 + x^2 y$. Computing partial derivatives: $$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 2xy, \qquad \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = 2xy$$ Since $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$, the equation is exact.

Applying the working rule (integrating $M$ w.r.t. $x$ and only the $x$-free term $y^3$ of $N$ w.r.t. $y$): $$\int (x^3 + xy^2)\,dx + \int y^3\,dy = C_1 \implies \frac{x^4}{4} + \frac{x^2 y^2}{2} + \frac{y^4}{4} = C_1$$

Multiplying by $4$: $x^4 + 2x^2y^2 + y^4 = C$, i.e., $(x^2 + y^2)^2 = C$. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2025 · Q6(a) [3 Marks]

2. Determine the most general function $N(x, y)$ such that $(x^3 + xy^2)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$ is exact.

Hint: Set $\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 2xy$ and integrate with respect to $x$, remembering to add an arbitrary function $g(y)$!

For exactness, we require $\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^3 + xy^2) = 2xy$.

Integrating partially with respect to $x$ while holding $y$ constant:

$$N(x, y) = \int 2xy\,dx + g(y) = x^2 y + g(y)$$ where $g(y)$ is an arbitrary differentiable function of $y$ alone. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2025 · Q6(b) [5 Marks]

3. Show that $(4x + 3y^2)\,dx + 2xy\,dy = 0$ is not exact, find an integrating factor of the form $x^n$, and solve the equation.

Hint: Multiply by $x^n$: $(4x^{n+1} + 3x^n y^2)dx + 2x^{n+1}y\,dy = 0$. Equate $\partial(\mu M)/\partial y = 6x^n y$ with $\partial(\mu N)/\partial x = 2(n+1)x^n y$.

1. Non-Exactness: $M = 4x + 3y^2 \implies M_y = 6y$, while $N = 2xy \implies N_x = 2y$. Since $6y \neq 2y$, the DE is not exact.

2. Find $n$: Multiply by $\mu = x^n$: $(4x^{n+1} + 3x^n y^2)\,dx + 2x^{n+1}y\,dy = 0$. Enforcing exactness: $$6x^n y = 2(n+1)x^n y \implies 2(n+1) = 6 \implies n = 2 \implies \mu = x^2$$

3. Solve Exact DE: Multiplying by $x^2$ gives $(4x^3 + 3x^2y^2)\,dx + 2x^3y\,dy = 0$ (now $M'_y = 6x^2y = N'_x$). Integrating via the working rule ($N'$ has no $x$-free term): $$\int (4x^3 + 3x^2 y^2)\,dx = C \implies \mathbf{x^4 + x^3 y^2 = C} \quad \blacksquare$$

ISRT 1st Incourse 2025 · Q2 [4 Marks]

4. Define Integrating Factor. Find the integrating factor of the equation $(x^2y - 2xy^2)\,dx - (x^3 - 3x^2y)\,dy = 0$.

Hint: Notice both $M = x^2y - 2xy^2$ and $N = -x^3 + 3x^2y$ are homogeneous of degree $3$! Apply Rule 2: $\mu = \frac{1}{Mx + Ny}$.

Definition: An integrating factor $\mu(x, y) \neq 0$ is a multiplier that converts a non-exact equation $Mdx + Ndy = 0$ into an exact equation $\mu Mdx + \mu Ndy = 0$.

Check: $M = x^2y - 2xy^2$ and $N = -x^3 + 3x^2y$ are both homogeneous of degree $3$, with $M_y = x^2 - 4xy \neq -3x^2 + 6xy = N_x$ (not exact).

Rule 2 ($Mx + Ny$): $Mx + Ny = (x^3y - 2x^2y^2) + (-x^3y + 3x^2y^2) = x^2y^2 \neq 0$.

Integrating Factor: $\mu(x, y) = \dfrac{1}{Mx + Ny} = \dfrac{1}{x^2 y^2}$. (Bonus solution after multiplying: $\frac{x}{y} - 2\ln|x| + 3\ln|y| = C$.) $\blacksquare$

ISRT 1st Incourse 2025 · Q3 [5 Marks]

5. Define Bernoulli's equation and solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + \dfrac{x}{1 - x^2}\,y = x\sqrt{y}$.

Hint: Here $n = 1/2$, so set $v = y^{1/2} = \sqrt{y}$. This gives $\frac{dv}{dx} + \frac{x}{2(1-x^2)}v = \frac{x}{2}$ with $\mu(x) = (1-x^2)^{-1/4}$.

Step 1 (Divide by $\sqrt{y}$): $y^{-1/2}\dfrac{dy}{dx} + \dfrac{x}{1-x^2}y^{1/2} = x$.

Step 2 (Substitute $v = y^{1/2}$): $\dfrac{dv}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{2}y^{-1/2}\dfrac{dy}{dx} \implies \dfrac{dv}{dx} + \dfrac{x}{2(1-x^2)}v = \dfrac{x}{2}$.

Step 3 (Linear IF): $\int \dfrac{x}{2(1-x^2)}\,dx = -\dfrac{1}{4}\ln|1 - x^2| \implies \mu(x) = (1 - x^2)^{-1/4}$.

Step 4 (Integrate): $v(1-x^2)^{-1/4} = \int \dfrac{x}{2}(1-x^2)^{-1/4}\,dx$. Put $u = 1-x^2 \implies \frac{x}{2}dx = -\frac{du}{4}$: $$v(1-x^2)^{-1/4} = -\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{4}{3}u^{3/4} + C = -\frac{1}{3}(1-x^2)^{3/4} + C$$

Back-substituting $v = \sqrt{y}$: $\sqrt{y} = -\dfrac{1}{3}(1 - x^2) + C(1 - x^2)^{1/4}$. $\blacksquare$

ISRT 1st Incourse 2024 Q4 & Final 2024 [4 Marks]

6. Solve the differential equation $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + x\sin(2y) = x^3\cos^2 y$.

Hint: Divide by $\cos^2 y$, use $\sin(2y) = 2\sin y\cos y$, set $v = \tan y$ to get $\frac{dv}{dx} + 2xv = x^3$, and multiply by $\mu(x) = e^{x^2}$.

Step 1 (Divide by $\cos^2 y$): $\sec^2 y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx} + 2x\tan y = x^3$.

Step 2 (Substitute $v = \tan y$): $\dfrac{dv}{dx} = \sec^2 y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx} \implies \dfrac{dv}{dx} + 2x\,v = x^3$ (Linear ODE).

Step 3 (IF & Integrate): $\mu(x) = e^{\int 2x\,dx} = e^{x^2}$. Thus $v e^{x^2} = \int x^3 e^{x^2}\,dx$. Put $u = x^2 \implies x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}du$: $$v e^{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}\int u e^u\,du = \frac{1}{2}(u - 1)e^u + C = \frac{1}{2}(x^2 - 1)e^{x^2} + C$$

Back-substituting $v = \tan y$: $\tan y = \dfrac{1}{2}(x^2 - 1) + Ce^{-x^2}$. $\blacksquare$
