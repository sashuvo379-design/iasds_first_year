Section 13.1

Graph Fundamentals, Degrees & Graph Types

Reading…

Definition 13.1 · Graph, Adjacency, Incidence & Degree

A graph $G = (V, E)$ consists of a finite nonempty set of vertices (nodes) $V$ with $|V| = n$ and a set of edges $E$ with $|E| = m$, where each edge connects one or two vertices (its endpoints).

  • Adjacency & Incidence: Two vertices $u, v$ joined by an edge $e = \{u, v\}$ are adjacent (neighbors), and $e$ is incident to both $u$ and $v$.
  • Degree $\deg(v)$: In an undirected graph, $\deg(v)$ is the number of edges incident to $v$ (each self-loop contributes $2$).
  • Directed Degrees: In a digraph, $\deg^-(v)$ (in-degree) counts incoming arcs and $\deg^+(v)$ (out-degree) counts outgoing arcs, with $\sum \deg^-(v) = \sum \deg^+(v) = |E|$.
Theorem 13.1 · Handshaking Lemma & Parity Corollary

$\displaystyle\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|$, and every undirected graph has an even number of odd-degree vertices.

Show Proof Hide Proof

Proof of Handshaking Lemma: Every edge $e = \{u, v\} \in E$ has two endpoints and therefore contributes $+1$ to $\deg(u)$ and $+1$ to $\deg(v)$ (or $+2$ to $\deg(u)$ if $e$ is a self-loop). Summing degrees over all $v \in V$ counts each edge exactly twice, giving $\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|$.

Proof of Corollary: Partition $V$ into even-degree vertices $V_{\text{even}}$ and odd-degree vertices $V_{\text{odd}}$: $$\sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v) + \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2|E|$$ Since $2|E|$ is even and every term in the first sum is even, $\sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v)$ must also be even. A sum of odd integers is even if and only if there are an even number of terms. Thus $|V_{\text{odd}}|$ is even! $\blacksquare$

Adjacency Matrix $A_G$ & Bóna Theorem 10.16

For a graph on vertices $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$, the $n \times n$ adjacency matrix $A_G$ has entry $(A_G)_{ij}$ equal to the number of edges joining $v_i$ and $v_j$ ($0$ or $1$ for simple graphs; symmetric for undirected graphs).
Bóna Theorem 10.16: For every positive integer $k$, the $(i, j)$-th entry of the matrix power $(A_G)^k$ equals the exact number of walks of length $k$ from $v_i$ to $v_j$.

Trees, Forests & Spanning Trees

A tree $T$ is a connected, acyclic undirected graph (a disjoint union of trees is a forest). For any graph $T$ with $n$ vertices, the following 4 statements are logically equivalent:

1. Edge Count: $T$ is connected and has exactly $|E| = n - 1$ edges.
2. Unique Paths: Every pair of distinct vertices is joined by a unique path.
3. Minimally Connected: Removing any single edge disconnects $T$.
4. Maximally Acyclic: Adding any new edge creates exactly one cycle.

Spanning Tree: A subgraph $T = (V, E')$ of a connected graph $G = (V, E)$ that includes all $n$ vertices of $G$ and is a tree (hence has $n - 1$ edges).

Section 13.3

Eulerian Paths & Circuits (Edge-Covering Traversals)

Reading…

An Eulerian trail (Euler path) visits every edge of $G$ exactly once. An Eulerian circuit is a closed Eulerian trail (starts and ends at the same vertex).

0 Odd-Degree Vertices

Eulerian Circuit EXISTS ✓

Every vertex has even degree (in a connected graph). Can start/end at any vertex.

Exactly 2 Odd Vertices

Eulerian Path ONLY (No Circuit)

Must start at one odd-degree vertex and finish at the other odd-degree vertex.

> 2 Odd Vertices (4, 6, …)

NEITHER Exists ✕

No Eulerian path and no Eulerian circuit (e.g., Königsberg bridges with 4 odd vertices).

Directed Eulerian Circuit (Bóna Theorem 9.6)

A weakly connected directed graph has a directed Eulerian circuit if and only if it is balanced: $\deg^+(v) = \deg^-(v)$ for every vertex $v \in V$ (which also implies strong connectivity).

Section 13.4

Hamiltonian Paths, Circuits, Dirac's & Ore's Theorems

Reading…

A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex of $G$ exactly once. A Hamiltonian circuit (cycle) is a closed cycle visiting every vertex once and returning to the start.

Theorem 13.2 · Dirac's Theorem (1952)

Let $G$ be a simple graph with $n \ge 3$ vertices. If every vertex satisfies: $$\deg(v) \ge \frac{n}{2} \qquad (\forall v \in V)$$ then $G$ has a Hamiltonian circuit.

Theorem 13.3 · Ore's Theorem (1960)

Let $G$ be a simple graph with $n \ge 3$ vertices. If for every pair of non-adjacent vertices $u, v$: $$\deg(u) + \deg(v) \ge n$$ then $G$ has a Hamiltonian circuit. (Strictly stronger than Dirac!)

🚨

CRITICAL ISRT EXAM TRAP: Dirac & Ore Are SUFFICIENT, NOT NECESSARY!

If a graph fails Dirac's or Ore's condition, you CANNOT conclude that it has no Hamiltonian circuit! Example: the 5-cycle $C_5$ has $n = 5$ and $\deg(v) = 2 < 2.5$ (fails Dirac) and non-adjacent sum $2 + 2 = 4 < 5$ (fails Ore)—yet $C_5$ itself is a Hamiltonian circuit!

How to DISPROVE a Hamiltonian circuit (Necessary Conditions): A graph cannot have a Hamiltonian circuit if it is (1) disconnected, (2) has any vertex of degree $\le 1$, (3) is a tree with $n \ge 3$, or (4) has a cut-vertex (whose removal disconnects the graph).

Greedy Algorithms — Principles, Pros & Pitfalls

Reading…

A greedy algorithm constructs a solution step by step, always selecting the option that looks locally optimal at the current step, in the hope that a sequence of locally best choices leads to a globally optimal solution.

1. Locally Optimal

Picks the immediate best choice at each decision step.

2. No Backtracking

Once a choice is made, it is irrevocable—never revised later.

3. Top-Down

Reduces the problem iteratively into one smaller subproblem.

4. Fast & Simple

Easy to design and runs in low polynomial time.

✓ Where Greedy Succeeds (Optimal)
  • Kruskal's & Prim's Algorithms (Minimum Spanning Tree)
  • Dijkstra's Algorithm (Shortest path with $w(e) \ge 0$)
  • Canonical Coin Change (e.g., $\{1, 5, 10, 25\}$ cents)
  • Activity Selection & Huffman Coding
✕ Main Disadvantage: Does NOT Always Give Global Optimum

Coin Change Counterexample (ISRT 2025 Q7): Coins $\{1, 3, 4\}$, target $6$.
• Greedy: Picks $4$, then $1$, then $1$ ($4 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{3\text{ coins}}$).
• True Optimal: $3 + 3 = \mathbf{2\text{ coins}}$!

Also fails in Dijkstra's algorithm when edges have negative weights.

Dijkstra's Shortest-Path Algorithm

Reading…

Given a weighted graph $G = (V, E, w)$ with non-negative edge weights $w(e) \ge 0$ and a source vertex $s \in V$, Dijkstra's greedy algorithm finds the shortest path distance $d(v)$ from $s$ to every reachable vertex $v$ using a settled set $S$ and predecessor array $\text{prev}(v)$.

Dijkstra's 4-Step Algorithm
  1. Initialize: Set $d(s) = 0$, $d(v) = \infty$ for all $v \neq s$, $\text{prev}(v) = \text{null}$, and settled set $S = \emptyset$.
  2. Greedy Selection: Choose an unsettled vertex $u \notin S$ with the minimum tentative distance $d(u)$, and add $u$ to $S$ (its distance is now finalized).
  3. Edge Relaxation: For every unsettled neighbor $v \notin S$ of $u$, test whether going through $u$ improves $d(v)$: $$d(v) \leftarrow \min\!\big(d(v),\; d(u) + w(u, v)\big)$$ If $d(u) + w(u, v) < d(v)$, update $d(v)$ and set $\text{prev}(v) \leftarrow u$.
  4. Terminate & Reconstruct (Bóna Theorem 17.16): Repeat Steps 2–3 until $S = V$ (or target $t \in S$). Trace $\text{prev}(t)$ backward to $s$ to recover the exact shortest path.
Why Negative Weights Break Dijkstra: Once Dijkstra moves $u$ into $S$, it assumes no future path can lower $d(u)$ (valid when all remaining edges add $\ge 0$). A negative-weight edge discovered later can create a shorter path into an already-settled vertex, which Dijkstra ignores! (See Lab 2 Preset 3 below.)

ISRT Final 2024 Q1(3) & Final 2025 Q1(5) [3 Marks]

1. Define walk, trail, path, and tree with examples; clearly differentiate between a path and a trail.

Hint: Focus on what can repeat: Walk (edges & vertices may repeat) → Trail (no repeated edges, vertices may repeat) → Path (neither edges nor vertices repeat). Tree = connected + acyclic.

Walk: Any alternating sequence of adjacent vertices and edges (both may repeat), e.g., $A \to B \to C \to B \to D$.

Trail: A walk with no repeated edges (vertices may repeat), e.g., $A \to B \to C \to D \to B \to E$.

Path: A trail with no repeated vertices (and hence no repeated edges), e.g., $A \to B \to C \to D$.

Key Difference: Every path is a trail, but a trail is NOT necessarily a path because a trail may revisit a vertex via distinct edges.

Tree: A connected undirected graph containing no cycles (with $n - 1$ edges), e.g., a 4-vertex star $K_{1,3}$ or line $A-B-C-D$. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2024 Q7(a) & Final 2025 Q7(3) [3 Marks]

2. Differentiate between a Hamiltonian path and an Eulerian path with suitable examples.

Hint: Contrast Edge-coverage vs. Vertex-coverage, repetition rules, existence criteria (odd degrees vs. no simple iff test), and give concrete examples.

1. Coverage: An Eulerian path traverses every edge exactly once (vertices may repeat); a Hamiltonian path visits every vertex exactly once (edges may be skipped).

2. Existence Criterion: A connected graph has an Eulerian path iff it has $0$ or $2$ vertices of odd degree; no simple necessary-and-sufficient condition is known for Hamiltonian paths.

3. Examples: In the House graph ($AB, BC, CD, DA, CE, DE$), $C \to D \to E \to C \to B \to A \to D$ is an Eulerian path (revisits $C, D$), while $A \to B \to C \to E \to D$ is a Hamiltonian path (skips edge $CD$). $\blacksquare$

ISRT Final 2024 · Q7(b) [4 Marks]

3. State Dirac's and Ore's theorems. Test the 5-vertex cycle $C_5$ against both theorems and explain whether you can decide if it has a Hamiltonian circuit.

Hint: In $C_5$, $n = 5$ and every vertex has degree $2$. Both theorems fail—emphasize that they are sufficient, not necessary, and directly exhibit the cycle $A \to B \to C \to D \to E \to A$!

Dirac's Theorem: Simple graph with $n \ge 3$ and $\deg(v) \ge n/2$ for all $v \implies$ Hamiltonian circuit.

Ore's Theorem: Simple graph with $n \ge 3$ and $\deg(u) + \deg(v) \ge n$ for all non-adjacent $u, v \implies$ Hamiltonian circuit.

Test on $C_5$: Here $n = 5$ and $\deg(v) = 2$ for every vertex. Since $2 < 5/2 = 2.5$, Dirac fails. For any non-adjacent pair, $2 + 2 = 4 < 5$, so Ore fails.

Conclusion: Because Dirac's and Ore's conditions are sufficient, not necessary, their failure is inconclusive. By direct inspection, $A \to B \to C \to D \to E \to A$ IS a Hamiltonian circuit! $\blacksquare$

ISRT 2nd Incourse 2025 · Q6 [4 Marks]

4. Consider the 5-vertex House Graph on $\{A, B, C, D, E\}$ with edges $\{AB, BC, CD, DA, CE, DE\}$. Find the degree of each vertex, verify the Handshaking Lemma, and justify whether it has an Euler path or Euler circuit.

Hint: Count incident edges for each vertex; sum them to compare with $2|E| = 12$; count how many vertices have odd degree.

Degrees: $\deg(A) = 2,\; \deg(B) = 2,\; \deg(C) = 3,\; \deg(D) = 3,\; \deg(E) = 2$.

Handshaking Lemma: $\sum \deg(v) = 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 12 = 2 \times 6 = 2|E|$ ✓

Euler Verdict: The graph is connected and has exactly 2 vertices of odd degree ($C$ and $D$). Therefore it has an Euler path (e.g., $C \to D \to E \to C \to B \to A \to D$) starting at $C$ and ending at $D$, but no Euler circuit. $\blacksquare$

ISRT 2nd Incourse 2025 Q7 & Final 2024 Q7(3) [3 Marks]

6. Define a greedy algorithm with an example and explain one disadvantage using a suitable counterexample.

Hint: Define locally optimal step-by-step selection without backtracking; cite Kruskal's MST or Dijkstra ($w \ge 0$) as an example; give the $\{1, 3, 4\}$ coin-change counterexample for target $6$.

Definition: A greedy algorithm solves an optimization problem by making the locally best (most immediate) choice at each step without reconsidering past choices.

Example: Kruskal's Algorithm builds a Minimum Spanning Tree by repeatedly adding the smallest-weight edge that does not create a cycle.

Disadvantage & Counterexample: Greedy algorithms are not guaranteed to find the global optimum. For making change of $6$ units using coins $\{1, 3, 4\}$, the greedy strategy picks $4 + 1 + 1$ (3 coins), whereas the true optimal solution is $3 + 3$ (2 coins). $\blacksquare$
