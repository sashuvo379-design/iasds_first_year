Section 01

Congruence Modulo $m$ & Core Properties

Definition 1.1 · Congruence Modulo $m$

Let $m$ be a fixed positive integer (called the modulus). Two integers $a$ and $b$ are said to be congruent modulo $m$, written $a \equiv b \pmod m$, if and only if $m$ divides their difference $a - b$: $$a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b) \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } a - b = km$$ Equivalently, $a$ and $b$ leave the exact same remainder when divided by $m$.

Class Note Example (Prottasha): $-1 \equiv 7 \pmod 8$ because $7 - (-1) = 8$, which is divisible by $8$ (equivalently $-1 - 7 = -8 = 8(-1)$).

REFLEXIVE

$a \equiv a \pmod m$

Since $a - a = 0$ and $m \mid 0$.

SYMMETRIC

$a \equiv b \implies b \equiv a \pmod m$

Since $m \mid (a - b) \implies m \mid -(a - b)$.

TRANSITIVE

$a \equiv b,\; b \equiv c \implies a \equiv c \pmod m$

Adding $(a-b)+(b-c)=a-c$ preserves divisibility by $m$.

Since $a \equiv b \pmod m$ and $c \equiv d \pmod m$, there exist integers $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $a - b = k_1 m$ (so $a = b + k_1 m$) and $c - d = k_2 m$ (so $c = d + k_2 m$).

(a) Addition Rule:

$(a + c) - (b + d) = (a - b) + (c - d) = k_1 m + k_2 m = (k_1 + k_2)m$.

Because $k_1 + k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mid [(a+c)-(b+d)]$, which means $a + c \equiv b + d \pmod m$. $\blacksquare$

(b) Multiplication Rule:

$ac - bd = (b + k_1 m)(d + k_2 m) - bd = bd + b k_2 m + d k_1 m + k_1 k_2 m^2 - bd = m(b k_2 + d k_1 + k_1 k_2 m)$.

Since $b k_2 + d k_1 + k_1 k_2 m \in \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mid (ac - bd)$, hence $ac \equiv bd \pmod m$. (By induction, $a^n \equiv b^n \pmod m$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.) $\blacksquare$

Divisor Property (Factoring the Modulus)

If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $n = pq$, then $p \mid n \mid (a-b)$ and $q \mid n \mid (a-b)$, so: $$a \equiv b \pmod p \quad \text{and} \quad a \equiv b \pmod q$$ Converse (Coprime Glueing): If $a \equiv b \pmod p$, $a \equiv b \pmod q$, and $\gcd(p, q) = 1$, then $a \equiv b \pmod{pq}$. (Crucial for Wilson's Theorem problems!)

⚠️

Exam Trap: The Cancellation Law

You cannot blindly divide both sides of a congruence by $c$! The exact rule is: $$ac \equiv bc \pmod m \iff a \equiv b \left(\bmod\; \frac{m}{\gcd(c, m)}\right)$$ Cancellation preserves the modulus $m$ only when $\gcd(c, m) = 1$.

Trap: 6 ≡ 2 (mod 4) ⇒ dividing by 2 gives 3 ≡ 1 (mod 2), NOT mod 4!
Safe: 15 ≡ 3 (mod 4), gcd(3,4)=1 ⇒ 5 ≡ 1 (mod 4) ✓

Section 02

Complete & Reduced Residue Systems (CRS & RRS)

Complete Residue System (CRS) mod $m$

A set of $m$ integers $\{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m\}$ such that every integer is congruent modulo $m$ to exactly one element of the set (i.e., $m$ pairwise incongruent integers covering all remainders $0, 1, \dots, m-1$).

Example mod 6: {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5} or {0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25}.

Reduced Residue System (RRS) mod $m$

A set of $\phi(m)$ pairwise incongruent integers $\{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_{\phi(m)}\}$ such that every element is relatively prime to $m$, i.e., $\gcd(r_i, m) = 1$ for all $i$.

Example mod 8: {1, 3, 5, 7} or {11, 33, 55, 77} (since φ(8) = 4).

Quick Exam Checklist: Testing a Set $S$ Modulo $m$

Step 1 · Count

Does $|S| = m$ (for CRS) or $|S| = \phi(m)$ (for RRS)?

Step 2 · Reduce

Compute $x \bmod m \in \{0, \dots, m-1\}$ for each $x \in S$. All remainders must be distinct.

Step 3 · Coprime (RRS)

For RRS only: verify $\gcd(x, m) = 1$ for every $x \in S$.

1. Size Check: $R'$ contains $\phi(m)$ elements (corresponding to the $\phi(m)$ elements of $R$).

2. Coprimality Check: For each $r_i \in R$, we have $\gcd(r_i, m) = 1$. Since $\gcd(a, m) = 1$ as well, no prime factor of $m$ divides $a$ or $r_i$. Thus $\gcd(ar_i, m) = 1$ for every $i$.

3. Pairwise Incongruence Check: Suppose $ar_i \equiv ar_j \pmod m$ for some $i, j$. Because $\gcd(a, m) = 1$, the Cancellation Law allows us to divide both sides by $a$, giving $r_i \equiv r_j \pmod m$. Since $R$ is an RRS, its elements are pairwise incongruent, so $i = j$. Thus all elements of $R'$ are pairwise incongruent modulo $m$.

Therefore, $R'$ is a Reduced Residue System modulo $m$. $\blacksquare$

Section 03

Linear Diophantine Equations ($ax + by = c$)

Theorem 3.1 · Existence & General Solution

Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $a, b$ not both zero, and let $d = \gcd(a, b)$. The linear Diophantine equation $ax + by = c$ has integer solutions $(x, y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ if and only if $d \mid c$.

If $(x_0, y_0)$ is any one particular integer solution, then all integer solutions are parameterized by $t \in \mathbb{Z}$: $$x = x_0 + \left(\frac{b}{d}\right)t, \qquad y = y_0 - \left(\frac{a}{d}\right)t \qquad (t \in \mathbb{Z})$$

4-Step Exam Recipe: Finding All Positive Integral Solutions ($x > 0,\; y > 0$)

ISRT Final 2019, 2024, 2025
STEP 1 · GCD CHECK

Find $d = \gcd(a, b)$ via Euclidean Algorithm. Verify $d \mid c$ (otherwise stop: no solution).

STEP 2 · PARTICULAR SOL

Back-substitute to get $au + bv = d$. Multiply by $c/d$ to obtain $x_0 = u(c/d)$ and $y_0 = v(c/d)$.

STEP 3 · GENERAL FORM

Write $x = x_0 + \frac{b}{d}t$ and $y = y_0 - \frac{a}{d}t$ for arbitrary $t \in \mathbb{Z}$.

STEP 4 · BOUND $t$

Solve $x_0 + \frac{b}{d}t > 0$ and $y_0 - \frac{a}{d}t > 0$ to trap $t$ in a finite integer range, then plug each valid $t$ back in.

Section 04

Linear Congruences ($ax \equiv b \pmod m$)

By definition of congruence, $ax \equiv b \pmod m$ means $m \mid (ax - b)$, which is completely equivalent to the linear Diophantine equation: $$ax - my = b \quad (y \in \mathbb{Z})$$

Theorem 4.1 · Solution Count Theorem

Let $d = \gcd(a, m)$. The linear congruence $ax \equiv b \pmod m$ has a solution if and only if $d \mid b$. When $d \mid b$, there are exactly $d$ mutually incongruent solutions modulo $m$, given by: $$x_k \equiv x_0 + k \cdot \frac{m}{d} \pmod m, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, d - 1$$ where $x_0$ is any particular solution of the reduced congruence $\frac{a}{d}x \equiv \frac{b}{d} \pmod{\frac{m}{d}}$.

Method 1 · Universal

Extended Euclidean Algorithm

Works for all solvable congruences. Divide $a, b, m$ by $d = \gcd(a, m)$, find the modular inverse of $a/d \pmod{m/d}$ via Bézout's identity, multiply by $b/d$, and generate the $d$ solutions spaced by $m/d$.

Method 2 · ISRT 2020 & 2023 Favorite

Euler's Theorem Method

When $\gcd(a, m) = 1$, Euler's Theorem guarantees $a^{\phi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod m$, so $a^{-1} \equiv a^{\phi(m)-1} \pmod m$. Multiplying both sides gives the unique solution directly: $$x \equiv b \cdot a^{\phi(m)-1} \pmod m$$

Section 05

Euler's Totient Function $\phi(n)$

Definition 5.1 · Euler's $\phi$-Function

For any positive integer $n$, Euler's totient function $\phi(n)$ counts the number of positive integers less than or equal to $n$ that are relatively prime to $n$: $$\phi(n) = \big|\{k \in \mathbb{Z}^+ : 1 \le k \le n \text{ and } \gcd(k, n) = 1\}\big|$$

BASE VALUE
$\phi(1) = 1$

Since $\gcd(1, 1) = 1$.

PRIME $p$
$\phi(p) = p - 1$

Every $1, \dots, p-1$ is coprime to $p$.

PRIME POWER $p^\alpha$
$\phi(p^\alpha) = p^\alpha - p^{\alpha-1}$

Exclude the $p^{\alpha-1}$ multiples of $p$.

MULTIPLICATIVITY
$\phi(rs) = \phi(r)\phi(s)$

Valid whenever $\gcd(r, s) = 1$.

Step 1 (Prime Power): Among $\{1, 2, \dots, p^\alpha\}$, an integer is not coprime to $p^\alpha$ iff it is a multiple of $p$: namely $p, 2p, 3p, \dots, p^{\alpha-1}\cdot p$. There are $p^{\alpha-1}$ such multiples, so:

$$\phi(p^\alpha) = p^\alpha - p^{\alpha-1} = p^\alpha\left(1 - \frac{1}{p}\right)$$

Step 2 (Apply Multiplicativity): Since the distinct prime powers $p_1^{\alpha_1}, p_2^{\alpha_2}, \dots, p_k^{\alpha_k}$ are pairwise relatively prime, repeatedly applying $\phi(rs) = \phi(r)\phi(s)$ yields:

$$\phi(n) = \prod_{i=1}^k \phi(p_i^{\alpha_i}) = \prod_{i=1}^k (p_i^{\alpha_i} - p_i^{\alpha_i-1}) = \prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{\alpha_i}\left(1 - \frac{1}{p_i}\right) = n \prod_{i=1}^k \left(1 - \frac{1}{p_i}\right) \quad \blacksquare$$

Let $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{\phi(n)}$ be all positive integers less than $n$ that are relatively prime to $n$, and let $S = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{\phi(n)}$.

Key Observation: $\gcd(a_i, n) = 1 \iff \gcd(n - a_i, n) = 1$. Therefore, as $a_i$ runs over all coprime integers in $[1, n-1]$, the complementary numbers $n - a_i$ run over the exact same set in reverse order!

Writing $S$ forward and backward and adding term-by-term:

$$2S = \sum_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} a_i + \sum_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} (n - a_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} n = n \cdot \phi(n) \implies S = \frac{1}{2}n\phi(n) \quad \blacksquare$$
Section 06

Classical Theorems: Euler's, Fermat's Little & Wilson's Theorems

Theorem 6.1

Euler's Theorem

If $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, then: $$a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$$

Class Note: $a=15, n=4 \implies \phi(4)=2$ and $15^2 = 225 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

Theorem 6.2

Fermat's Little Theorem

Special case of Euler's theorem when $n = p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$ (so $\phi(p) = p - 1$): $$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$$

Equivalently, $a^p \equiv a \pmod p$ for every integer $a$.

Theorem 6.3

Wilson's Theorem

If $p$ is a prime number, then $(p - 1)! + 1$ is divisible by $p$: $$(p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$$

Equivalently, $(p-1)! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p$.

Let $R = \{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_{\phi(n)}\}$ be a Reduced Residue System modulo $n$.

Since $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, by Theorem 2.1 the scaled set $R' = \{ar_1, ar_2, \dots, ar_{\phi(n)}\}$ is also an RRS modulo $n$. Therefore, the elements of $R'$ are congruent modulo $n$ to the elements of $R$ in some rearranged order.

Multiplying all $\phi(n)$ congruences together:

$$(ar_1)(ar_2)\cdots(ar_{\phi(n)}) \equiv r_1 r_2 \cdots r_{\phi(n)} \pmod n \implies a^{\phi(n)} \prod_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} r_i \equiv \prod_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} r_i \pmod n$$

Since $\gcd(r_i, n) = 1$ for every $i$, their product $P = \prod r_i$ also satisfies $\gcd(P, n) = 1$. Canceling $P$ from both sides yields $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$. $\blacksquare$

DU / ISRT Exam Notation

The $\lfloor n$ Factorial Symbol

In ISRT exam papers (e.g., 2019 & 2025 Finals) and Prottasha's notes, factorials are often printed using the traditional British notation $\lfloor 18$ or $|18$ to mean $18!$. So $\lfloor 18 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{437}$ simply means $18! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{437}$.

Composite Modulus Splitting Trick

Wilson's theorem only applies to primes! When asked to prove divisibility by a composite modulus like $437 = 19 \times 23$ or $899 = 29 \times 31$:

  1. Factor the modulus into coprime primes $p \cdot q$.
  2. Prove congruence $\equiv 0 \pmod p$ and $\equiv 0 \pmod q$ separately using Wilson's theorem.
  3. Combine via $\gcd(p, q) = 1 \implies pq$ divides the expression!

ISRT Final 2019, 2024, 2025 & 1st Incourse 2020 [4 Marks]

1. Find all positive integral solutions of the linear Diophantine equation $62x + 11y = 788$.

Hint: Use the Euclidean Algorithm to show $62(-3) + 11(17) = 1$, scale by $788$, then solve $x = -2364 + 11t > 0$ and $y = 13396 - 62t > 0$.

Step 1 (Euclidean Algorithm): $62 = 11(5) + 7$, $11 = 7(1) + 4$, $7 = 4(1) + 3$, $4 = 3(1) + 1$. Thus $\gcd(62, 11) = 1 \mid 788$, so solutions exist.

Step 2 (Back-substitution): $1 = 4 - 3 = 4 - (7 - 4) = 2(4) - 7 = 2(11 - 7) - 7 = 2(11) - 3(7) = 2(11) - 3(62 - 5\cdot 11) = 62(-3) + 11(17)$.

Step 3 (Particular & General Solution): Multiplying by $788$: $x_0 = -3(788) = -2364$, $y_0 = 17(788) = 13396$. General solution: $x = -2364 + 11t$, $y = 13396 - 62t$ ($t \in \mathbb{Z}$).

Step 4 (Positive Integral Condition): $x > 0 \implies 11t > 2364 \implies t \ge 215$. And $y > 0 \implies 62t < 13396 \implies t \le 216$. Thus $t \in \{215, 216\}$:

• $t = 215 \implies (x, y) = (1, 66)$  |  • $t = 216 \implies (x, y) = (12, 4)$.

ISRT Year Final 2019 & 2025 [5–6 Marks]

2. State Wilson's theorem. Prove that $18! + 1$ is divisible by $437$, that is, $18! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{437}$.

Hint: Factor $437 = 19 \times 23$. Apply Wilson's theorem directly for $p = 19$, and rewrite $22! = 22 \cdot 21 \cdot 20 \cdot 19 \cdot 18!$ modulo $23$.

Wilson's Theorem: If $p$ is a prime number, then $(p - 1)! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p$.

Note that $437 = 19 \times 23$, where $19$ and $23$ are distinct primes with $\gcd(19, 23) = 1$.

Modulo $19$: By Wilson's theorem for $p = 19$, $(19-1)! + 1 = 18! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{19}$.

Modulo $23$: By Wilson's theorem for $p = 23$, $22! \equiv -1 \pmod{23}$. Expanding $22!$:

$22! = 22 \cdot 21 \cdot 20 \cdot 19 \cdot 18! \equiv (-1)(-2)(-3)(-4) \cdot 18! = 24 \cdot 18! \equiv 1 \cdot 18! \pmod{23}$

Hence $18! \equiv -1 \pmod{23} \implies 18! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{23}$.

Since $19 \mid (18!+1)$, $23 \mid (18!+1)$, and $\gcd(19,23)=1$, we conclude $437 \mid (18!+1)$, i.e., $18! + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{437}$. $\blacksquare$

Class Notes (Prottasha) · Number Theory [Classic Proof]

3. Prove that $28! + 233$ is divisible by $899$.

Hint: Factor $899 = 29 \times 31$. Find $233 \bmod 29$ and $233 \bmod 31$, then use Wilson's theorem for $p = 29$ and $p = 31$.

Notice $899 = 29 \times 31$, where $29$ and $31$ are distinct primes ($\gcd(29, 31) = 1$).

Modulo $29$: By Wilson's theorem, $28! \equiv -1 \pmod{29}$. Also $233 = 8 \times 29 + 1 \implies 233 \equiv 1 \pmod{29}$. Adding gives $28! + 233 \equiv -1 + 1 = 0 \pmod{29}$.

Modulo $31$: By Wilson's theorem, $30! \equiv -1 \pmod{31}$. Since $30! = 30 \cdot 29 \cdot 28! \equiv (-1)(-2)\cdot 28! = 2\cdot 28! \pmod{31}$, we have $2 \cdot 28! \equiv -1 \equiv 30 \pmod{31}$. Since $\gcd(2, 31) = 1$, dividing by $2$ gives $28! \equiv 15 \pmod{31}$. Also $233 = 7 \times 31 + 16 \implies 233 \equiv 16 \pmod{31}$. Adding gives $28! + 233 \equiv 15 + 16 = 31 \equiv 0 \pmod{31}$.

Since both $29$ and $31$ divide $28! + 233$ and $\gcd(29, 31) = 1$, $899 \mid (28! + 233)$. $\blacksquare$

ISRT Year Final 2020 & 2023 [3–6 Marks]

4. Find the solution of the linear congruence $15x \equiv 32 \pmod{91}$ using Euler's theorem.

Hint: $91 = 7 \times 13 \implies \phi(91) = 6 \times 12 = 72$. Thus $x \equiv 32 \cdot 15^{71} \pmod{91}$. Tip: compute $15^2, 15^4, 15^8$ and notice $15^{12} \equiv 1 \pmod{91}$!

Since $91 = 7 \times 13$ and $15 = 3 \times 5$, we have $\gcd(15, 91) = 1$ (unique solution mod $91$).

Compute $\phi(91) = \phi(7)\phi(13) = (7-1)(13-1) = 6 \times 12 = 72$.

By Euler's theorem, $15^{72} \equiv 1 \pmod{91}$. Multiplying $15x \equiv 32 \pmod{91}$ by $15^{71}$ gives $x \equiv 32 \cdot 15^{71} \pmod{91}$.

Using repeated squaring mod $91$: $15^2 = 225 \equiv 43$, $15^4 \equiv 43^2 = 1849 \equiv 29$, $15^8 \equiv 29^2 = 841 \equiv 22$, so $15^{12} = 15^8 \cdot 15^4 \equiv 22 \cdot 29 = 638 \equiv 1 \pmod{91}$.

Therefore $15^{71} = (15^{12})^5 \cdot 15^{11} \equiv 15^8 \cdot 15^2 \cdot 15 \equiv 22 \cdot 43 \cdot 15 \equiv 36 \cdot 15 = 540 \equiv 85 \equiv -6 \pmod{91}$.

Finally, $x \equiv 32 \cdot (-6) = -192 = -3(91) + 81 \equiv 81 \pmod{91}$.

Answer: $x \equiv 81 \pmod{91}$. (Check: $15 \times 81 = 1215 = 13 \times 91 + 32$.)

ISRT Year Final 2017 · AST 104 [6 Marks]

5. Find all incongruent solutions of: (i) $14x \equiv 36 \pmod{48}$,  (ii) $12x \equiv 98 \pmod{20}$,  (iii) $3x + 6 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$.

Hint: Always check $d = \gcd(a, m)$ first! In (i) $d=2 \mid 36$ (2 solutions); in (ii) $d=4 \nmid 98$ (no solution); in (iii) rewrite as $3x \equiv 10 \pmod{11}$ first.

(i) $14x \equiv 36 \pmod{48}$: $d = \gcd(14, 48) = 2 \mid 36$, so there are $2$ incongruent solutions. Dividing by $2$: $7x \equiv 18 \pmod{24}$. Since $7 \cdot 7 = 49 \equiv 1 \pmod{24}$, $x \equiv 7(18) = 126 \equiv 6 \pmod{24}$. The $2$ solutions mod $48$ are $x \equiv 6,\; 30 \pmod{48}$.

(ii) $12x \equiv 98 \pmod{20}$: $d = \gcd(12, 20) = 4$. Since $4 \nmid 98$ ($98 = 4\cdot 24 + 2$), this congruence has no solution.

(iii) $3x + 6 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$: Subtract $6$: $3x \equiv -1 \equiv 10 \pmod{11}$. Since $\gcd(3, 11) = 1$ and $3 \cdot 4 = 12 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$, multiply by $4$: $x \equiv 40 \equiv 7 \pmod{11}$. Unique solution: $x \equiv 7 \pmod{11}$.

ISRT 1st Incourse 2023 · AST 103 [1.5 × 2 Marks]

6. (a) Find the value of $(15^3 \bmod 17) \bmod 4$.  (b) Find the number of positive integers up to $40$ that are relatively prime to $40$.

Hint: For (a), replace $15$ with $-2 \pmod{17}$ so you don't need to cube $15$! For (b), compute $\phi(40)$ using $40 = 2^3 \cdot 5$.

(a) Since $15 \equiv -2 \pmod{17}$, we have $15^3 \equiv (-2)^3 = -8 \equiv 9 \pmod{17}$. Therefore $(15^3 \bmod 17) = 9$, and $9 \bmod 4 = \mathbf{1}$.

(b) We need $\phi(40)$. Factoring $40 = 2^3 \cdot 5^1$, we apply the product formula: $$\phi(40) = 40\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1 - \frac{1}{5}\right) = 40 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \mathbf{16}$$
