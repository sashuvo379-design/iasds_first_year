Section 01

Concept of Regression & The Population Model

Recall that Pearson's correlation r is symmetric—swapping X and Y gives the exact same value. In Regression Analysis, direction matters! Before fitting a model, we must designate which variable explains or predicts the other:

Independent Variable (X)

Also called the explanatory or predictor variable (input). Placed on the horizontal X-axis—used to gain information about Y (e.g., Height, Weight, Car Age, Income).

Dependent Variable (Y)

Also called the response or target variable (output). Placed on the vertical Y-axis—its expected value is predicted from X (e.g., Cholesterol Level, Car Price, Expenditure).

Conditional Mean Function:   E(Y | X) = α + β X
Population Simple Linear Regression Model:   Yi = α + β Xi + εi   (i = 1, 2, ..., n)

When the study involves only one independent variable, it is called Simple Linear Regression (SLR). (Adding more predictors, like Diabetes = w₀ + w₁BMI + w₂Exercise + w₃Weight, yields Multiple Linear Regression.) Here εi is the random error term capturing all factors affecting Y other than X.

Section 02

The 5 Classical Linear Regression Assumptions

Past ISRT finals (Final 2019 Q7b, Final 2020 Q7a) routinely ask you to state the underlying assumptions of the simple linear regression model Yi = α + βXi + εi:

1. Linearity

The true underlying relationship between X and the mean of Y is linear.

2. Independence

Error terms (εi) are statistically independent of one another.

3. Normality

For any given value of X, the errors εi are normally distributed.

4. Zero Mean

The probability distribution of errors has zero mean: E(εi) = 0.

5. Constant Variance

Errors have constant variance Var(εi) = σ² across all X (homoscedasticity).

Section 03

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Estimation & Interpretation

Developed by Carl Friedrich Gauss while tracking the dwarf planet Ceres, the Method of Least Squares chooses the unique line ŷi = α̂ + β̂ xi that minimizes the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)—the sum of squared vertical deviations between observed values yi and predicted values ŷi:

Minimize SSE = Σ (yi − ŷi)² = Σ (yi − α̂ − β̂ xi)²

Slope (β̂) = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / Σ(xi − x̄)² = [ Σxy − (1/n)ΣxΣy ] / [ Σx² − (1/n)(Σx)² ]
Intercept (α̂) = ȳ − β̂ x̄ = (1/n)Σy − β̂ (1/n)Σx
Interpreting Slope (β̂) & Intercept (α̂)
  • Slope (β̂): For every 1-unit increase in the independent variable X, the dependent variable Y is estimated to change (increase if +, decrease if −) by β̂ units on average.
  • Intercept (α̂): The point where the line crosses the Y-axis—literally, the estimated average value of Y when X = 0. (Warning: if X = 0 is outside the observed range, e.g., weight = 0 kg, the literal intercept may not have a physical meaning!)
Slide Worked Example: Weight vs. Cholesterol (n = 10)

Given Σx = 215, Σy = 2284, Σx² = 4761, Σxy = 49205:

  • β̂ = [49205 − (1/10)(215)(2284)] / [4761 − (1/10)(215)²] = 0.7148
  • α̂ = 228.4 − 0.7148(21.5) = 213.03
  • Fitted Line: Cholesterol = 213.03 + 0.7148 × Weight
  • Prediction (Weight = 21 kg): 213.03 + 0.7148(21) = 228.04.
Why Square the Errors? (Kelleher & Tierney Ch. 4) Squaring prevents positive residuals (underestimates) and negative residuals (overestimates) from canceling each other out—and makes SSE smooth and differentiable. However, squaring also weights far-away points heavily, which is why outliers can pull the OLS line strongly toward themselves!

Section 05 • High-Yield Proofs

OLS Derivation & Fundamental Properties Vault

Derivation 1 Final '19, '20, '22 & 2nd Incourse '24 Derive the OLS Estimators α̂ and β̂
Objective: Minimize the Sum of Squared Errors:
S(α̂, β̂) = Σi=1n ei² = Σi=1n (yi − α̂ − β̂ xi)²
Step 1 (Partial Derivatives = 0 → Normal Equations):
∂S / ∂α̂ = −2 Σ(yi − α̂ − β̂ xi) = 0 ⇒ Σyi = n α̂ + β̂ Σxi --- (1)
∂S / ∂β̂ = −2 Σ xi(yi − α̂ − β̂ xi) = 0 ⇒ Σxiyi = α̂ Σxi + β̂ Σxi² --- (2)
Step 2 (Solve Equation 1 for α̂):
Divide (1) by n: ȳ = α̂ + β̂ x̄ ⇒ α̂ = ȳ − β̂ x̄.
Step 3 (Substitute α̂ into Equation 2 and Solve for β̂):
Σxiyi = (ȳ − β̂ x̄) Σxi + β̂ Σxi²
Σxiyi − ȳ Σxi = β̂ (Σxi² − x̄ Σxi)
Σxi(yi − ȳ) = β̂ Σxi(xi − x̄) ⇒ β̂ = Σxi(yi − ȳ) / Σxi(xi − x̄).
Step 4 (Dr. Masud's Slide 22 Algebraic Identity):
Since Σ x̄(yi − ȳ) = x̄(nȳ − nȳ) = 0 and Σ x̄(xi − x̄) = 0, subtract both zero terms:
β̂ = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / Σ(xi − x̄)² = [Σxy − (1/n)ΣxΣy] / [Σx² − (1/n)(Σx)²]. [Derived]
Property 2 Three Built-In Consequences of the OLS Normal Equations
1. Sum of Residuals is Always Zero: From Normal Equation (1), Σei = Σ(yi − α̂ − β̂ xi) = 0.
2. Residuals are Orthogonal to X: From Normal Equation (2), Σeixi = 0 (used in Lesson 9 to prove SST = SSR + SSE!).
3. Fitted Line Always Passes Through (x̄, ȳ): Plug X = x̄ into ŷ = α̂ + β̂ X = (ȳ − β̂ x̄) + β̂ x̄ = ȳ.

Final 2023 • Q7 [12 Marks] Car Depreciation: Age vs. Price (8 Cars)

Data (n = 8): Age (X, years): 8, 3, 6, 9, 2, 5, 6, 3  |  Price (Y, $100s): 45, 210, 100, 33, 267, 134, 109, 235.

  • (a) Scatter Diagram: Points fall tightly along a downward-sloping straight line → strong negative linear relationship (verify in Lab above!).
  • (b) Regression of Price (Y) on Age (X):
    Σx = 42 (x̄ = 5.25), Σy = 1133 (ȳ = 141.625), Σx² = 264, Σxy = 4450
    Sxx = 264 − 42²/8 = 43.5  |  Sxy = 4450 − (42 × 1133)/8 = −1498.25
    β̂ = −1498.25 / 43.5 = −34.4425  |  α̂ = 141.625 − (−34.4425)(5.25) = 322.448
    Fitted Line: Ŷ = 322.45 − 34.44 X.
  • (c) Interpretation: β̂ = −34.44 means each additional year of age reduces the car's estimated average price by $3,444. α̂ = 322.45 estimates the price of a brand-new (0-year-old) car at $32,245.
  • (d) Predict Price of a 7-year-old car: Ŷ = 322.448 − 34.4425(7) = 81.35 (hundreds) = $8,135.
Final 2020 • Q7(2) [6 Marks] 5-Observation Regression & Prediction at X = 7

Data (n = 5): X = {4, 5, 3, 6, 10}, Y = {4, 6, 5, 7, 7}.

  • Σx = 28 (x̄ = 5.6), Σy = 29 (ȳ = 5.8), Σx² = 186, Σxy = 173
  • Sxx = 186 − 28²/5 = 29.2  |  Sxy = 173 − (28×29)/5 = 10.6
  • β̂ = 10.6 / 29.2 = 0.3630  |  α̂ = 5.8 − 0.3630(5.6) = 3.7671
  • (i) Regression Equation: Y' = 3.7671 + 0.3630 X
  • (ii) Prediction when X = 7: Y' = 3.7671 + 0.3630(7) = 6.308.
Final 2017 • Q7(b) [9 Marks] Fleet Vehicle Weight (100s lbs) vs. Highway MPG

Data (n = 10): Weight (X): 29, 35, 28, 44, 25, 34, 30, 33, 28, 24  |  MPG (Y): 31, 27, 29, 25, 31, 29, 28, 28, 28, 33.

  • Σx = 310 (x̄ = 31), Σy = 289 (ȳ = 28.9), Σx² = 9916, Σxy = 8857
  • Sxx = 9916 − 310²/10 = 306  |  Sxy = 8857 − (310×289)/10 = −102
  • β̂ = −102 / 306 = −1/3 ≈ −0.3333  |  α̂ = 28.9 − (−1/3)(31) = 39.2333
  • (i) Relationship: MPG = 39.233 − 0.333 × Weight (each additional 100 lbs of vehicle weight lowers highway fuel economy by 0.333 mpg on average).
  • (ii) Predict for 4,200 lbs (X = 42): Ŷ = 39.2333 − (1/3)(42) = 39.2333 − 14 = 25.23 mpg.
Final 2024 • Q1(a)–(d) [4 Marks] Interpreting Expenditure = 5654 + 0.88 × Income

Given: r = 0.79 and Expenditure = 5654 + 0.88 × Income (in Tk).

  • (a) Relationship: There is a strong positive linear relationship between income and expenditure (r = 0.79), meaning higher-income households tend to spend significantly more.
  • (c) Average spending with zero income (X = 0): The intercept α̂ = Tk 5,654 (autonomous baseline expenditure).
  • (d) Increase in income by 1 Taka: The slope β̂ = Tk 0.88—expenditure is expected to increase by 0.88 Taka on average.
