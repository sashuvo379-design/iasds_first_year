Section 01

Bivariate Data, Graphical Displays & Scatter Diagrams

Bivariate data consist of paired observations (xi, yi) collected on two variables for each element. Bivariate analysis investigates whether a statistical association exists, how strong it is, and whether one variable can be predicted from the other.

Qualitative vs. Qualitative

Bivariate Bar Diagram (clustered/stacked bars) or Contingency Table (e.g., Party Affiliation vs. Opinion on Tax Reform).

Quantitative vs. Qualitative

Bar Diagram of Mean ± SD (whiskers on top of mean bars) or Scatter Plot of Medians across each category.

Quantitative vs. Quantitative

Scatter Diagram (Scatter Plot): plots the independent variable X on the horizontal axis and dependent variable Y on the vertical axis.

4 Things a Scatter Diagram Reveals at a Glance (Final 2019 Q6a & Final 2024 Q7a)
  1. Type of relationship: Linear vs. nonlinear (curved).
  2. Existence of a linear relationship (or random cloud = none).
  3. Strength of the relationship (tightly clustered along a line vs. widely scattered).
  4. Direction of the relationship: Positive (bottom-left to top-right) vs. Negative (top-left to bottom-right).
Section 02

Covariance & The 4-Quadrant Intuition

Draw vertical and horizontal reference lines at the means x̄ and ȳ on a scatter plot. They slice the plane into four quadrants based on the signs of (xi − x̄) and (yi − ȳ):

Positive Cross-Products: QI & QIII
  • QI: (x − x̄) > 0 and (y − ȳ) > 0 ⇒ product +
  • QIII: (x − x̄) < 0 and (y − ȳ) < 0 ⇒ product +
  • If most points fall in QI and QIII, Cov(X, Y) > 0 (positive linear trend).
Negative Cross-Products: QII & QIV
  • QII: (x − x̄) < 0 and (y − ȳ) > 0 ⇒ product −
  • QIV: (x − x̄) > 0 and (y − ȳ) < 0 ⇒ product −
  • If most points fall in QII and QIV, Cov(X, Y) < 0 (negative linear trend).
Sample Covariance:   Cov(X, Y) = [1 / (n − 1)] Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) = [Σ xi yi − (1/n)(Σxi)(Σyi)] / (n − 1)

Key Properties of Covariance: (1) Symmetric: Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X); (2) Unbounded: ranges from −∞ to +∞; (3) Independent of origin shift, but dependent on scale (units change the magnitude, making it hard to judge strength directly!).

Section 03

Karl Pearson's Product-Moment Correlation Coefficient (r)

To eliminate unit dependence and bound the strength between −1 and +1, Karl Pearson rescaled covariance by the product of the two standard deviations:

r = Cov(X, Y) / (sx · sy) = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / √[ Σ(xi − x̄)² · Σ(yi − ȳ)² ]
Computational Shortcut:   r = [ Σxy − (1/n)ΣxΣy ] / √[ {Σx² − (1/n)(Σx)²} · {Σy² − (1/n)(Σy)²} ]

Real-World Examples (Slides & Kelleher)
  • Positive (+): Height & weight, income & expenditure, shoe size & height (r = 0.898), BMI & diabetes risk (r = 0.877).
  • Negative (−): Price & demand, altitude & temperature, exercise & body weight (r = −0.710).
  • Zero (0): Tea consumed & intelligence, computer price & Coca-Cola consumption.
Crucial Warnings & Exam Traps
  • Always −1 ≤ r ≤ +1: Obtaining r = 1.23 (Final 2017 Q6d) guarantees an arithmetic error!
  • Two points only (n = 2): Always yields r = +1 or r = −1 (a line passes perfectly through any 2 points).
  • Independence ⇒ r = 0, but r = 0 does NOT imply independence (e.g., Y = X² is deterministic yet has r = 0!).
  • Correlation ≠ Causation!

Section 05 • Mihad Notes & Final 2024/2025

Correlation Ratio (η) & Detecting Nonlinearity

Pearson's r only measures linear association—and fails when a relationship is curved (or when X is nominal and Y is quantitative). The Correlation Ratio (ηyx) measures the overall (linear + nonlinear) strength of relationship by comparing between-group variation of group means to total variation:

η²yx = [ Σi ni (ȳi − ȳ)² ] / [ Σi Σj (yij − ȳ)² ] = (Between-Group Variation) / (Total Variation)
0 ≤ η ≤ 1   |   Always: η² ≥ r² (or |η| ≥ |r|)   |   Departure from Linearity = η² − r²
Key Decision Rules (2nd Incourse 2025)
  • Nominal X vs. Quantitative Y: Use Correlation Ratio (η).
  • Nonlinear quantitative relationship: Use η, because |η| > |r|.
  • Strictly linear relationship: η² = r² (i.e., η = |r|).
  • Asymmetry: Unlike r, generally ηyx ≠ ηxy.
Worked Example (Mihad's Class Notes)

Suppose X = 1: Y = {2, 4} (ȳ₁ = 3); X = 2: Y = {7, 9} (ȳ₂ = 8); X = 3: Y = {5, 3} (ȳ₃ = 4). Overall mean ȳ = 5.

  • Between-Group SS: 2(3−5)² + 2(8−5)² + 2(4−5)² = 8 + 18 + 2 = 28
  • Total SS: (2−5)²+(4−5)²+(7−5)²+(9−5)²+(5−5)²+(3−5)² = 34
  • η² = 28/34 = 0.824 ⇒ η = 0.907 (very strong inverted-V curve!), while r = 0.17!
  • Departure from linearity: η² − r² = 0.824 − 0.029 = 0.795 (strong nonlinearity).
Section 06 • High-Yield Proofs

Proofs & Derivations Vault (4 Core Theorems)

Theorem 1 2nd Incourse 2024 Prove that −1 ≤ r ≤ +1
Define standardized variables ui = (xi − x̄) / sx and vi = (yi − ȳ) / sy.
Note that Σui² = n − 1, Σvi² = n − 1, and Σ(ui vi) = (n − 1) · r.
Upper bound (r ≤ +1): Since a sum of squares is never negative:
Σ(ui − vi)² ≥ 0 ⇒ Σui² − 2Σuivi + Σvi² ≥ 0
(n − 1) − 2(n − 1)r + (n − 1) ≥ 0 ⇒ 2(n − 1)(1 − r) ≥ 0 ⇒ 1 − r ≥ 0 ⇒ r ≤ 1.
Lower bound (r ≥ −1): Similarly:
Σ(ui + vi)² ≥ 0 ⇒ Σui² + 2Σuivi + Σvi² ≥ 0
(n − 1) + 2(n − 1)r + (n − 1) ≥ 0 ⇒ 2(n − 1)(1 + r) ≥ 0 ⇒ 1 + r ≥ 0 ⇒ r ≥ −1.
Combining both inequalities gives −1 ≤ r ≤ +1. [Proved]
Theorem 2 2nd Incourse 2025 Q3(b) Correlation Coefficient is Independent of Origin & Scale
Let ui = a1 + b1xi and vi = a2 + b2yi (with b1, b2 ≠ 0). Then ū = a1 + b1x̄ and v̄ = a2 + b2ȳ.
Deviations: ui − ū = b1(xi − x̄), vi − v̄ = b2(yi − ȳ). (Origin shifts a1, a2 cancel!)
ruv = Σ(ui − ū)(vi − v̄) / √[ Σ(ui − ū)² · Σ(vi − v̄)² ]
= [ b1 b2 Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) ] / √[ b1² Σ(xi − x̄)² · b2² Σ(yi − ȳ)² ]
= [ b1 b2 / (|b1| · |b2|) ] · rxy.
If b1 and b2 have the same sign (both + or both −), ruv = +rxy.
If b1 and b2 have opposite signs, ruv = −rxy (same magnitude, flipped sign). [Proved]
Theorem 3 Final 2017 Q6(a) For Any Two Distinct Data Pairs (n = 2), r = ±1
For (x1, y1) and (x2, y2): x̄ = (x1+x2)/2, ȳ = (y1+y2)/2.
Thus x1 − x̄ = (x1−x2)/2, x2 − x̄ = −(x1−x2)/2; and y1 − ȳ = (y1−y2)/2, y2 − ȳ = −(y1−y2)/2.
Numerator: Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) = ¼(x1−x2)(y1−y2) + ¼(x1−x2)(y1−y2) = ½(x1−x2)(y1−y2).
Denominator: √[ ½(x1−x2)² · ½(y1−y2)² ] = ½ |x1−x2| · |y1−y2|.
Dividing numerator by denominator gives r = ±1 (+1 if slope > 0, −1 if slope < 0). [Proved]
Theorem 4 If Two Variables are Independent, r = 0 (But Converse is False!)
In expectation form: ρ = Cov(X, Y) / √[Var(X)Var(Y)] = [E(XY) − E(X)E(Y)] / √[Var(X)Var(Y)].
If X and Y are independent, E(XY) = E(X)·E(Y), so the numerator is 0 ⇒ ρ = 0.
Counterexample to converse: Let X ∈ {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2} and Y = X² ∈ {4, 1, 0, 1, 4}. Here x̄ = 0 and Σxiyi = −8 − 1 + 0 + 1 + 8 = 0 ⇒ r = 0, even though Y is 100% determined by X!

Final 2022 Q6(b) & Final 2025 [9 Marks] Midterm vs. Final Exam Scores (Compute Pearson's r)

Data (n = 7): Midterm (X): 79, 95, 81, 66, 87, 94, 59  |  Final (Y): 85, 97, 78, 76, 94, 84, 67.

Worked Solution:

  • (i) Expectation: Positively related (students who perform well on the midterm generally perform well on the final).
  • (ii) Scatter Plot: Points rise steadily from bottom-left (59, 67) to top-right (95, 97), so we expect r to be close to +1 (load the Final '22/'25 preset in Lab 1 above to view it!).
  • (iii) Exact Computation:
    Σx = 561, Σy = 581, Σx² = 46,069, Σy² = 48,875, Σxy = 47,291
    Sxy = 47291 − (561 × 581)/7 = 47291 − 46563 = 728
    Sxx = 46069 − (561²)/7 = 46069 − 44960.14 = 1108.857
    Syy = 48875 − (581²)/7 = 48875 − 48223 = 652
    r = 728 / √(1108.857 × 652) = 728 / 850.28 = +0.8562 (Strong positive linear correlation—consistent with parts i & ii!).
Final 2025 Q5 [3 Marks] Years of Schooling vs. Income: r = 0.64 vs. η = 0.80
  • (i) From Scatter Diagram: Moderate-to-strong positive upward trend (more years of schooling generally correspond to higher monthly income), with noticeable vertical spread.
  • (ii) Slope of Ŷ = −43.68 + 8.66X: Each additional year of schooling is associated with an average increase of 8.66 thousand Taka (Tk 8,660) in monthly income.
  • (iii) r = 0.64 vs. η = 0.80 — Should you revise your comment? Yes! Because η (0.80) > |r| (0.64) (and η² − r² = 0.64 − 0.4096 = 0.2304), the true relationship has a substantial nonlinear component. A simple straight line understates the overall strength of association between schooling and income.
