Section 01

What is Statistics? History, Branches & Scope

The word statistics carries two distinct meanings in everyday and academic language:

Meaning 1 • Plural Sense

Numerical Facts

Recorded figures representing real-world measurements—such as family income, student age, a quarterback's completion percentage, or starting salaries of graduates.

Meaning 2 • Singular Sense

The Scientific Discipline

Statistics is the science of collecting, analyzing, presenting, and interpreting data, as well as making decisions based on such analyses.

Historical Evolution of Statistics

Historically known as the "Science of Statecraft", the word derives from the Latin status, Italian statista, German statistik, and French statistique—each referring to a political state.

Ancient Era
Ancient Egypt, Rome & China systematically recorded population, land, and resources for taxation and governance.
17th Century
Blaise Pascal & Pierre-Simon Laplace developed probability theory out of gambling and uncertainty.
19th Century
C. F. Gauss introduced the normal distribution; Galton & Karl Pearson created correlation and regression.
20th Century
R. A. Fisher pioneered experimental design & ANOVA; Neyman & Egon Pearson formalized hypothesis testing & confidence intervals.
Modern Era
Deep integration with Data Science, Machine Learning, and AI across healthcare, finance, engineering, and public policy.

Two Aspects & Two Branches

Theoretical vs. Applied
  • Theoretical (Mathematical) Statistics: Focuses on the development, derivation, and proof of statistical theorems, formulas, rules, and laws.
  • Applied Statistics: Applies those theorems, formulas, and methods to solve real-world problems in medicine, economics, sports, industry, and government.
Descriptive vs. Inferential
  • Descriptive Statistics: Methods for organizing, displaying, and describing data using tables, graphs, and numerical summary measures (e.g., mean, histogram).
  • Inferential Statistics: Methods that use sample results to make decisions, estimates, or predictions about a larger population (e.g., surveying 2,000 graduates to estimate the national starting salary).

Uses, Scope & Abuses of Statistics

Why do almost all university disciplines require statistics? (1) To read and critically evaluate studies in your field, (2) to conduct original research and forecast outcomes, and (3) to become informed consumers and citizens. Career paths span Statisticians, Data Scientists, Biostatisticians, Financial Analysts, Market Research Analysts, Actuaries, and Operations Research Analysts.

Exam Focus: Limitations & Abuses of Applied Statistics Statistics is a powerful tool, but it can be misused or limited when:
  • Biased or non-representative samples are generalized to the whole population.
  • Association is mistaken for causation (ignoring confounding variables).
  • Misleading graphs (truncated axes, distorted areas) or cherry-picked averages (e.g., using the mean on heavily skewed income data) are used to manipulate opinion.
  • Aggregate limitation: Statistics deals with aggregates/groups rather than explaining an isolated individual case with certainty.
Section 02

Population, Sample, Parameter, Statistic & Basic Terms

Population vs. Sample

  • Population: Consists of all elements—individuals, items, or objects—whose characteristics are being studied. (It does not have to be people; it can be houses, books, cars, or companies.)
  • Sample: A portion (subset) of the population selected for study.

Parameter vs. Statistic

  • Population Parameter: A numerical summary measure (mean, median, variance, SD) calculated for a population data set.
  • Sample Statistic: A numerical summary measure calculated for a sample data set.
Exam Trap (1st Incourse 2025 MCQ #1) Population parameters are conventionally denoted by lowercase Greek letters (e.g., μ, σ, ρ), whereas sample statistics use Roman letters (e.g., x̄, s, r). Easy mnemonic: Population → Parameter; Sample → Statistic.

Section 03

Types of Variables & Cross-Section vs. Time-Series Data

Quantitative Variables

Can be measured numerically (incomes, heights, gross sales, number of cars, accidents). Divided into two subtypes:

  • Discrete: Values are countable with no intermediate values between consecutive counts (e.g., 0, 1, 2, ... — number of houses, cars, siblings, typographical errors).
  • Continuous: Can assume any value within an interval, limited only by measurement precision (e.g., height, weight, age, length, time taken on an exam: 42.674 min).
Qualitative (Categorical) Variables

Cannot assume a meaningful numerical value, but can be classified into two or more nonnumeric categories.

  • Student class standing (freshman, sophomore, junior, senior)
  • Gender, blood group, brand of a computer, make of a car, opinions of people, spring break locations.
Exam Trap: Money is Treated as Continuous! In Dr. Masud's slides and Mihad's notes, any variable involving money (salaries, monthly TV cable bills, lottery revenues, prices) is explicitly classified as a Quantitative Continuous variable.

Cross-Section Data vs. Time-Series Data

Cross-Section Data

Data collected on different elements at the same point in time (or for the same period of time).

Examples: Incomes of 100 families in 2009; Charitable givings of 6 retailers in 2007; Supermarkets in 40 cities on Dec 31, 2009.

Time-Series Data

Data collected on the same element for the same variable at different points in time (or periods).

Examples: U.S. exports from 1983 to 2009; Total indoor movie screens each year from 2003 to 2008; Monthly food bill of one family during 2009.

Section 04

The Four Scales of Measurement (NOIR)

Beyond qualitative vs. quantitative, variables are ranked into a four-level hierarchy of measurement precision: Nominal → Ordinal → Interval → Ratio. Each higher level retains all properties of the levels below it and adds one new mathematical property.

1. Nominal

Mutually exclusive, exhaustive categories with no inherent order.

Examples: Blood group (A, B, AB, O), Gender, Eye color, Zip code, Student ID, Religion, Nationality.

2. Ordinal

Categories can be ranked/ordered, but exact gaps between ranks are unknown or unequal.

Examples: Letter grades (A–F), 1st/2nd/3rd place, Rating scale (poor/good/excellent), Player rankings.

3. Interval

Ordered + equal, meaningful differences between units, but no true zero (0 is arbitrary).

Examples: Temperature (°C, °F), IQ score, SAT score, Calendar year.

4. Ratio

All interval properties + a true zero (0 = complete absence), so ratios are meaningful (2× as much).

Examples: Height, Weight, Age, Time, Salary, Kelvin temperature, Blood pressure, Number of siblings.

Classic Distinction: Celsius vs. Kelvin & Student IDs
  • 0°C or 0°F does not mean zero heat (so 20°C is not "twice as hot" as 10°C) → Interval. By contrast, 0 Kelvin is absolute zero (zero thermal motion) → Ratio.
  • Student ID / Zip Code / Jersey Number use digits, but they only label identity—averaging two IDs is meaningless → Nominal.

Section 06

Sources of Data, Sampling Methods, Surveys & Experiments

1. Sources of Data: Primary vs. Secondary

Data come from three main sources: Internal sources (company accounting/HR/police records), External sources (organizations outside the firm), and Surveys & Experiments.

Primary Data

Collected originally by the investigator or organization for a specific purpose; pure, original, and never previously subjected to statistical treatment.

Merits: Tailored to exact research goals, higher reliability/control.
Demerits: Expensive, time-consuming, requires trained personnel.

Secondary Data

Obtained from a source that did not originally collect it, or reused from published official sources (e.g., BBS, BANBEIS, NIPORT, Bangladesh Bank, BARC; major surveys like BDHS, MICS, LFS, MMS, UHS).

Advantages over Primary: Saves time and cost, immediately available, enables large-scale/historical comparisons.
Limitations: Original purpose may not match; possible missing variables or incompatible definitions.

2. Census vs. Sample Survey

A Census includes every member of the population, whereas a Sample Survey studies a selected subset. We usually prefer a sample survey for three core reasons:

  1. Time: Populations are often huge; sampling yields timely results.
  2. Cost: Surveying every element typically exceeds budget constraints.
  3. Impossibility: Some populations cannot be fully reached (e.g., homeless individuals), and some tests are destructive (e.g., burning out every lightbulb or draining every battery to test average lifespan).

3. Interactive Sampling Methods Explorer

Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

Every member of the population has a known, equal chance of being selected (e.g., drawing 10 names from a box of 100 students, or using random numbers).

  • With Replacement: Selected element is returned to the population before the next draw (population size stays constant; repeats allowed, e.g., rolling a die).
  • Without Replacement: Selected element is not returned (population shrinks by 1 each draw; no repeats—most common in real surveys).

Systematic Random Sampling

Randomly select one unit from the first k units, then include every k-th unit thereafter. Example: To select 10 students from 50, compute k = 50/10 = 5, pick a random start between 1 and 5, and choose every 5th student (or every 7th shopper entering a mall).

Stratified Random Sampling

Divide a heterogeneous population into homogeneous subgroups called strata (based on gender, income, smoking status, etc.), then draw a random sample from every stratum. Ensures every subgroup is represented.

Cluster Sampling

Divide the population into (often geographical) groups called clusters—where each cluster mirrors the population—randomly select a few clusters, and survey all elements inside those chosen clusters (e.g., interviewing all teachers in 2 randomly chosen school buildings).

Non-Random Samples (Some members have zero chance of selection)

  • Convenience Sample: Selecting the most accessible members for speed (e.g., polling shoppers at a single mall).
  • Judgment Sample: Selecting members based on an expert's prior knowledge/judgment; rarely guaranteed to be representative for large populations.

4. Sampling vs. Non-Sampling Errors & Questionnaires

Sampling vs. Non-Sampling Error

  • Sampling Error (Chance Error): The natural difference between a sample result and the true population value simply because only a subset was observed. Occurs only in sample surveys.
  • Non-Sampling Error (Systematic Error): Human mistakes in data collection, recording, coding, or tabulation. Occurs in both sample surveys and censuses.

Questionnaire Preparation Standards

Called the "heart of a survey operation." Good questions must be:

  • Easily understood, simple, and logically sequenced.
  • Concrete & realistic (ask "razor blades used last week" instead of "annually").
  • Include uncertainty options ("Don't know", "No preference") and avoid hard-to-analyze ambiguous open-ended questions.

5. Observational Study vs. Controlled Experiment

Observational Study

The researcher observes or surveys subjects without controlling factors or randomly assigning treatments (e.g., comparing people who voluntarily chose Diet 1 vs. Diet 2). Because other variables are not controlled, effects can be confounded (e.g., higher income vs. higher rent is confounded by family size and neighborhood). Association does not prove causation!

Controlled Experiment

The researcher actively controls factors and randomly assigns subjects into treatment/control groups (e.g., randomly assigning volunteers to Diet 1 vs. Diet 2, or herb vs. placebo). Designed experiments allow us to establish cause-and-effect relationships.

Section 07

Data Preparation, Cleaning, Outliers & Ethics

Before running any statistical analysis or hypothesis test, raw data must undergo data processing and cleaning:

1. Data Cleaning & Verification

Human entry errors are inevitable in large datasets. Verify every numerical and categorical entry; when an impossible or suspicious value appears, trace its case number back to the original survey/record and correct it.

2. Handling Missing Data

Evaluate both the number of missing cases and the pattern (pattern is more influential!). Distinguish randomly missing (scattered) from non-randomly missing (clustered on sensitive items like income). For imputation, use the mean for continuous data and the median for ordinal data.

3. Detecting Univariate Outliers

Extreme scores at either tail of a variable's distribution. Detect them using boxplots, stem-and-leaf plots, or standardized z-scores. General rule from slides: a raw score with z > 3 or z < -3 is flagged as an outlier.

4. Ethical Issues in Data Collection

Key ethical responsibilities include obtaining informed consent, protecting respondent privacy & confidentiality (anonymizing personal identifiers), avoiding data fabrication/cherry-picking, and ensuring voluntary, unbiased participation.

Final 2025 • Q1(a),(b) [2 Marks] Qualitative vs. Quantitative & Measurement Scales

Question: (a) Distinguish between qualitative and quantitative variables with one example of each. [1]  (b) Identify the scale of measurement of: (i) IQ Score, (ii) number of siblings. [1]

Model Solution:

  • (a) A qualitative (categorical) variable cannot take meaningful numerical values and classifies elements into nonnumeric categories (e.g., blood group, gender). A quantitative variable is measured numerically and supports arithmetic operations (e.g., height, number of siblings).
  • (b) (i) IQ Score: Interval scale (equal unit differences exist, e.g., 109 to 110, but there is no true zero representing total absence of intelligence). (ii) Number of siblings: Ratio scale (countable quantitative data with a true zero—0 means no siblings, and 4 siblings is twice as many as 2).
Final 2024 • Q4(a),(b),(c) [8 Marks] Four Levels of Measurement & Data Ethics

Question: (a) Explain the four levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio with appropriate examples. [4] (b) Identify and justify the most appropriate level for: (i) Blood type (A, B, AB, O), (ii) Temperature in Celsius, (iii) Student ID number, (iv) Rankings in a singing competition. [2] (c) Describe one ethical issue in data collection. [2]

Model Solution:

  • (a) Define all four scales clearly (categories → order → equal intervals → true zero) with 1–2 examples each (see Section 4).
  • (b)
    1. Blood type: Nominal — mutually exclusive categories with no natural ranking.
    2. Temperature in Celsius: Interval — equal 1°C increments, but 0°C is arbitrary (freezing point of water, not zero heat).
    3. Student ID number: Nominal — numeric digits serve purely as unique labels; arithmetic/ranking is meaningless.
    4. Rankings in a singing competition: Ordinal — ordered (1st, 2nd, 3rd), but the gap in talent between 1st and 2nd need not equal the gap between 2nd and 3rd.
  • (c) Ethical Issue (e.g., Privacy & Informed Consent): Collecting personal or sensitive data (medical records, income) without informing participants of the study's purpose, obtaining voluntary consent, or safeguarding confidentiality risks harming respondents and violates research ethics.

1st Incourse 2023 • Q2(a) & Mid-Term 2017 • Q3(a),(c),(d) Short-Answer Essentials
  • Descriptive vs. Inferential Statistics (1st Incourse 2023 Q2a [1]): Descriptive summarizes and displays observed data via tables, graphs, and summary measures; inferential uses sample data to estimate parameters, test hypotheses, and make predictions about a population.
  • Advantages of Secondary Data over Primary Data (Final 2017 Q1i [1]): Lower cost, faster availability, wider geographical/historical coverage, and no need for field logistics.
  • Mutually Exclusive & Exhaustive Categories (Final 2017 Q1j [1]): Mutually exclusive means no element can belong to more than one category (e.g., Blood groups A, B, AB, O). Exhaustive means every possible element fits into at least one listed category.
  • Data Processing Phase (Mid-Term 2017 Q3d [2]): Converting raw collected data into clean, analysis-ready form through editing/cleaning (fixing entry errors via case numbers), coding, handling missing values, checking outliers, and tabulating.
