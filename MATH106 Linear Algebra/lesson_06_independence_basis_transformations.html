Linear Independence, Basis, Dimension & Transformations

A basis is the minimal, non-redundant skeleton of a vector space—just enough independent vectors to reach every point uniquely. Master testing independence across $\mathbb{R}^n$, $P_n$, and $M_{22}$, computing coordinate vectors $[\mathbf{v}]_S$, and deriving explicit formulas for linear transformations $T(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ from their action on a basis.

Strang's Key Idea for Linear Transformations (Ch. 7.2): Once you know $T(\mathbf{v}_1), \dots, T(\mathbf{v}_n)$ for a basis, linearity forces $T(\mathbf{v}) = c_1 T(\mathbf{v}_1) + \dots + c_n T(\mathbf{v}_n)$ for every vector $\mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n$.

THEORY 2. Linear Independence, Basis & Dimension

2.1 Linear Independence Test

Vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ are linearly independent if the vector equation

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$$

has only the trivial solution $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$.

  • $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$: Stack as columns of $A$. Independent $\iff \det(A) \neq 0$.
  • Too many vectors: Any set of $k > \dim(V)$ vectors is automatically dependent (e.g., 4 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$).
  • Zero vector rule: Any set containing $\mathbf{0}$ is dependent (but a dependent set doesn't have to contain $\mathbf{0}$!).

2.2 Basis, Dimension & The Half-Check Shortcut

$S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ is a basis for $V$ if: (1) $S$ is linearly independent, AND (2) $\text{span}(S) = V$.

Standard Dimensions:
• $\dim(\mathbb{R}^n) = n$ &qquad • $\dim(P_n) = n + 1$ &qquad • $\dim(M_{mn}) = mn$
Exam Shortcut (Half-Check Theorem): If $\dim(V) = n$ and $S$ has exact size $n$, you only need to check ONE condition: $$S \text{ is linearly independent } \iff S \text{ spans } V \iff S \text{ is a basis!}$$
Interactive Proof: Uniqueness of Basis Representation Anton Thm 4.4.1

Theorem: If $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ is a basis for $V$, then every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ can be written in one and only one way as $\mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n$.

Step 1: Existence (uses Spanning)
Because $S$ spans $V$, every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ has at least one representation $\mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n$.
Step 2: Assume two representations and subtract
Suppose $\mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n = k_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + k_n\mathbf{v}_n$. Subtracting yields: $$(c_1 - k_1)\mathbf{v}_1 + (c_2 - k_2)\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + (c_n - k_n)\mathbf{v}_n = \mathbf{0}.$$
Step 3: Apply Linear Independence
Since $S$ is linearly independent, every coefficient must be zero: $c_i - k_i = 0 \implies c_i = k_i$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$. Thus the coordinate vector $[\mathbf{v}]_S = (c_1, \dots, c_n)$ is unique! $\blacksquare$

OPERATORS 3. Linear Transformations $T: V \to W$ Defined on a Basis

Definition of Linearity

A mapping $T: V \to W$ is a linear transformation if for all $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in V$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}), \qquad T(c\mathbf{u}) = c\,T(\mathbf{u})$$

Consequence: $T(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}$ always, and $T(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n) = c_1 T(\mathbf{v}_1) + \dots + c_n T(\mathbf{v}_n)$.

3-Step Exam Recipe (Find $T(x_1, x_2, x_3)$)

  1. Express arbitrary $\mathbf{x}$ in basis $S$: Solve $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\mathbf{v}_3 = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ for $c_1, c_2, c_3$ in terms of $x_1, x_2, x_3$.
  2. Apply linearity: Substitute $c_1, c_2, c_3$ into $T(\mathbf{x}) = c_1 T(\mathbf{v}_1) + c_2 T(\mathbf{v}_2) + c_3 T(\mathbf{v}_3)$.
  3. Simplify & evaluate: Collect terms in $x_1, x_2, x_3$, then plug in the requested vector (and double-check on $\mathbf{v}_1$!).

Problem 1: Linear Independence in $M_{22}$ with Parameter $k$ 2nd Incourse 2020 Q2 (4 Marks)

Determine all values of $k$ for which the following matrices are linearly independent in $M_{22}$:

$$A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & k \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ k & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
Hint: Set $c_1 A_1 + c_2 A_2 + c_3 A_3 = 0_{2\times 2}$. The $(1,2)$ entry is $0=0$, leaving a $3\times 3$ homogeneous system in $(c_1, c_2, c_3)$. Set its determinant $\neq 0$!
Solution:
1. Setting $c_1 A_1 + c_2 A_2 + c_3 A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ gives the homogeneous system: $$\begin{cases} c_1 - c_2 + 2c_3 = 0 \\ c_1 + kc_2 + 2c_3 = 0 \\ kc_1 + c_2 + 3c_3 = 0 \end{cases}$$ 2. Compute the coefficient determinant (apply $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$): $$\det = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & k & 2 \\ k & 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & k+1 & 0 \\ k & 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = (k+1)(3 - 2k) = -(k+1)(2k - 3).$$ 3. Linear independence holds iff $\det \neq 0$, i.e., $\mathbf{k \neq -1}$ and $\mathbf{k \neq \frac{3}{2}}$.
Problem 2: Basis for $P_2$ & Coordinate Vector $[p]_S$ 2nd Incourse 2025 Q2 (5 Marks)

Show that $S = \{p_1, p_2, p_3\}$ is a basis for $P_2$, where $p_1 = 1 + x + x^2$, $p_2 = x + x^2$, $p_3 = x^2$. Then find the coordinate vector of $p = 7 - x + 2x^2$ relative to $S$.

Quick check — enter $[p]_S = (c_1, c_2, c_3)$:
Solution:
1. Since $\dim(P_2) = 3$ and $|S| = 3$, it suffices to show $S$ is linearly independent. Representing coefficients of $(1, x, x^2)$ as columns: $$\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1 \neq 0 \implies S \text{ is a basis for } P_2.$$ 2. Solve $c_1 p_1 + c_2 p_2 + c_3 p_3 = 7 - x + 2x^2$: $$\begin{cases} c_1 = 7 \\ c_1 + c_2 = -1 \implies c_2 = -8 \\ c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 2 \implies 7 - 8 + c_3 = 2 \implies c_3 = 3 \end{cases}$$ Coordinate vector: $[p]_S = \mathbf{(7, -8, 3)}$.
Problem 3: Finding $T(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ from Basis Images Final 2025 Q6(b) · Final 2022 Q7(a) (6 Marks)

Consider the basis $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}$ for $\mathbb{R}^3$ where $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1,1)$, $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1,1,0)$, $\mathbf{v}_3 = (1,0,0)$, and let $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be the linear operator with $T(\mathbf{v}_1) = (2,-1,4)$, $T(\mathbf{v}_2) = (3,0,1)$, $T(\mathbf{v}_3) = (-1,5,1)$. Find a formula for $T(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ and use it to compute $T(2, 4, -1)$.

Hint: Write $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = c_1(1,1,1) + c_2(1,1,0) + c_3(1,0,0)$. Reading from the bottom component up immediately gives $c_1 = x_3$, $c_2 = x_2 - x_3$, $c_3 = x_1 - x_2$!
Solution:
1. Coordinates in basis $S$: $$\begin{cases} c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = x_1 \\ c_1 + c_2 = x_2 \\ c_1 = x_3 \end{cases} \implies c_1 = x_3,\quad c_2 = x_2 - x_3,\quad c_3 = x_1 - x_2.$$ 2. Apply linearity: $$T(x_1, x_2, x_3) = x_3(2, -1, 4) + (x_2 - x_3)(3, 0, 1) + (x_1 - x_2)(-1, 5, 1)$$ $$= \mathbf{(-x_1 + 4x_2 - x_3,\;\; 5x_1 - 5x_2 - x_3,\;\; x_1 + 3x_3)}.$$ 3. Evaluate at $(2, 4, -1)$: $$T(2, 4, -1) = (-2 + 16 + 1,\; 10 - 20 + 1,\; 2 - 3) = \mathbf{(15, -9, -1)}.$$
Problem 4: Linear Independence vs. Dependence in $\mathbb{R}^3$ Final 2025 Q3(c) · 2nd Incourse 2025 Q4(b)

Determine whether each set of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is linearly independent or linearly dependent:
(a) $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, -2, 3),\; \mathbf{v}_2 = (5, 6, -1),\; \mathbf{v}_3 = (3, 2, 1)$   (Final 2025 Q3c)
(b) $\mathbf{u} = (1, 2, 3),\; \mathbf{v} = (-4, 5, 6),\; \mathbf{w} = (7, -8, 9)$   (2nd Incourse 2025 Q4b)

Solution:
• (a) Linearly Dependent: $$\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & 3 \\ -2 & 6 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1(6+2) - 5(-2-6) + 3(2-18) = 8 + 40 - 48 = \mathbf{0}.$$ (Explicit dependency: $\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 = (6, 4, 2) = 2\mathbf{v}_3$.)

• (b) Linearly Independent: $$\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -4 & 7 \\ 2 & 5 & -8 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = 1(45+48) + 4(18+24) + 7(12-15) = 93 + 168 - 21 = \mathbf{240} \neq 0.$$
