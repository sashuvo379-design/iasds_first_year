Geometric Linear Regression (OLS as Projection)

This is where Linear Algebra becomes Statistics. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is not just a calculus minimization trick—it is the orthogonal projection of the response vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ onto the column space $\text{Col}(X) = \text{span}\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}\}$. Master both pictures, derive the normal equations $X^T X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^T\mathbf{y}$, and see why $R^2 = \cos^2\theta$.

One Idea, Two Languages (MATH106_LR): Setting the calculus derivatives to zero ($\partial S / \partial b_j = 0$) is precisely making the residual vector perpendicular to every column of $X$ ($X^T \hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{0}$).
✓Distinguish the $\mathbb{R}^2$ Scatterplot Picture from the $\mathbb{R}^n$ Vector Picture
✓Derive & solve the Normal Equations $X^T X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^T\mathbf{y}$
✓Prove orthogonality minimizes residual length via Pythagoras
✓Decompose $\text{TSS} = \text{ESS} + \text{RSS}$ and interpret $R^2 = \cos^2\theta$

THEORY 2. Two Pictures & The Normal Equations

2.1 Scatterplot ($\mathbb{R}^2$) vs. Vector Picture ($\mathbb{R}^n$)

Scatterplot PictureVector Picture

One dot per observation ($n$ dots)One arrow per variable ($\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$)
We fit a line $\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$We project $\mathbf{y}$ onto plane $\text{Col}(X) = \text{span}\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}\}$

Key Distinction: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the projected point on the plane $\text{Col}(X)$; $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ are its coordinates in the basis $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}\}$.

2.2 The Normal Equations ($X^T X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^T\mathbf{y}$)

Stack the $n$ equations $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i$ into $\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{u}$ where $X = [\,\mathbf{1}\;\; \mathbf{x}\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}$. Requiring the residual $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{y} - X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ to be perpendicular to both columns of $X$:

$$\hat{\mathbf{u}} \perp \mathbf{1} \text{ and } \hat{\mathbf{u}} \perp \mathbf{x} \iff X^T(\mathbf{y} - X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}$$ $$\iff \mathbf{X^T X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^T\mathbf{y}} \implies \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}$$

Solving this $2\times 2$ system gives the exact calculus formulas: $$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x}.$$

Interactive Proof: Why OLS Must Be an Orthogonal Projection MATH106_LR Slide 15 · Anton Thm 6.4.1

Orthogonality is not an assumption—it is mathematically forced by minimizing distance!

Step 1: Decompose the distance from $\mathbf{y}$ to any candidate fit $\mathbf{w} \in \text{Col}(X)$
Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \in \text{Col}(X)$ satisfy $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \perp \text{Col}(X)$, and let $\mathbf{w}$ be any other vector in $\text{Col}(X)$. Write: $$\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{w} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{y} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})}_{\perp\;\text{Col}(X)} + \underbrace{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \mathbf{w})}_{\in\;\text{Col}(X)}.$$
Step 2: Apply the Pythagorean Theorem in $\mathbb{R}^n$
Because the two pieces are orthogonal, their cross-term dot product is $0$, leaving: $$\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{w}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{y} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|^2 + \|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \mathbf{w}\|^2.$$
Step 3: Conclude unique global minimum
Since $\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \mathbf{w}\|^2 \ge 0$ (with equality iff $\mathbf{w} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$), we have $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{w}\|^2 \ge \|\mathbf{y} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|^2$. Any non-perpendicular projection gives a strictly longer residual! $\blacksquare$

GOODNESS OF FIT 3. Pythagoras ($\text{TSS} = \text{ESS} + \text{RSS}$), $R^2$ & Hat Matrix $H$

3.1 Orthogonal Decomposition of Variance ($R^2 = \cos^2\theta$)

Because $\mathbf{1} \in \text{Col}(X)$, the centered fitted vector $(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1})$ lies inside $\text{Col}(X)$, so it is orthogonal to $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$:

$$\underbrace{\|\mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}\|^2}_{\text{TSS}} = \underbrace{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}\|^2}_{\text{ESS}} + \underbrace{\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}\|^2}_{\text{RSS}}$$ $$R^2 = \frac{\text{ESS}}{\text{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}}{\text{TSS}} = \cos^2\theta$$

Here $\theta$ is the angle between the centered vectors $\mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{x} - \bar{x}\mathbf{1}$. A good fit ($R^2 \approx 1$) means $\mathbf{y}$ lies almost flat inside $\text{Col}(X)$!

3.2 The Projection (Hat) Matrix $H = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T$

Substituting $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^T X)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{y}$ into $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ reveals the Hat Matrix $H$ (it puts the hat on $\mathbf{y}$!):

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = H\mathbf{y}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}} = (I_n - H)\mathbf{y}, \qquad H = X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T$$
  • Symmetric & Idempotent: $H^T = H$ and $H^2 = H$ (same for $I_n - H$!).
  • Degrees of Freedom (Link to Lesson 7!): $\text{rank}(H) = \text{tr}(H) = k$ ($=2$ in simple regression) and $\text{rank}(I_n - H) = \text{tr}(I_n - H) = n - k$.

Problem 1: Complete $\mathbb{R}^3$ Geometric Regression & $R^2$ MATH106_LR Slides 17–24

Given three students with hours studied $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3)^T$ and exam scores $\mathbf{y} = (7, 9, 17)^T$: (a) Set up and solve $X^T X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^T\mathbf{y}$; (b) Compute $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$, and verify $\hat{\mathbf{u}} \perp \text{Col}(X)$; (c) Compute TSS, ESS, RSS, and $R^2$.

Solution:
(a) $X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \implies X^T X = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$, $X^T\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 33 \\ 76 \end{bmatrix}$. Solving $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \hat{\beta}_0 \\ \hat{\beta}_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 33 \\ 76 \end{bmatrix}$ gives $\mathbf{\hat{\beta}_0 = 1,\; \hat{\beta}_1 = 5}$.
(b) $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = 1\cdot\mathbf{1} + 5\cdot\mathbf{x} = (6, 11, 16)^T$ and $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = (1, -2, 1)^T$.
Orthogonality: $\hat{\mathbf{u}}^T\mathbf{1} = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$ and $\hat{\mathbf{u}}^T\mathbf{x} = 1 - 4 + 3 = 0$. ✓
(c) Here $\bar{y} = 11$: $\text{TSS} = (-4)^2+(-2)^2+6^2 = 56$, $\text{RSS} = 1^2+(-2)^2+1^2 = 6$, $\text{ESS} = (-5)^2+0^2+5^2 = 50$. Check: $56 = 50 + 6$ and $R^2 = \frac{50}{56} \approx \mathbf{0.893}$.
Problem 2: Why the Sample Mean $\bar{y}$ Is an Orthogonal Projection! Strang 4.3 Q12 (Statistics Foundation)

Project $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)^T$ onto the line through $\mathbf{1} = (1, \dots, 1)^T$ by solving the normal equation $\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{1}\,\hat{c} = \mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{y}$. Show that $\hat{c} = \bar{y}$ and that $\|\mathbf{y} - \hat{c}\mathbf{1}\|^2 = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2$.

Solution:
1. Compute the scalar products: $\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{1} = 1^2 + \dots + 1^2 = n$ and $\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{y} = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i$.
2. Normal equation: $n\hat{c} = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i \implies \mathbf{\hat{c} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \bar{y}}$.
3. Error vector: $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}$, whose squared length is $\|\mathbf{e}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = n s^2$ (the numerator of sample variance!).
Takeaway: The sample mean $\bar{y}$ is the constant that minimizes the sum of squared deviations!
Problem 3: Invertibility Condition for $X^T X$ Anton 6.5 Q7 · MATH106_LR Slide 8

Prove that the $n\times 2$ design matrix $X = [\,\mathbf{1}\;\; \mathbf{x}\,]$ has linearly independent columns (and hence $X^T X$ is invertible) if and only if at least two values among $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ are distinct.

Solution:
Two nonzero vectors $\mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ are linearly dependent $\iff \mathbf{x}$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{1}$, i.e., $x_1 = x_2 = \dots = x_n = c$ (all $x_i$ are identical, so $S_{xx} = 0$).
Conversely, if at least two $x_i$ are distinct, $\mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ are linearly independent ($\text{rank}(X) = 2$).
Direct determinant check: $$\det(X^T X) = \begin{vmatrix} n & \sum x_i \\ \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2 \end{vmatrix} = n\sum x_i^2 - \left(\sum x_i\right)^2 = n\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = n S_{xx} > 0.$$ Hence $X^T X$ is invertible iff $S_{xx} > 0$ (at least two distinct $x_i$). $\blacksquare$
Problem 4: Lecture 5-Observation Study Hours vs. Exam Score MATH106_LR Slides 4–10

For the five students with hours studied $x = (2, 3, 5, 7, 8)$ and scores $y = (56, 58, 72, 78, 86)$, we have $\bar{x} = 5$, $\bar{y} = 70$, $S_{xy} = 130$, and $S_{xx} = 26$. Find the OLS regression line $\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$.

Quick check — enter $\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1$:
Solution:
$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{130}{26} = \mathbf{5}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x} = 70 - 5(5) = \mathbf{45}.$$ Fitted line: $\mathbf{\hat{y} = 45 + 5x}$, with residuals $e = (+1, -2, +2, -2, +1)$ summing to $0$.
