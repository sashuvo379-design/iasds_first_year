Linear Systems, Elimination & $LU$ Factorization

Solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is the central problem of Linear Algebra. Here you will connect the Row Picture (intersecting lines/planes) and the Column Picture (linear combinations), master Gaussian & Gauss-Jordan elimination (REF/RREF), diagnose consistency with the Rouché-Capelli theorem, and factor $A = LU$ to solve multiple right-hand sides cheaply.

Two Lenses on $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: In the Row Picture, each equation is a line (or plane) and the solution is their intersection. In the Column Picture, we ask: what combination of the columns of $A$ builds $\mathbf{b}$?

THEORY 2. Gaussian Elimination, REF vs. RREF & Consistency

2.1 Elementary Row Operations & Echelon Forms

Three legal row operations preserve the exact solution set of $[A|\mathbf{b}]$: (1) Swap two rows ($R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$), (2) Scale a row by $k \neq 0$, (3) Add a multiple of one row to another ($R_i \leftarrow R_i + kR_j$).

REF (Gaussian): Leading 1's step strictly right; zeros below pivots. $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & * & * \\ 0 & 1 & * \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ RREF (Gauss-Jordan): Zeros both above and below every leading 1. $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Practical Slide Tip: When a system has infinitely many solutions, push all the way to RREF—reading off the free variables and parametric solution becomes automatic without sign errors!

2.2 Rouché-Capelli Theorem & Homogeneous Systems

For an $m \times n$ system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ (with $m$ equations, $n$ unknowns):

  • Inconsistent (No Solution): $\text{rank}(A) < \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}])$ — RREF has a contradiction row $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & | & c \end{bmatrix}$ with $c \neq 0$.
  • Unique Solution: $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}]) = n$ (no free variables).
  • Infinitely Many Solutions: $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}]) = r < n$ (exactly $n - r$ free parameters).
Homogeneous Systems ($A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$):
• Always consistent (the trivial solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ always works).
• Has nontrivial solutions $\iff \text{rank}(A) < n$ (for square $A$: $\det(A) = 0$).
• If $n > m$ (more unknowns than equations), a homogeneous system always has infinitely many solutions!

FACTORIZATION 3. Elimination = Factorization ($A = LU$)

What is $A = LU$?

When elimination proceeds without row swaps, it factors $A$ into:

  • $U$: Upper triangular matrix (the echelon form after forward elimination).
  • $L$: Unit lower triangular matrix ($1$'s on diagonal) storing the multipliers $\ell_{ij}$ used to zero out entry $(i, j)$ via $R_i \leftarrow R_i - \ell_{ij}R_j$.

Why Factorize? (The Two-Step Triangular Solve)

To solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ (especially for many right-hand sides $\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \dots$):

$$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \iff L(U\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b} \implies \begin{cases} \text{1. Solve } L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b} \text{ (forward sub.)} \\ \text{2. Solve } U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} \text{ (back sub.)} \end{cases}$$
Interactive Walkthrough: Lecture $3\times 3$ $LU$ Factorization

Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$ into $L$ and $U$ step-by-step.

Step 1: Eliminate below Pivot 1 ($a_{11} = 2$)
Operations: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - \mathbf{2}R_1$ ($\ell_{21} = 2$) and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \mathbf{4}R_1$ ($\ell_{31} = 4$). $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad L \text{ so far} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{4} & * & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 2: Eliminate below Pivot 2 ($u_{22} = 1$)
Operation: $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \mathbf{3}R_2$ ($\ell_{32} = 3$). $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{4} & \mathbf{3} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 3: Verify $LU = A$ (The multipliers rebuild $A$!)
$$LU = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = A. \quad \checkmark$$

Problem 1: Asterisk (`*`) Augmented Matrix Classification 1st Incourse 2025 Q2(a) (3 Marks)

In each augmented matrix, `*` represents an unspecified real number. Classify each system as Consistent & Unique, Infinitely Many, Inconsistent, or Inconclusive:

(a) $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & * & * & | & * \\ 0 & 1 & * & | & * \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & | & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
(b) $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & * & * & | & * \\ 0 & 1 & * & | & * \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & | & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
(c) $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & * & * & | & * \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & | & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & | & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Explanation:
• (a) Consistent & Unique: In REF with 3 pivots for 3 unknowns ($z = 1$, back-substitution uniquely determines $y$ and $x$).
• (b) Inconsistent: The last row reads $0x + 0y + 0z = 1$, a contradiction.
• (c) Inconclusive: Rows 2 & 3 both force $x = 1$. Substituting into Row 1 gives $1 + a y + b z = c \implies ay + bz = c - 1$. If $a=b=0$ and $c \neq 1$, it's inconsistent; otherwise it has infinitely many solutions. Not enough info to decide!
Problem 2: Parameter $k$ for Consistency (Famous Trap!) 1st Incourse 2025 Q1(b) (2 Marks)

Find all values of $k$ for which the augmented matrix $\begin{bmatrix} k & 1 & | & -2 \\ 4 & -1 & | & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ corresponds to a consistent linear system.

Hint: Add Row 2 to Row 1! Don't stop at $\det(A) = 0$—actually plug in $k = -4$ to see whether the lines are parallel or coincident!
Solution:
Perform $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + R_2$: $$\begin{bmatrix} k & 1 & | & -2 \\ 4 & -1 & | & 2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 + R_2} \begin{bmatrix} k+4 & 0 & | & 0 \\ 4 & -1 & | & 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$ • Case 1 ($k \neq -4$): $x = 0$ and $y = -2$ (unique solution, consistent).
• Case 2 ($k = -4$): Row 1 becomes $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (no contradiction!), leaving $4x - y = 2$ (infinitely many solutions, also consistent!).
Conclusion: The system is consistent for all real values of $k \in \mathbb{R}$.
Problem 3: Nonlinear Trigonometric System Final 2025 Q4(b) · 1st Incourse 2020 (5 Marks)

Solve for the unknown angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ where $0 \le \alpha \le 2\pi$, $0 \le \beta \le 2\pi$, and $0 \le \gamma \le \pi$:

$$\begin{cases} 2\sin\alpha - \cos\beta + 3\tan\gamma = 3 \\ 4\sin\alpha + 2\cos\beta - 2\tan\gamma = 2 \\ 6\sin\alpha - 3\cos\beta + \tan\gamma = 9 \end{cases}$$
Hint: Substitute $u = \sin\alpha$, $v = \cos\beta$, $w = \tan\gamma$ to get a standard $3\times 3$ linear system, solve for $(u, v, w)$, then invert each trig function within its given domain!
Solution:
1. Let $u = \sin\alpha$, $v = \cos\beta$, $w = \tan\gamma$. Row-reducing $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 & | & 3 \\ 4 & 2 & -2 & | & 2 \\ 6 & -3 & 1 & | & 9 \end{bmatrix}$:
• $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 3R_1$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 & | & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & -8 & | & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & -8 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
2. Back-substitution:
• $-8w = 0 \implies w = 0$.
• $4v - 8(0) = -4 \implies v = -1$.
• $2u - (-1) + 0 = 3 \implies 2u = 2 \implies u = 1$.
3. Convert back to angles on the specified intervals:
• $\sin\alpha = 1$ on $[0, 2\pi] \implies \mathbf{\alpha = \frac{\pi}{2}}$.
• $\cos\beta = -1$ on $[0, 2\pi] \implies \mathbf{\beta = \pi}$.
• $\tan\gamma = 0$ on $[0, \pi] \implies \mathbf{\gamma = 0 \text{ or } \gamma = \pi}$.
Problem 4: Conditions on $b_1, b_2, b_3$ for Consistency MISC Slides Example 8

Determine the conditions on $b_1, b_2, b_3$, if any, so that the system $a - 2b - c = b_1$, $-4a + 5b + 2c = b_2$, $-4a + 7b + 4c = b_3$ is consistent.

Solution:
Row-reducing $[A|\mathbf{b}]$: $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 & | & b_1 \\ -4 & 5 & 2 & | & b_2 \\ -4 & 7 & 4 & | & b_3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2+4R_1,\,R_3+4R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 & | & b_1 \\ 0 & -3 & -2 & | & b_2+4b_1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 & | & b_3+4b_1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3,\, R_3-3R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 & | & b_1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 & | & b_3+4b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 & | & b_2-8b_1-3b_3 \end{bmatrix}.$$ Since there are 3 nonzero pivots ($1, -1, -2$), $\det(A) = -2 \neq 0$ and $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}]) = 3$ for every $\mathbf{b}$.
Answer: No condition on $b_1, b_2, b_3$ is needed; the system is always consistent with a unique solution.
Problem 5: Solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ via $LU$ Factorization Lecture Slides Worked Example

Solve $\begin{cases} 2x_1 + x_2 = 5 \\ 8x_1 + 7x_2 = 23 \end{cases}$ by finding $A = LU$ and solving $L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$ followed by $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$.

Solution:
1. Factor $A = LU$: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 4R_1$ gives $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
2. Forward substitution ($L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$): $$y_1 = 5, \qquad 4y_1 + y_2 = 23 \implies y_2 = 23 - 20 = 3 \implies \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}.$$ 3. Back substitution ($U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$): $$3x_2 = 3 \implies x_2 = 1, \qquad 2x_1 + x_2 = 5 \implies x_1 = 2 \implies \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
