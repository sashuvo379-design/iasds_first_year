Determinants, Adjoint, Inverses & Cramer's Rule

One number—the determinant—decides whether a matrix is invertible, measures how it scales area or volume, powers the explicit Adjoint inverse formula, and drives Cramer's Rule. Master both its algebraic rules and its geometric meaning, then drill high-frequency DU exam proofs and row-reduction determinants.

Geometric Anchor: $|\det(A)|$ is the area/volume scaling factor of the transformation $A$, and its sign tells whether orientation is preserved ($+$) or flipped ($-$). When $\det(A) = 0$, space collapses to a lower dimension, information is lost, and no inverse can undo it.

THEORY 2. Cofactor Expansion & Determinant Properties

2.1 Minors, Cofactors & Expansion

Delete row $i$ and column $j$ of $A$ to get the minor $M_{ij}$. Attach the checkerboard sign to get the cofactor:

$$C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}, \qquad \text{Signs: } \begin{bmatrix} + & - & + \\ - & + & - \\ + & - & + \end{bmatrix}$$ $$\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} C_{ij} \quad (\text{along any row } i \text{ or column } j)$$
  • Pro tip: Always expand along the row or column with the most zeros.
  • Triangular / Diagonal matrices: $\det(A) = a_{11}a_{22}\cdots a_{nn}$ (product of main diagonal!).
  • Alien Cofactor Theorem: Multiplying row $i$ by cofactors of a different row $k \neq i$ always sums to $0$.

2.2 Row Operations & Algebraic Rules

Row (or Column) Operation Effects:

  • Swap two rows/cols: $\det$ flips sign ($\times -1$).
  • Scale one row/col by $k$: $\det$ is multiplied by $k$.
  • Add multiple of one row/col to another: $\det$ is unchanged!
  • Zero row / identical or proportional rows: $\det(A) = 0$.
$$\det(I) = 1, \quad \det(A^T) = \det(A), \quad \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$$ $$\det(kA) = k^n \det(A) \;\;(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}), \qquad \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$$
Warning: $\det(A + B) \neq \det(A) + \det(B)$ in general! (Example: $\det(I + I) = \det(2I) = 2^n \neq 1 + 1$).

INVERSES 3. Adjoint Formula, Gauss-Jordan & Cramer's Rule

Method 1: Adjoint Formula

The adjoint (adjugate) is the transpose of the cofactor matrix: $\text{adj}(A) = C^T$.

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\,\text{adj}(A)$$

Great for $2\times 2$ ($\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$) and symbolic proofs.

Method 2: Gauss-Jordan

Adjoin $I_n$ to $A$ and row-reduce the left half to $I_n$:

$$[\,A \mid I\,] \xrightarrow{\text{row ops}} [\,I \mid A^{-1}\,]$$

Best numerical method for $3\times 3$ and larger matrices. If a zero row appears on the left, $A$ is singular.

Cramer's Rule ($A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$)

When $\det(A) \neq 0$, replace column $j$ of $A$ with $\mathbf{b}$ to form $A_j$:

$$x_j = \frac{\det(A_j)}{\det(A)}, \quad j=1,\dots,n$$

Ideal when an exam asks for just one variable (e.g., solve for $y$ only) or small $3\times 3$ systems.

Interactive Exam Proof: $A^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{\det(A)}\,\text{adj}(A)$ DU Final 2023 Q4(d) · Final 2025 Q4(c)

Key inverse identities also tested: Uniqueness ($B=BA C = C$) and Reverse Product Rule ($(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$).

Step 1: Multiply $A$ by $\text{adj}(A) = C^T$ entry-by-entry
The $(i, k)$ entry of $A\,\text{adj}(A)$ is the dot product of Row $i$ of $A$ with Column $k$ of $C^T$ (which is Row $k$ of the cofactor matrix $C$): $$\big(A\,\text{adj}(A)\big)_{ik} = a_{i1}C_{k1} + a_{i2}C_{k2} + \cdots + a_{in}C_{kn}.$$
Step 2: Split into Diagonal ($i = k$) vs. Off-Diagonal ($i \neq k$) cases
• When $i = k$: this is the exact cofactor expansion of $\det(A)$ along Row $i$, so the entry equals $\mathbf{\det(A)}$.
• When $i \neq k$: this is the expansion of a matrix with two identical rows (Row $i$ copied into Row $k$), so the entry equals $\mathbf{0}$.
Step 3: Assemble the matrix identity
$$A\,\text{adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} \det(A) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \det(A) & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \det(A) \end{bmatrix} = \det(A)\,I_n.$$
Step 4: Divide by $\det(A) \neq 0$ (valid since $A$ is invertible) and left-multiply by $A^{-1}$
$$A\left(\frac{1}{\det(A)}\,\text{adj}(A)\right) = I_n \implies \mathbf{A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\,\text{adj}(A)}. \quad \blacksquare$$

Problem 1: Nontrivial Solution iff $\alpha = \beta$ 1st Incourse 2025 Q3 (4 Marks)

By examining the determinant of the coefficient matrix, show that the homogeneous system below has a nontrivial solution if and only if $\alpha = \beta$:

$$\begin{cases} x + y + \alpha z = 0 \\ x + y + \beta z = 0 \\ \alpha x + \beta y + z = 0 \end{cases}$$
Hint: Subtract Row 1 from Row 2 ($R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$) to immediately create two zeros in Row 2, then expand along Row 2!
Solution:
1. Form the determinant of the coefficient matrix $A$ and apply $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$: $$\det(A) = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & \alpha \\ 1 & 1 & \beta \\ \alpha & \beta & 1 \end{vmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - R_1} \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & \alpha \\ 0 & 0 & \beta - \alpha \\ \alpha & \beta & 1 \end{vmatrix}.$$ 2. Expand along Row 2 (note position $(2,3)$ has sign $(-1)^{2+3} = -1$): $$\det(A) = -(\beta - \alpha)\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ \alpha & \beta \end{vmatrix} = -(\beta - \alpha)(\beta - \alpha) = -(\alpha - \beta)^2.$$ 3. A homogeneous $n\times n$ system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ has a nontrivial solution $\iff \det(A) = 0$: $$-(\alpha - \beta)^2 = 0 \iff \mathbf{\alpha = \beta}. \quad \blacksquare$$
Problem 2: Symbolic Determinant by Row Reduction 1st Incourse 2025 Q5 (4 Marks)

Use row reduction to show that:

$$\begin{vmatrix} 1 & a & a^2 - bc \\ 1 & b & b^2 - ca \\ 1 & c & c^2 - ab \end{vmatrix} = 0$$
Hint: Apply $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1$. Then factor $(b - a)$ out of Row 2 and $(c - a)$ out of Row 3!
Solution:
1. Perform $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1$ (leaves $\det$ unchanged): $$\begin{vmatrix} 1 & a & a^2 - bc \\ 0 & b - a & (b^2 - a^2) + c(b - a) \\ 0 & c - a & (c^2 - a^2) + b(c - a) \end{vmatrix}.$$ 2. Notice that $(b^2 - a^2) + c(b - a) = (b - a)(b + a + c)$ and $(c^2 - a^2) + b(c - a) = (c - a)(c + a + b)$.
3. Factor $(b - a)$ from Row 2 and $(c - a)$ from Row 3: $$(b - a)(c - a) \begin{vmatrix} 1 & a & a^2 - bc \\ 0 & 1 & a + b + c \\ 0 & 1 & a + b + c \end{vmatrix}.$$ 4. Since Row 2 and Row 3 are identical, the determinant is $\mathbf{0}$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 3: Solving a $3\times 3$ System by Cramer's Rule 1st Incourse 2024 Q2 (5 Marks)

Solve the following system using Cramer's Rule:

$$\begin{cases} 4x + 5y = 2 \\ 11x + y + 2z = 3 \\ x + 5y + 2z = 1 \end{cases}$$
Solution:
1. $\det(A) = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 5 & 0 \\ 11 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 4(2 - 10) - 5(22 - 2) + 0 = -32 - 100 = \mathbf{-132} \neq 0$.
2. $\det(A_1) = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2(2 - 10) - 5(6 - 2) = -16 - 20 = \mathbf{-36}$.
3. $\det(A_2) = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 2 & 0 \\ 11 & 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 4(6 - 2) - 2(22 - 2) = 16 - 40 = \mathbf{-24}$.
4. $\det(A_3) = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 5 & 2 \\ 11 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 5 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 4(1 - 15) - 5(11 - 3) + 2(55 - 1) = -56 - 40 + 108 = \mathbf{12}$.
5. Solution: $x = \dfrac{-36}{-132} = \mathbf{\dfrac{3}{11}}, \quad y = \dfrac{-24}{-132} = \mathbf{\dfrac{2}{11}}, \quad z = \dfrac{12}{-132} = \mathbf{-\dfrac{1}{11}}.$
Problem 4: Proving Inverse Uniqueness & $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ Final 2025 Q4(a) · Anton Thm 1.4.4 & 1.4.6

(a) Show that the inverse of an invertible matrix $A$ is unique.
(b) If $A$ and $B$ are invertible $n\times n$ matrices, show that $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

Solution:
(a) Uniqueness: Suppose $B$ and $C$ are both inverses of $A$, so $BA = I$ and $AC = I$. Then: $$B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = IC = C.$$ Hence the inverse is unique. $\blacksquare$

(b) Product Rule: Multiply $AB$ on both sides by $B^{-1}A^{-1}$: $$(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I,$$ $$(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I.$$ By uniqueness of the inverse, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 5: Determinant Scaling Drill ($4\times 4$ Matrix) Anton 2.3 Q34 · Strang 5.1 Q1

Suppose $A$ is a $4\times 4$ matrix with $\det(A) = -2$. Find $\det(2A^T)$.

Your answer for $\det(2A^T)$:
Solution:
Since $A$ is $4\times 4$ ($n = 4$): $$\det(2A^T) = 2^4 \det(A^T) = 16 \det(A) = 16(-2) = \mathbf{-32}.$$ Bonus checks for the same $4\times 4$ matrix: $\det(-A) = (-1)^4(-2) = -2$; $\det(A^{-1}) = -\frac{1}{2}$; $\det(A^3) = (-2)^3 = -8$.
