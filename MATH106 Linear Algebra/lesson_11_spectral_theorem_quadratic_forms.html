Spectral Theorem & Quadratic Forms

Real symmetric matrices ($A = A^T$) are the crown jewels of Linear Algebra: they always have real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, and decompose cleanly as $A = PDP^T$. That single fact powers the Principal Axes Theorem, eliminating cross-product terms from quadratic forms $Q = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ and driving PCA, covariance matrices, and multivariable optimization.

Core Geometric Insight (MATH106_quadraticform): Cross-product terms like $4x_1 x_2$ simply mean a conic or surface is tilted away from the coordinate axes. An orthogonal change of variable $\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{y}$ rotates your axes onto the eigenvectors so $Q = \sum \lambda_i y_i^2$!

✓Classify definiteness via Eigenvalue Test & Leading Principal Minors

THEOREM 2. The Spectral Theorem ($A = PDP^T$)

2.1 Three Equivalent Statements

For a real $n\times n$ matrix $A$, the following are equivalent:

(i) $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable ($P^T A P = D$ with $P^T = P^{-1}$)
(ii) $A$ has an orthonormal set of $n$ eigenvectors
(iii) $A$ is symmetric ($A = A^T$)
  • Every real symmetric matrix has all real eigenvalues and is never defective ($GM = AM$ always!).
  • Eigenvectors from different eigenspaces are automatically orthogonal.
  • Spectral Decomposition: $A = PDP^T = \lambda_1\mathbf{p}_1\mathbf{p}_1^T + \dots + \lambda_n\mathbf{p}_n\mathbf{p}_n^T$.

2.2 3-Step Algorithm: Orthogonal Diagonalization

  1. Find a basis for each eigenspace of $A$ by solving $(\lambda I - A)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
  2. Apply Gram-Schmidt within each eigenspace separately (if $\dim E_\lambda \ge 2$, orthogonalize its basis; if $\dim E_\lambda = 1$, just normalize to unit length!).
  3. Assemble $P = [\mathbf{p}_1 \cdots \mathbf{p}_n]$: Then $P^T A P = D = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ in matching order.
Why we don't need Gram-Schmidt across different eigenvalues: Cross-eigenspace orthogonality is guaranteed automatically for symmetric $A$!
Interactive Proofs: Symmetry Necessity & Automatic Cross-Eigenspace Orthogonality MATH106_EV_3 Slide 10 · Anton Thm 7.2.1
Proof 1: If $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable ($P^T A P = D$), then $A$ MUST be symmetric
Multiply by $P$ on the left and $P^T$ on the right: $A = PDP^T$. Taking the transpose (since $D^T = D$): $$A^T = (PDP^T)^T = (P^T)^T D^T P^T = PDP^T = A.$$
Proof 2: Distinct eigenvalues of symmetric $A$ give orthogonal eigenvectors
Suppose $A\mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1$ and $A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2$ with $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$. Compute $\mathbf{v}_1^T A \mathbf{v}_2$ two ways: $$\mathbf{v}_1^T (A\mathbf{v}_2) = \lambda_2 (\mathbf{v}_1^T\mathbf{v}_2), \qquad (A^T\mathbf{v}_1)^T\mathbf{v}_2 = (A\mathbf{v}_1)^T\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_1 (\mathbf{v}_1^T\mathbf{v}_2).$$
Subtract the two expressions
$$(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)(\mathbf{v}_1^T\mathbf{v}_2) = 0.$$ Since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, we must have $\mathbf{v}_1^T\mathbf{v}_2 = 0$, i.e., $\mathbf{v}_1 \perp \mathbf{v}_2$. $\blacksquare$

QUADRATIC FORMS 3. Principal Axes Theorem & Definiteness Classification

3.1 Reading Off Symmetric $A$ & Eliminating Cross Terms

Every quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ has a unique symmetric matrix $A$: diagonal entries = coefficients of $x_i^2$; off-diagonal entries = half the coefficients of $x_i x_j$. Setting $\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{y}$ (where $P$ orthogonally diagonalizes $A$) gives:

$$Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T (P^T A P)\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^T D \mathbf{y} = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2$$

Range Preservation: Because $P$ is invertible (an orthogonal rotation), the set of values attained by $Q$ is completely unchanged—so sign behavior, maxima, and minima can be read directly off the eigenvalues!

Classification Definition ($\forall \mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}$) Eigenvalue Test (Thm 7.3.2) Leading Principal Minors $\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \dots, \Delta_n$
Positive Definite (PD) $\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x} > 0$ All $\lambda_i > 0$ $+, +, +, \dots$ (all strictly positive)
Negative Definite (ND) $\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x} < 0$ All $\lambda_i < 0$ $-, +, -, +, \dots$ (strictly alternating, starting $-$)
Positive Semidefinite (PSD) $\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x} \ge 0$ All $\lambda_i \ge 0$ (at least one $0$) All principal minors $\ge 0$ (singular, $\det A = 0$)
Negative Semidefinite (NSD) $\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x} \le 0$ All $\lambda_i \le 0$ (at least one $0$) Nonpositive/nonnegative alternating, $\det A = 0$
Indefinite Takes both $+$ and $-$ values Mixed signs (at least one $+$ & one $-$) Neither PD nor ND (e.g., both $+$ and $-$ appear)
Classic Exam Trap (Slide 22): The signs of the entries of $A$ tell you nothing about definiteness! For example, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has all entries $\ge 0$, yet its eigenvalues are $4, 1, -2$ (indefinite)!

Problem 1: Orthogonal Change of Variables Eliminating Cross Terms Final 2025 Q2(c) (6 Marks) · QF Slides Ex. 2

Find an orthogonal change of variables that eliminates the cross-product terms in the quadratic form $Q = x_1^2 - x_3^2 - 4x_1 x_2 + 4x_2 x_3$, and express $Q$ in terms of the new variables $y_1, y_2, y_3$.

Hint: Build symmetric $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its characteristic polynomial factors cleanly as $\lambda(\lambda+3)(\lambda-3) = 0$!
Solution:
1. Symmetric matrix: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.
2. Eigenvalues: $\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^3 - 9\lambda = \lambda(\lambda + 3)(\lambda - 3) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 0,\; \lambda_2 = -3,\; \lambda_3 = 3$.
3. Unit eigenvectors (automatically orthogonal because eigenvalues are distinct!): $$\mathbf{p}_1 = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{p}_2 = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{p}_3 = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$ 4. Substitution $\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{y}$ with $P = [\mathbf{p}_1\;\mathbf{p}_2\;\mathbf{p}_3]$ eliminates all cross terms: $$Q = \mathbf{y}^T(P^T A P)\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{-3y_2^2 + 3y_3^2} \quad (\text{indefinite}).$$
Problem 2: Matrix Form of a General Quadratic Equation Final 2025 Q2(a) (3 Marks)

Express the quadratic equation $y^2 + 7x - 8y - 5 = 0$ in the matrix form $\mathbf{x}' A \mathbf{x} + K\mathbf{x} + f = 0$.

Solution:
Let $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$. Here the $x^2$ and $xy$ terms have coefficient $0$, and the $y^2$ term has coefficient $1$: $$\begin{bmatrix} x & y \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -8 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} + (-5) = 0.$$ So $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $K = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -8 \end{bmatrix}$, and $f = -5$.
Problem 3: Orthogonal Diagonalization with Gram-Schmidt Inside $E_2$ MATH106_EV_3 Slides 13–16

Find an orthogonal matrix $P$ that orthogonally diagonalizes the symmetric matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Hint: Eigenvalues are $\lambda = 2, 2, 8$. Inside the 2D eigenspace $E_2$ ($x_1+x_2+x_3=0$), the raw basis $\{(-1,1,0)^T, (-1,0,1)^T\}$ is NOT orthogonal—run Gram-Schmidt on them!
Solution:
1. $\det(\lambda I - A) = (\lambda - 2)^2(\lambda - 8) = 0 \implies \lambda = 2, 2, 8$.
2. For $\lambda = 2$: $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0 \implies$ raw basis $\mathbf{u}_1 = (-1, 1, 0)^T$, $\mathbf{u}_2 = (-1, 0, 1)^T$.
Apply Gram-Schmidt inside $E_2$: $$\mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \mathbf{u}_2 - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \implies \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$ 3. For $\lambda = 8$: $x_1 = x_2 = x_3 \implies \mathbf{v}_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)^T$ (already orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$!).
4. Assemble $P = [\mathbf{v}_1\;\mathbf{v}_2\;\mathbf{v}_3]$: then $P^T A P = \text{diag}(2, 2, 8)$. ✓
Problem 4: Matrix Representation & Classification via Principal Minors QF Slides Practice Q1

Write $Q = 5x^2 - 4xy + 8y^2$ in the form $\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x}$, find its leading principal minors $\Delta_1, \Delta_2$, and classify its definiteness.

Quick check — enter $\Delta_1, \Delta_2$:
Solution:
1. Symmetric matrix: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ -2 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$ (off-diagonal $= \frac{1}{2}(-4) = -2$).
2. Leading principal minors: $\Delta_1 = 5 > 0$ and $\Delta_2 = \det(A) = 40 - 4 = \mathbf{36} > 0$.
3. Pattern $+, + \implies$ Positive Definite! (Eigenvalues are $\lambda = 4, 9$, so $Q = 4y_1^2 + 9y_2^2$.)
