Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, Diagonalization & Cayley-Hamilton

Out of all directions in space, eigenvectors are the special axes that a matrix only stretches or flips—never rotates. Master finding characteristic polynomials, comparing Algebraic vs. Geometric multiplicity, diagonalizing $A = PDP^{-1}$ to compute huge matrix powers $A^k$, and applying the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem to invert matrices using only multiplication and addition.

Core Intuition (MATH106_EV): Eigenspaces are the “grain” of a linear transformation. Diagonalization ($A = PDP^{-1}$) means switching into eigen-coordinates where $A$ acts as pure, uncoupled scaling along each axis!

THEORY 2. Characteristic Equation, Multiplicity & Free Shortcuts

2.1 Finding Eigenpairs & Instant Sanity Checks

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \;\;(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}) \iff (A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \iff \det(\lambda I - A) = 0$$
  • Trace & Determinant Checks: $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \text{tr}(A)$ and $\prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \det(A)$.
  • Constant Term Shortcut: If $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^n + c_1\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_n$, then $\mathbf{\det(A) = (-1)^n c_n}$!
  • Triangular & Block Diagonal Matrices: Eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries; for block diagonal $\text{diag}(B_1, B_2)$, $p(\lambda) = p_{B_1}(\lambda)\,p_{B_2}(\lambda)$.

2.2 Multiplicity ($AM$ vs. $GM$) & Complex Eigenvalues

$$1 \le \underbrace{\dim E_\lambda}_{GM(\lambda)} \le \underbrace{\text{root multiplicity}}_{AM(\lambda)}$$
  • Semisimple ($GM = AM$): Enough eigenvectors exist for $\lambda$.
  • Defective ($GM < AM$): Missing eigenvectors (like shear $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$: $AM(2)=2$, $GM(2)=1$). Not diagonalizable!
  • Complex Conjugate Pairs ($a \pm bi$): No real invariant line—instead an invariant plane with rotation $\theta = \arg(a+bi)$ and scaling $r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
Interactive Exam Proofs: Eigenvalues of $A^k$ & $\lambda = 0$ Invertibility MATH106_EV_3 Slides 2–7
Theorem 1 (Induction): If $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}$, then $A^k\mathbf{x} = \lambda^k\mathbf{x}$ for all $k \ge 1$
Base case ($k=1$): $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}$ holds by hypothesis.
Inductive step: Assume $A^k\mathbf{x} = \lambda^k\mathbf{x}$. Multiply both sides by $A$: $$A^{k+1}\mathbf{x} = A(A^k\mathbf{x}) = A(\lambda^k\mathbf{x}) = \lambda^k(A\mathbf{x}) = \lambda^k(\lambda\mathbf{x}) = \lambda^{k+1}\mathbf{x}.$$
Watch Out! Eigenspace Merging for $A^k$
If distinct eigenvalues map to the same power (e.g., $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$ both give $\lambda^{26} = 1$), their eigenspaces merge into a single larger eigenspace!
Theorem 2: $A$ is invertible $\iff \lambda = 0$ is NOT an eigenvalue of $A$
$\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue $\iff \exists\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ with $A\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \iff N(A) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\} \iff \det(A) = 0 \iff A$ is singular. Equivalently, evaluating $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ at $\lambda = 0$ gives $c_n = \det(-A) = (-1)^n\det(A)$, so $A$ is invertible $\iff c_n \neq 0$. $\blacksquare$

POWER TOOLS 3. Diagonalization ($A = PDP^{-1}$) & Cayley-Hamilton

3.1 Diagonalization & Fast Matrix Powers

$A$ is diagonalizable $\iff GM(\lambda) = AM(\lambda)$ for every $\lambda$ $\iff$ $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors (columns of $P$).

$$A = PDP^{-1} \iff P^{-1}AP = D = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$$ $$A^k = PD^k P^{-1} = P\,\text{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \dots, \lambda_n^k)\,P^{-1}$$

Sufficient shortcut: If an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, it is guaranteed to be diagonalizable!

3.2 The Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

Theorem: Every square matrix $A$ satisfies its own characteristic equation $f(\lambda) = \lambda^n + a_1\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + a_{n-1}\lambda + a_n = 0$:

$$f(A) = A^n + a_1 A^{n-1} + \dots + a_{n-1}A + a_n I = 0$$ $$\text{Multiply by } A^{-1} \implies \mathbf{A^{-1} = -\frac{1}{a_n}\left(A^{n-1} + a_1 A^{n-2} + \dots + a_{n-1}I\right)}$$

Notice $a_n = (-1)^n \det(A)$, so dividing by $a_n$ works exactly when $A$ is invertible! Also collapses high powers like $A^4$ into $A^2, A, I$.

$\lambda < 0$ Flips eigenvector to opposite direction on the same line Reverses arrow orientation
$\lambda = 0$ Crushes entire eigenspace $E_0 = N(A)$ onto the origin $A$ is singular ($\det A = 0$)
Complex $\lambda = a \pm bi$ Rotation by $\theta = \arg(a+bi)$ + scaling by $r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ No real invariant line
Defective ($GM < AM$) Shearing component cannot be uncoupled into pure axis scaling Not diagonalizable

Problem 1: Reading $\det(A)$, $\text{tr}(A)$, Size & Invertibility 2nd Incourse 2025 Q4(a) · 2020 Q1(c) · Final 2024

(a) A $4\times 4$ matrix $A$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 1, \lambda_2 = -2, \lambda_3 = 3, \lambda_4 = -3$. Find $\det(A)$ and $\text{tr}(A)$.
(b) Another matrix $B$ has characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3)^2(\lambda - 4)^3$. What is the size of $B$? Is $B$ invertible?

Quick check for (a) — enter det(A), tr(A):
Solution:
• (a) $\det(A) = \prod \lambda_i = (1)(-2)(3)(-3) = \mathbf{18}$, and $\text{tr}(A) = \sum \lambda_i = 1 - 2 + 3 - 3 = \mathbf{-1}$.
• (b) Degree of $p(\lambda)$ is $1 + 2 + 3 = \mathbf{6}$, so $B$ is $\mathbf{6 \times 6}$. Since $\lambda = 0$ is not a root ($p(0) = (-1)(-3)^2(-4)^3 = 576 \neq 0$), $B$ is invertible.
Problem 2: Eigenvalues & Verifying $P$ Diagonalizes $A$ 2nd Incourse 2025 Q3 (5 Marks)

Find the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 4 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and show that $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ diagonalizes $A$.

Hint: To show $P$ diagonalizes $A$ quickly, verify $\det(P) \neq 0$ and check $AP = PD$ column-by-column (no need to even invert $P$)!
Solution:
1. Characteristic polynomial: $\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda - 6 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 3) = 0$. Eigenvalues: $\mathbf{\lambda_1 = 1,\; \lambda_2 = 2,\; \lambda_3 = 3}$. (Check: $\text{tr}(A) = -1+4+3 = 6 = 1+2+3$. ✓)
2. Verify $P$ is invertible: $\det(P) = 1(12-9) - 2(4-3) + 1(3-3) = 3 - 2 = 1 \neq 0$.
3. Verify $AP = PD$: $$AP = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 4 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 6 & 9 \\ 1 & 6 & 12 \end{bmatrix} = P\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = PD.$$ Hence $P^{-1}AP = \text{diag}(1, 2, 3)$, so $P$ diagonalizes $A$. ✓
Problem 3: Characteristic Equation of a $4\times 4$ Block-Diagonal Matrix Final 2025 Q5(a) (6 Marks)

Find the characteristic equation for the $4\times 4$ block-diagonal matrix: $$B = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -9 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 & -7 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Hint: For a block-diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(B_1, B_2)$, the determinant splits cleanly: $\det(\lambda I - B) = \det(\lambda I - B_1)\,\det(\lambda I - B_2)$!
Solution:
1. Top-left $2\times 2$ block $B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -9 \\ 4 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$: $\text{tr}(B_1) = 8$, $\det(B_1) = -20 + 36 = 16 \implies \lambda^2 - 8\lambda + 16 = (\lambda - 4)^2$.
2. Bottom-right $2\times 2$ block $B_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -7 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$: $\text{tr}(B_2) = 0$, $\det(B_2) = -4 + 7 = 3 \implies \lambda^2 + 3$.
3. Characteristic equation: $\mathbf{(\lambda - 4)^2(\lambda^2 + 3) = 0}$ (eigenvalues: $\lambda = 4, 4, \pm i\sqrt{3}$).
Problem 4: Using Cayley-Hamilton to Find $A^{-1}$ 2nd Incourse 2024 Q2 (4 Marks) · MATH106_EV_3 Slides 19–21

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find the characteristic equation, verify Cayley-Hamilton, and hence find $A^{-1}$.

Solution:
1. Characteristic equation: $\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^3 - 3\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 1 = 0$.
2. Cayley-Hamilton: $A^3 - 3A^2 - 5A + I = 0$.
3. Multiply by $A^{-1}$: $A^2 - 3A - 5I + A^{-1} = 0 \implies A^{-1} = -A^2 + 3A + 5I$.
4. Since $A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 6 & 4 \\ 6 & 7 & 6 \\ 5 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $$A^{-1} = -\begin{bmatrix} 9 & 6 & 4 \\ 6 & 7 & 6 \\ 5 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 6 \\ 9 & 3 & 0 \\ 3 & 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 & -6 \\ -2 & -1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.$$
Problem 5: Eigenvalues & Eigenspace Merging ($A^{25}$ vs. $A^{26}$) MATH106_EV_3 Slides 4–6 & Practice Q1

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -2 & -2 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, which has eigenvalues $\lambda = 1$ ($E_1 = \text{span}\{(-1,1,0)^T, (-1,0,1)^T\}$) and $\lambda = -1$ ($E_{-1} = \text{span}\{(2,-1,1)^T\}$). Find the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of $A^{25}$ and $A^{26}$, and explain why they differ!

Solution:
• For $A^{25}$ (odd power): $1^{25} = 1$ and $(-1)^{25} = -1$. The eigenvalues stay distinct, so no merging occurs and $E_1(A^{25}) = E_1(A)$, $E_{-1}(A^{25}) = E_{-1}(A)$.
• For $A^{26}$ (even power): Both $1^{26} = 1$ and $(-1)^{26} = 1$ map to the same eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$! Since $A$ is diagonalizable ($A = PDP^{-1}$), $A^{26} = P I_3 P^{-1} = I_3$, so the eigenspaces merge into $E_1(A^{26}) = \mathbf{\mathbb{R}^3}$!
