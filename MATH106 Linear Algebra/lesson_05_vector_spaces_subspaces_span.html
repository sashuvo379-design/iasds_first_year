Vector Spaces, Subspaces & Span

Welcome to Module 2—the conceptual leap of Linear Algebra. Here we move from individual vectors to entire spaces of vectors (including matrices $M_{mn}$, polynomials $P_n$, and functions), test when a subset is a true subspace, and connect span directly to the Column Space $C(A)$ and Null Space $N(A)$.

Strang's Golden Rule for Subspaces: A subspace must pass through the origin $\mathbf{0}$ and stay closed under all linear combinations $c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v}$. If adding or scaling ever lets you escape the set, it is not a subspace!

AXIOMS 2. Real Vector Spaces (All 10 Axioms)

A nonempty set $V$ with addition ($\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$) and scalar multiplication ($k\mathbf{u}$) is a real vector space if all 10 axioms hold for every $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in V$ and $k, m \in \mathbb{R}$:

DU Final 2025 Q3(a) (3 Marks)
A1. Closure (+): $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in V$
A2. Commutativity: $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$
A3. Associativity (+): $\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{w}$
A4. Zero Vector: $\exists\,\mathbf{0}\in V$ with $\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{u}$
A5. Additive Inverse: $\forall \mathbf{u},\; \exists\,(-\mathbf{u})\in V$ with $\mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}$
A6. Closure (·): $k\mathbf{u} \in V$
A7. Distributive I: $k(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = k\mathbf{u} + k\mathbf{v}$
A8. Distributive II: $(k+m)\mathbf{u} = k\mathbf{u} + m\mathbf{u}$
A9. Scalar Assoc.: $k(m\mathbf{u}) = (km)\mathbf{u}$
A10. Identity Scalar: $1\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}$
Euclidean Space $\mathbb{R}^n$
All $n$-tuples $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ with component-wise ops. Zero vector: $(0,\dots,0)$.
Matrix Space $M_{mn}$
All $m\times n$ real matrices. Zero vector: $0_{m\times n}$. Dimension $= mn$.
Polynomial Space $P_n$
All polynomials of degree $\le n$: $a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_n x^n$. Dimension $= n+1$.

THEORY 3. Subspaces, Span & Matrix Spaces ($C(A), N(A), C(A^T)$)

3.1 The Subspace Test (Theorem 4.2.1)

To prove a subset $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace, you don't need all 10 axioms—just 3 quick checks:

1. Nonempty: $\mathbf{0} \in W$ (quickest disqualifier!).
2. Closed under $+$: $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \implies \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W$.
3. Closed under scaling: $\mathbf{u} \in W, k \in \mathbb{R} \implies k\mathbf{u} \in W$.

All Subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$: $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, any line through $\mathbf{0}$, any plane through $\mathbf{0}$, and $\mathbb{R}^3$ itself.
Subspaces of $M_{nn}$: Symmetric matrices, skew-symmetric matrices, upper/lower triangular, and diagonal matrices. (Invertible matrices are NOT a subspace because $0$ is not invertible!)

3.2 Span & Fundamental Spaces of $A_{m\times n}$

$\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_r\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_r\mathbf{v}_r : c_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is always the smallest subspace containing those vectors.

  • Column Space $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$: Span of the columns of $A$. Key link: $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ is consistent $\iff \mathbf{b} \in C(A)$.
  • Row Space $C(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$: Span of the rows of $A$. Row operations do not change the row space.
  • Null Space $N(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$: Solution space of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Always a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$!
Watch the Ambient Dimension! For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$: $N(A)$ and Row Space live in $\mathbb{R}^n$, while $C(A)$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^m$.
Interactive Exam Proof: Intersection $W_1 \cap W_2$ is a Subspace Anton Thm 4.2.2 · Strang 3.1 Q15(c)

Contrast: The union $W_1 \cup W_2$ of two lines (like the $x$- and $y$-axes) is not a subspace because $(1,0)+(0,1)=(1,1)$ leaves both axes!

Step 1: Verify $W_1 \cap W_2$ is nonempty (contains $\mathbf{0}$)
Since $W_1$ and $W_2$ are both subspaces of $V$, we have $\mathbf{0} \in W_1$ and $\mathbf{0} \in W_2$. Therefore $\mathbf{0} \in W_1 \cap W_2$.
Step 2: Prove closure under addition
Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W_1 \cap W_2$. Then $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W_1$ and $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W_2$. Because both $W_1$ and $W_2$ are closed under addition, $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} \in W_1$ and $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} \in W_2$. Hence $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} \in W_1 \cap W_2$.
Step 3: Prove closure under scalar multiplication
Let $\mathbf{u} \in W_1 \cap W_2$ and $k \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $\mathbf{u} \in W_1$ and $\mathbf{u} \in W_2$, so $k\mathbf{u} \in W_1$ and $k\mathbf{u} \in W_2$. Hence $k\mathbf{u} \in W_1 \cap W_2$. Thus $W_1 \cap W_2$ is a subspace of $V$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 1: Trigonometric Functions in $\text{span}\{\cos^2 x, \sin^2 x\}$ 2nd Incourse 2025 Q1(a) (3 Marks)

Let $f = \cos^2 x$ and $g = \sin^2 x$. Which of the following lie in $\text{span}\{f, g\}$?
(i) $\cos 2x$ &qquad (ii) $\sin x$

Quick check:
Hint: Recall the double-angle identity for $\cos 2x$. For $\sin x$, test whether $c_1\cos^2 x + c_2\sin^2 x = \sin x$ can hold at $x = 0$, $x = \pi/2$, and $x = -\pi/2$!
Solution:
• (i) $\cos 2x \in \text{span}\{f, g\}$: By the trig identity $\cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = (1)f + (-1)g$, so $\cos 2x$ lies in the span.
• (ii) $\sin x \notin \text{span}\{f, g\}$: Suppose $c_1\cos^2 x + c_2\sin^2 x = \sin x$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  – Plug in $x = \pi/2$: $c_1(0) + c_2(1) = 1 \implies c_2 = 1$.
  – Plug in $x = -\pi/2$: $c_1(0) + c_2(1) = -1 \implies c_2 = -1$.
Contradiction ($1 = -1$)! (Equivalently, $\cos^2 x$ and $\sin^2 x$ are even functions, so any linear combination of them is even, whereas $\sin x$ is odd.) Thus $\sin x$ is not in the span.
Problem 2: Expressing a Polynomial as a Linear Combination in $P_2$ 2nd Incourse 2024 Q3 (4 Marks)

Express $p = 6 + 11x + 6x^2$ as a linear combination of $p_1 = 2 + x + 4x^2$, $p_2 = 1 - x + 3x^2$, and $p_3 = 3 + 2x + 5x^2$.

Hint: Set $c_1 p_1 + c_2 p_2 + c_3 p_3 = p$ and equate coefficients of $1$, $x$, and $x^2$ to form a $3\times 3$ linear system!
Solution:
Equating coefficients of $1, x, x^2$ in $c_1(2+x+4x^2) + c_2(1-x+3x^2) + c_3(3+2x+5x^2) = 6 + 11x + 6x^2$: $$\begin{cases} 2c_1 + c_2 + 3c_3 = 6 \\ c_1 - c_2 + 2c_3 = 11 \\ 4c_1 + 3c_2 + 5c_3 = 6 \end{cases}$$ Solving by Gaussian elimination gives $c_1 = 4,\; c_2 = -5,\; c_3 = 1$.
Answer: $p = \mathbf{4p_1 - 5p_2 + p_3}$.
Problem 3: Proving a Vector Does Not Lie in a Span ($\mathbb{R}^4$) Final 2024 Q2(c) (4 Marks)

Show that there do not exist scalars $c_1, c_2, c_3$ such that $$c_1(1, 0, 1, 0) + c_2(1, 0, -2, 1) + c_3(2, 0, 1, 2) = (1, -2, 2, 3).$$

Hint: Look closely at the second component of every vector on the left-hand side!
Solution:
Equating components yields four equations: $$\begin{cases} c_1 + c_2 + 2c_3 = 1 \\ \mathbf{0c_1 + 0c_2 + 0c_3 = -2} \\ c_1 - 2c_2 + c_3 = 2 \\ c_2 + 2c_3 = 3 \end{cases}$$ The second equation simplifies to $0 = -2$, which is impossible for any scalars $c_1, c_2, c_3$. Therefore, the system is inconsistent and $(1, -2, 2, 3)$ does not lie in the span. $\blacksquare$
Problem 4: Proving Linear Combinations Form a Subspace Anton Thm 4.2.3 · DU Core Proof

Let $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r\}$ be a nonempty set of vectors in a vector space $V$. Prove that $W = \text{span}(S)$ is a subspace of $V$.

Solution:
1. Nonempty: Setting all scalars to $0$ gives $\mathbf{0} = 0\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + 0\mathbf{v}_r \in W$.
2. Closure under $+$: Let $\mathbf{u} = \sum_{i=1}^r a_i\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^r b_i\mathbf{v}_i$ be in $W$. Then $$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^r (a_i + b_i)\mathbf{v}_i \in W.$$ 3. Closure under scalar mult.: For any $k \in \mathbb{R}$, $$k\mathbf{u} = \sum_{i=1}^r (ka_i)\mathbf{v}_i \in W.$$ By Theorem 4.2.1, $W = \text{span}(S)$ is a subspace of $V$. $\blacksquare$
