Core Theory & Theorems (Lecture 14 + Notes)

2.1 Multivariable Chain Rule & Euler's Homogeneous Theorem

Case Dependency Tree Formula
1 Independent Variable ($t$) $w(x, y)$ with $x(t), y(t)$ $$\frac{dw}{dt} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$$
2 Independent Variables ($u, v$) $w(x, y)$ with $x(u, v), y(u, v)$ $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}$$ (and similarly for $\partial w/\partial v$)
Euler's Theorem (Homogeneous) $f(tx, ty) = t^n f(x, y)$ (degree $n$) $$x f_x(x, y) + y f_y(x, y) = n f(x, y)$$

2.2 Directional Derivatives & The Gradient Vector (Lecture 14)

Partial derivatives $f_x$ and $f_y$ measure slopes only along $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$. To find the rate of change in any direction given by a unit vector $\mathbf{u} = \langle a, b\rangle = \langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta\rangle$ ($\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1$): $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(x, y) = f_x(x, y)a + f_y(x, y)b = \nabla f(x, y) \cdot \mathbf{u}$$ where the gradient vector (Definition 8) is: $$\nabla f(x, y) = \langle f_x, f_y\rangle = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\hat{j} \qquad \text{or in 3D:} \qquad \nabla f(x, y, z) = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z\rangle$$

#1 Exam Mistake — Always Normalize $\mathbf{v}$ First! If a problem gives a non-unit vector $\mathbf{v}$ (like $\mathbf{v} = 2\hat{i} + 5\hat{j}$) or two points $P \to Q$, you MUST divide by its length first: $\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}$ before taking $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$!
Theorem 15 — Maximizing the Directional Derivative (Ashraful's Note): Since $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\|\cdot 1 \cdot \cos\phi$:
  • Maximum rate of increase (Steepest Ascent): Occurs in the direction of $+\nabla f$ ($\phi = 0$), with value $+\|\nabla f\|$ (gradient-এর দিক বরাবর সবচেয়ে বেশি rate of change হবে).
  • Maximum rate of decrease (Steepest Descent): Occurs in the direction of $-\nabla f$ ($\phi = \pi$), with value $-\|\nabla f\|$.
  • Zero rate of change: Occurs perpendicular to $\nabla f$ ($\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}$), i.e., tangent to the level curve!

Lecture 14 • Example 6 (Also Ashraful's Notes)

Example 1: $f(x, y) = x e^y$ from $P(2, 0)$ to $Q(\frac{1}{2}, 2)$ & Steepest Ascent

(a) Find the rate of change of $f(x, y) = x e^y$ at $P(2, 0)$ toward $Q(\frac{1}{2}, 2)$. (b) In what direction does $f$ increase fastest, and what is the maximum rate?

Step 1 — Gradient at $P(2, 0)$: $\nabla f(x, y) = \langle e^y, x e^y\rangle \implies \nabla f(2, 0) = \langle e^0, 2e^0\rangle = \langle 1, 2\rangle$.
Step 2 — Unit Vector from $P$ to $Q$: $\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle \frac{1}{2} - 2,\; 2 - 0\rangle = \langle -\frac{3}{2}, 2\rangle$. Length: $\|\overrightarrow{PQ}\| = \sqrt{\frac{9}{4} + 4} = \frac{5}{2}$. Thus $\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\overrightarrow{PQ}}{5/2} = \left\langle -\dfrac{3}{5}, \dfrac{4}{5}\right\rangle$.
Step 3 — Directional Derivative: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, 0) = \langle 1, 2\rangle \cdot \left\langle -\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5}\right\rangle = -\frac{3}{5} + \frac{8}{5} = \mathbf{1}$.
Step 4 — Maximum Rate of Change (Thm 15): Fastest increase is in the direction of $\nabla f(2, 0) = \langle 1, 2\rangle$, with maximum rate $\|\nabla f(2, 0)\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2} = \mathbf{\sqrt{5}}$.
Lecture 14 • Example 4

Example 2: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, -1)$ for $f(x, y) = x^2 y^3 - 4y$ along $\mathbf{v} = 2\hat{i} + 5\hat{j}$

Find the directional derivative at $(2, -1)$ in the direction of $\mathbf{v} = 2\hat{i} + 5\hat{j}$.

Step 1 — Gradient at $(2, -1)$: $\nabla f(x, y) = \langle 2xy^3,\; 3x^2 y^2 - 4\rangle \implies \nabla f(2, -1) = \langle -4, 8\rangle$.
Step 2 — Normalize $\mathbf{v}$: $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{29} \implies \mathbf{u} = \left\langle \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{29}},\; \dfrac{5}{\sqrt{29}}\right\rangle$.
Step 3 — Dot Product: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, -1) = \dfrac{(-4)(2) + (8)(5)}{\sqrt{29}} = \mathbf{\dfrac{32}{\sqrt{29}}}$.

Example 3: Rate of Distance Change Between Two Orbiting Objects

Object 1 moves along $x_1 = 4\cos t, y_1 = 2\sin t$; Object 2 moves along $x_2 = 2\sin 2t, y_2 = 3\cos 2t$. At what rate is the distance $s$ between them changing at $t = \pi$?

Step 1 — Positions & Distance at $t = \pi$: $(x_1, y_1) = (-4, 0)$ and $(x_2, y_2) = (0, 3)$. Distance $s = \sqrt{(0 - (-4))^2 + (3 - 0)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$.
Step 2 — Velocities at $t = \pi$: $\frac{dx_1}{dt} = -4\sin\pi = 0$, $\frac{dy_1}{dt} = 2\cos\pi = -2$;   $\frac{dx_2}{dt} = 4\cos 2\pi = 4$, $\frac{dy_2}{dt} = -6\sin 2\pi = 0$.
Step 3 — Partial Derivatives of $s$: $\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_1} = -\frac{4}{5}$, $\frac{\partial s}{\partial y_1} = -\frac{3}{5}$, $\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_2} = \frac{4}{5}$, $\frac{\partial s}{\partial y_2} = \frac{3}{5}$.
Step 4 — Apply 4-Variable Chain Rule: $\dfrac{ds}{dt} = \left(-\frac{4}{5}\right)(0) + \left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)(-2) + \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)(4) + \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)(0) = \dfrac{6 + 16}{5} = \mathbf{\dfrac{22}{5} = 4.4}$.

Year Final Exam 2025 • Q6(b) 5 Marks

Question: Show that the function $f(x, y) = e^{x/y}$ is a homogeneous function of degree $n$ and show that $x f_x(x, y) + y f_y(x, y) = n f(x, y)$.

Hint: Replace $(x, y)$ by $(tx, ty)$ in $f(x, y)$: the $t$'s cancel inside $e^{(tx)/(ty)} = t^0 e^{x/y}$, so the degree of homogeneity is $n = 0$!
Full Solution:
1. Check Homogeneity: For any scalar $t > 0$: $$f(tx, ty) = e^{(tx)/(ty)} = e^{x/y} = t^0 f(x, y)$$ Thus $f(x, y) = e^{x/y}$ is homogeneous of degree $n = 0$.

2. Verify Euler's Theorem ($x f_x + y f_y = 0 \cdot f$):
Compute partial derivatives:
• $f_x(x, y) = \dfrac{1}{y} e^{x/y}$
• $f_y(x, y) = -\dfrac{x}{y^2} e^{x/y}$
Substitute into $x f_x + y f_y$: $$x f_x(x, y) + y f_y(x, y) = x\left(\frac{1}{y} e^{x/y}\right) + y\left(-\frac{x}{y^2} e^{x/y}\right) = \frac{x}{y}e^{x/y} - \frac{x}{y}e^{x/y} = 0 = 0 \cdot f(x, y) \quad \blacksquare$$
Lecture 14 Slide • Example 7 3D Steepest Ascent

Question: The temperature in space is $T(x, y, z) = \dfrac{80}{1 + x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2}$ ($^\circ\text{C}$). In which direction does $T$ increase fastest at $(1, 1, -2)$, and what is the maximum rate of increase?

Full Solution:
Let $D = 1 + x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2$. Using the Chain Rule: $$\nabla T = \frac{-80}{D^2}\langle 2x, 4y, 6z\rangle = \frac{160}{D^2}\langle -x, -2y, -3z\rangle$$ At $(1, 1, -2)$: $D = 1 + 1 + 2 + 12 = 16 \implies D^2 = 256$, and $\dfrac{160}{256} = \dfrac{5}{8}$: $$\nabla T(1, 1, -2) = \frac{5}{8}\langle -1, -2, 6\rangle$$ • Fastest increase direction: $\langle -1, -2, 6\rangle$ (or unit vector $\frac{\langle -1, -2, 6\rangle}{\sqrt{41}}$).
• Maximum rate of increase: $\|\nabla T(1, 1, -2)\| = \dfrac{5}{8}\sqrt{1 + 4 + 36} = \mathbf{\dfrac{5}{8}\sqrt{41} \approx 4.00^\circ\text{C/m}}$.

(Ex 7) Find $\nabla f(2, 1)$ and $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, 1)$ for $f(x, y) = \dfrac{x}{y}$ with $\mathbf{u} = \frac{3}{5}\hat{i} + \frac{4}{5}\hat{j}$.
(Ex 11) Find the directional derivative of $f(x, y) = e^x \sin y$ at $(0, \frac{\pi}{3})$ in the direction of $\mathbf{v} = \langle -6, 8\rangle$.

Ex 7: $\nabla f(x, y) = \left\langle \frac{1}{y}, -\frac{x}{y^2}\right\rangle \implies \nabla f(2, 1) = \langle 1, -2\rangle$. Since $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1$: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, 1) = 1\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) - 2\left(\frac{4}{5}\right) = \mathbf{-1}$.

Ex 11: $\nabla f(x, y) = \langle e^x\sin y,\; e^x\cos y\rangle \implies \nabla f(0, \frac{\pi}{3}) = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right\rangle$. Normalize $\mathbf{v} = \langle -6, 8\rangle$ ($\|\mathbf{v}\| = 10$): $\mathbf{u} = \left\langle -\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5}\right\rangle$.
Dot product: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\left(-\dfrac{3}{5}\right) + \dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{4}{5}\right) = \mathbf{\dfrac{4 - 3\sqrt{3}}{10}}$.
