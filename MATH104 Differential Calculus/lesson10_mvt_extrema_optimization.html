Theorems & Derivative Tests (Lecture 10 + Notes)

2.1 Rolle's Theorem (4.8.1), Mean Value Theorem (4.8.2) & Consequences

Theorem Hypotheses (Must Check!) Guaranteed Conclusion
Rolle's Theorem (4.8.1) Continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f(a) = f(b) = 0$. At least one $c \in (a, b)$ with $f'(c) = 0$ (horizontal tangent).
Mean Value Theorem (4.8.2) Continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. At least one $c \in (a, b)$ with $f'(c) = \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$ (tangent parallel to secant).
Constant Difference (4.8.3) $f'(x) = g'(x)$ for all $x$ on an interval. $f(x) = g(x) + k$ for some constant $k$ (vertical shift).
Extreme Value Theorem $f$ is continuous on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$. $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a, b]$.
Classic Highway Speeding Application (Example 5): If you travel $5\text{ mi}$ in $5\text{ min}$ ($\frac{1}{12}\text{ h}$), your average velocity is $60\text{ mi/h}$. Even if police clock you at $50\text{ mi/h}$ at the start and $55\text{ mi/h}$ at the end, the Mean Value Theorem guarantees your instantaneous velocity hit at least $60\text{ mi/h}$ somewhere in between!

2.2 Critical vs. Stationary Points, Concavity & Derivative Tests

Exam Favorite (Year Final 2017 Q1b): Critical Point vs. Stationary Point! • Critical Point: Any interior $x_0$ in the domain of $f$ where either $f'(x_0) = 0$ OR $f'(x_0)$ does not exist (e.g., a corner or cusp like $x^{2/3}$ at $x = 0$).
• Stationary Point: A critical point where specifically $f'(x_0) = 0$ (horizontal tangent). Every stationary point is a critical point, but not every critical point is stationary!
Sign Condition Curve Behavior Test / Classification
$f'(x) > 0$ on $(a, b)$  /  $f'(x) < 0$ on $(a, b)$ Increasing on $[a, b]$  /  Decreasing on $[a, b]$ 1st Derivative Test: $+ \to -$ gives Relative Max; $- \to +$ gives Relative Min.
$f''(x) > 0$ (open up — উপরের দিকে খোলা) Concave Up ($f'$ is increasing) 2nd Derivative Test: $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0 \implies$ Relative Minimum.
$f''(x) < 0$ (open down — নিচের দিকে খোলা) Concave Down ($f'$ is decreasing) 2nd Derivative Test: $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0 \implies$ Relative Maximum.
$f''$ changes sign across $x_0$ (with $f$ continuous) Inflection Point at $(x_0, f(x_0))$ Note: If $f''(c) = 0$ at a stationary point, 2nd Derivative Test is inconclusive (use 1st Deriv. Test).

Example 1: Concavity & Extrema of $f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}$

Use $f'$ and $f''$ to determine where $f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}$ is concave up/down, locate inflection points, and use the Second Derivative Test to find relative extrema.

Step 1 — First Derivative & Stationary Point: $f'(x) = -x e^{-x^2/2}$. Since $e^{-x^2/2} > 0$ always, $f'(x) = 0 \implies x = 0$.
Step 2 — Second Derivative (Product Rule): $f''(x) = (-1)e^{-x^2/2} + (-x)(-x e^{-x^2/2}) = (x^2 - 1)e^{-x^2/2} = (x - 1)(x + 1)e^{-x^2/2}$.
Step 3 — Concavity & Inflection Points: $f''(x) = 0 \implies x = \pm 1$. Testing regions: $(-\infty, -1) \implies (+)$ concave up; $(-1, 1) \implies (-)$ concave down; $(1, \infty) \implies (+)$ concave up. Inflection points at $(-1, e^{-1/2})$ and $(1, e^{-1/2})$.
Step 4 — Second Derivative Test at $x = 0$: $f''(0) = (0 - 1)e^0 = -1 < 0$. Therefore, $f$ has a relative maximum at $x = 0$ with value $f(0) = \mathbf{1}$ (and no relative minimum).

Example 2: Why $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ on $[-1, 8]$ Does Not Contradict MVT

Show there is no $c \in (-1, 8)$ with $f'(c) = \dfrac{f(8) - f(-1)}{8 - (-1)}$, and explain why this does not contradict the Mean Value Theorem.

Step 1 — Compute Average Slope: $f(8) = 8^{2/3} = 4$ and $f(-1) = (-1)^{2/3} = 1$, so $\dfrac{f(8) - f(-1)}{8 - (-1)} = \dfrac{4 - 1}{9} = \dfrac{1}{3}$.
Step 2 — Solve $f'(c) = \frac{1}{3}$: $f'(x) = \dfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = \dfrac{2}{3c^{1/3}} = \dfrac{1}{3} \implies c^{1/3} = 2 \implies c = 8$.
Step 3 — Why No Contradiction: $c = 8$ is an endpoint, NOT in the open interval $(-1, 8)$. This does not contradict MVT because $f'(x) = \frac{2}{3x^{1/3}}$ is undefined at $x = 0 \in (-1, 8)$ (a cusp), so the differentiability hypothesis fails!

Example 3: Prove $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ on $[-1, 1]$

Use Theorem 4.1.2(c) ($f'(x) = 0 \implies f$ is constant) to prove the inverse trig identity.

Step 1 — Define $f(x)$ & Differentiate: Let $f(x) = \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x$. On $(-1, 1)$: $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = 0$.
Step 2 — Conclude $f$ is Constant: Since $f$ is continuous on $[-1, 1]$ and $f'(x) = 0$ on $(-1, 1)$, $f(x) = k$ for some constant $k$ on $[-1, 1]$.
Step 3 — Evaluate at $x = 0$: $k = f(0) = \sin^{-1}(0) + \cos^{-1}(0) = 0 + \dfrac{\pi}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Thus $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. $\blacksquare$

Year Final Exam 2024 • Q7(c) & Q7(d) 2 + 4 = 6 Marks

(c) Find all critical points of $f(x) = 3x^{5/3} - 15x^{2/3}$.
(d) Find the radius and height of the right circular cylinder of largest volume that can be inscribed in a right circular cone with radius $6\text{ in}$ and height $10\text{ in}$.

Hint: In (c), factor $f'(x)$ and watch where $x^{-1/3}$ puts $x$ in the denominator ($x = 0$ is a critical point!). In (d), use similar triangles: $\dfrac{10 - h}{r} = \dfrac{10}{6} \implies h = 10 - \dfrac{5}{3}r$.
Part (c):
$f'(x) = 5x^{2/3} - 10x^{-1/3} = \dfrac{5(x - 2)}{x^{1/3}}$.
• $f'(x) = 0 \implies x = 2$ (stationary point).
• $f'(x)$ undefined $\implies x = 0$ (in domain of $f$, cusp).
Critical points: $x = 0$ and $x = 2$.

Part (d) (Inscribed Cylinder in Cone):
By similar triangles: $\dfrac{10 - h}{r} = \dfrac{10}{6} \implies h = 10 - \dfrac{5}{3}r$, with $r \in [0, 6]$.
Volume: $V(r) = \pi r^2 h = \pi r^2\left(10 - \dfrac{5}{3}r\right) = 10\pi r^2 - \dfrac{5\pi}{3}r^3$.
Differentiate: $V'(r) = 20\pi r - 5\pi r^2 = 5\pi r(4 - r) = 0 \implies r = 0$ or $\mathbf{r = 4\text{ in}}$.
Check $V''(4) = 20\pi - 10\pi(4) = -20\pi < 0 \implies$ absolute max! Height: $h = 10 - \frac{5}{3}(4) = \mathbf{\dfrac{10}{3}\text{ in}}$. *(Explore this live in Tab 3!)*
2nd In-course Exam 2019 • Q1 5 Marks

Question: Let $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$. (a) Find the secant line through $(-2, f(-2))$ and $(1, f(1))$. (b) Show there is only one $c \in (-2, 1)$ satisfying MVT for this secant line. (c) Find the tangent line at $(c, f(c))$.

(a) Secant Line: $f(-2) = (-2)^3 - 4(-2) = 0$, $f(1) = 1 - 4 = -3$.
Slope $m_{\text{sec}} = \dfrac{-3 - 0}{1 - (-2)} = -1$. Equation: $y - 0 = -1(x + 2) \implies \mathbf{y = -x - 2}$.

(b) Unique $c \in (-2, 1)$: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4$. Setting $3c^2 - 4 = -1 \implies 3c^2 = 3 \implies c = \pm 1$. Since $c = +1$ is an endpoint, $c = -1$ is the unique point in $(-2, 1)$.

(c) Parallel Tangent Line at $c = -1$: $f(-1) = (-1)^3 - 4(-1) = 3$, slope $= -1$.
Equation: $y - 3 = -1(x + 1) \implies \mathbf{y = -x + 2}$ (parallel to $y = -x - 2$!).
Year Final Exam 2017 • Q4(b) & Q4(c) (Also Ashraful's Notes) 4 + 6 = 10 Marks

(b) Given $f(x) = x + 2\sin x$ on $[0, 2\pi]$, find intervals of increase/decrease, concavity, and inflection points.
(c) An antibiotic sells at $\$200/\text{unit}$. Cost is $C(x) = 500{,}000 + 80x + 0.003x^2$, max capacity $30{,}000$ units. How many units maximize profit?

Part (b):
• $f'(x) = 1 + 2\cos x = 0 \implies \cos x = -\frac{1}{2} \implies x = \frac{2\pi}{3}, \frac{4\pi}{3}$. Increasing on $[0, \frac{2\pi}{3}]$ and $[\frac{4\pi}{3}, 2\pi]$; decreasing on $[\frac{2\pi}{3}, \frac{4\pi}{3}]$.
• $f''(x) = -2\sin x = 0 \implies x = \pi$ in $(0, 2\pi)$. Concave down on $(0, \pi)$ ($f'' < 0$); concave up on $(\pi, 2\pi)$ ($f'' > 0$). Inflection point: $(\pi, \pi)$.

Part (c) (Profit Maximization on $[0, 30{,}000]$):
Profit $P(x) = R(x) - C(x) = 200x - (500{,}000 + 80x + 0.003x^2) = 120x - 0.003x^2 - 500{,}000$.
$P'(x) = 120 - 0.006x = 0 \implies x = \dfrac{120}{0.006} = \mathbf{20{,}000\text{ units}}$ (inside $[0, 30{,}000]$).
Since $P''(x) = -0.006 < 0$ everywhere, $x = 20{,}000$ units gives the absolute maximum profit ($\$700{,}000$).
